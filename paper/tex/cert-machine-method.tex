\begin{abstract}
Machines now produce mathematical claims --- constants, constructions, bounds, formal proofs --- faster than anyone can check them, and each arrives with a checker its producer wrote. We describe cert-machine, which decides such claims independently: a claim is read as a predicate over a declared set of inputs and receives one of three verdicts, certified, refuted with a counterexample, or refused with the reason, computed in exact or outward-rounded interval arithmetic by code that shares nothing with the claimant, and every verdict is recorded with the evidence for it. Applied to three bodies of machine-generated claims, it confirmed all \MtKwRows{} kissing-number bounds of an autonomous discovery system by deciding $\MtKwPairsSci$ pairs exactly; it replayed the certificates behind three unverified bounds of an optimization-constants registry, which then removed the marks and cites the replay, and it refused a ``certified'' package whose evidence the package does not contain; and it found OpenAI's Lean certificate for a Navier--Stokes blowup partial at the announced commit and complete at the next, beside the gaps between what OpenAI's mathematics release claims and what it checks. The verdicts are sound under one hypothesis, the inclusion property of interval extensions; the arithmetic that secures it showed no violation in \MtArbCases{} cases checked against an independent library, and planted false, true and malformed claims were each decided correctly. Refusal is reported as a result, so what remains undecided is explicit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Machines now propose mathematics faster than anyone can read it. Program search finds constructions in combinatorics and analysis \citep{RomeraParedes24,Novikov25}; autonomous research systems publish record configurations with their own verification files \citep{Yang26}; conjectures about constants come from numerical coincidences \citep{Raayoni21}; long proofs arrive with machine formalisations \citep{OpenAINS,OpenAIMath}. Each claim comes with a checker its producer wrote, and a producer's checker shares the producer's blind spots. The public record shows what that costs: a floating-point product printed as an exact constant; witnesses that hold only within a verifier's tolerance; a checker that compares in double precision what it encloses at a hundred digits; a pairwise condition proved by structure and never checked pair by pair; a clause of a theorem that the formal statement leaves out \citep{Register}.

This paper proposes one rule for such claims and describes a system that enforces it. A machine-generated claim should be published with a bounded, reproducible evidence object that a checker independent of the claim's producer can decide; the checker must be able to certify the claim, refute it, or refuse it explicitly, and the scope and assumptions of the decision must be kept with it. cert-machine is such a checker. It reads a claim from its published statement and bytes, never runs the claimant's program, decides the claim in exact rational or outward-rounded interval arithmetic, and records one of three verdicts with the object that justifies it. When it cannot decide, it says so and why, and the refusal is recorded like any other result. The system is sound and deliberately incomplete: a procedure that must always answer cannot be both, since some of the questions machines ask are undecidable \citep{Bastounis26}, and a procedure that may refuse can.

The contribution has three layers, which can be assessed separately (Table~\ref{tab:layers}). The decision semantics defines what the three verdicts mean and proves them sound under one hypothesis. The certification implementations reduce particular kinds of claim to decisions of that kind, each stating what discharges a claim. The evidence infrastructure pins every input and every piece of code by digest, re-derives every record, and refuses to publish a number that no record supports. Interval arithmetic, the Krawczyk operator and exact geometric predicates are decades old \citep{Moore09,Krawczyk69,Rump10,Shewchuk97}, and nothing here claims them; what is claimed is the semantics, its enforcement, and what the system decided.

\section{Materials and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Materials}\label{sec:materials}

\paragraph{The claims} The system's inputs are claims and the bytes they rest on. The public register holds \RegRows{} published claims from \RegSources{} sources --- constants, constructions, bounds, formal statements, printed tables --- each pinned by the digest of the file that states it \citep{Register}. The three exercises of Section~\ref{sec:bench} draw on the following bodies of material. The kissing configurations of the Qiushi Engine \citep{Yang26}: \MtKwRows{} point sets in dimensions \MtKwDims, up to \MtKwMaxPoints{} points, published as integer coordinates in five releases of the claimant's repository \citep{QiushiRelease}, every file pinned by the digest the claimant's own manifest and the hosting platform report. The optimization-constants registry \citep{OptConst} at commit \MtRgBeforeCommit{} for the replays and with its pull-request queue as fetched on 2026-10-15, and at \MtRgRegistryCommit{} after the merges of \MtRgMergeDate, read on \MtRgFetched: the certificates its entries cite, among them three Zenodo deposits, a gist revision and a \MtRqLamFiles-file bundle, each pinned by digest, and the registry's own pages after the merges, pinned beside them. OpenAI's Navier--Stokes repository \citep{OpenAINS} at its announced commit \NsCommit{} and its next, \NsHeadCommit: \MtNsFiles{} files pinned on \MtNsFetched{} (the two papers, the Clay statement, the Lean bridge files, the challenge statement, the announcement), and a Lean library of \MtNsLeanFiles{} files and \MtNsLeanLines{} lines. OpenAI's mathematics release \citep{OpenAIMath} at commit \MtOaCommit: \MtOaManuscripts{} manuscripts in \MtOaFamilies{} families and \MtOaChallenges{} Comparator challenges, censused file by file. 

\paragraph{The machines} Everything that finishes within about a day runs on an \MtDeskCpus-core laptop with \MtDeskMemory~GB of memory: the interval kernel and the certifiers in Node v24.14.1 (V8 13.6), the bulk pair checks in Python with numpy's BLAS, the Lean builds. The \MtOaChallenges{} challenges of the release ran on GitHub's hosted runners (4 vCPU, 16~GB, six hours); the challenges those could not finish were sent to rented CPU machines under a pre-registered budget cap, and none of them finished there either, which the record says. The seven largest kissing configurations, about $1.4\times10^{15}$ pairs each, ran on one rented \MtKwGpuName{} at \$\MtKwGpuPrice{} per hour for \MtKwGpuHours{} GPU-hours (\$\MtKwGpuCost{} billed), by a kernel that was first required to reproduce the laptop's answers on two smaller dimensions. Every machine that decided a record is named in it with its platform and software.

\paragraph{The records} Every decision writes a record: the inputs by path and SHA-256, the verdict, the witness, the scope, the machine, the time, and the second implementation's reading where one exists. The records are the materials of Section~\ref{sec:bench} and of every number in this paper: each number is a macro written by a generator from the record that holds it, and the generator refuses to write when a record no longer supports the sentence that quotes it (Section~\ref{sec:discipline}).

\paragraph{Terms} Five words recur. A \emph{pin} is a file named by path and SHA-256 digest and re-hashed whenever it is used. A \emph{record} is the file a decision writes: the pinned inputs, the verdict, the witness, the scope and the machine. A \emph{witness} is the object that justifies a verdict: a certificate, a counterexample or, for a split refusal, two proved points. A \emph{red control} is a deliberately wrong variant of a test, which must be rejected; a battery whose red controls do not all fire fails. A \emph{reading} compares a statement with the theorem it accompanies; it is recorded and issues no verdict. The verdict words are defined in Section~\ref{sec:grammar} and listed in Table~\ref{tab:grammar}.

\subsection{Decision semantics: the grammar}\label{sec:grammar}

\subsubsection{Claims over boxes}
A \emph{box} is a product $X = [\underline x_1, \overline x_1] \times \cdots \times [\underline x_n, \overline x_n]$ of closed intervals with rational endpoints; a double is a rational, so a box of doubles is a box. A \emph{declared envelope} $B$ is a box each of whose sides carries the source of its bounds --- a survey, a data sheet, a chart. A number someone printed is the box its digits allow: a printed $0.0634$ is $[0.06335, 0.06345]$, and a fit printed to four decimals is a box in parameter space.

\begin{definition}[interval extension]\label{def:ext}
Let $g : D \subseteq \R^n \to \R$. A map $G$ from boxes in $D$ to intervals is an \emph{interval extension} of $g$ if $g(X) \subseteq G(X)$ for every box $X \subseteq D$ (the inclusion property).
\end{definition}

The inclusion property is the one hypothesis of every verdict below, and Section~\ref{sec:arith} is about securing it. The extensions used are natural extensions --- the formula evaluated in interval arithmetic --- and the classical theorem that a composition of operations with the inclusion property has it \citep{Moore09,Neumaier90} carries it from the primitives to the model.

\begin{definition}[claim]\label{def:claim}
A \emph{claim} is a pair $(B, \varphi)$ of a box and a predicate $\varphi(x) \equiv \bigwedge_j\, g_j(x) \le c_j$ whose functions have interval extensions $G_j$ (an equality, or an exact predicate such as the orientation of three points, is the special case decided in exact arithmetic). It is read in one of two ways:
\begin{description}
\item[universal] the claim asserts $\varphi(x)$ for every $x \in B$ --- the reading of a mathematical statement (``for all $n \le N$'', ``on $[0,1]$'', ``for every member of this family'', ``for every pair of these points'');
\item[epistemic] the claim asserts $\varphi(x^\dagger)$ for an unknown true input $x^\dagger \in B$ --- the reading of an engineering number, where $B$ is the declared uncertainty about the input.
\end{description}
\end{definition}

The two readings differ in what a single proved counterexample does. Under the universal reading it refutes the claim; under the epistemic reading it shows only that the declared evidence does not settle the question.

\subsubsection{Three verdicts}

\begin{definition}[verdicts]\label{def:verdicts}
Let $\mathcal X$ be a finite cover of $B$ by boxes. The claim is
\begin{description}
\item[\vC] if $\sup G_j(X) \le c_j$ for every $j$ and every $X \in \mathcal X$;
\item[\vR] under the universal reading, if some box $X^\ast \subseteq B$ (typically a thin box, a point) has $\inf G_j(X^\ast) > c_j$ for some $j$; under the epistemic reading, if every $X \in \mathcal X$ has $\inf G_j(X) > c_j$ for some $j$, or the claim contradicts a bound that holds on all of $B$;
\item[\vF] otherwise. Under the epistemic reading a refusal is \emph{split} when two points $x^-, x^+ \in B$ have been proved to satisfy and to violate $\varphi$ respectively, and \emph{unresolved} when the enclosures did not separate within the budget.
\end{description}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[soundness]\label{prop:sound}
If every $G_j$ is an interval extension of $g_j$, then \vC\ implies $\varphi(x)$ for every $x \in B$; \vR\ implies, under the universal reading, $\neg\varphi(x)$ for some $x \in B$ and, under the epistemic reading, $\neg\varphi(x)$ for every $x \in B$; and a split \vF\ implies that neither $\forall x \in B\ \varphi(x)$ nor $\forall x \in B\ \neg\varphi(x)$ holds, so that $\varphi(x^\dagger)$ is not determined by the declared envelope.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $x \in B$ and choose $X \in \mathcal X$ containing it. By the inclusion property $g_j(x) \in g_j(X) \subseteq G_j(X)$, hence $g_j(x) \le \sup G_j(X) \le c_j$ for every $j$, which is $\varphi(x)$. The refutations are the same argument with the inequality reversed, on $X^\ast$ or on every member of the cover. For a split refusal, $x^-$ and $x^+$ are points of $B$ at which $\varphi$ is proved true and false respectively; either could be $x^\dagger$.
\end{proof}

An unresolved refusal claims nothing, and the grammar keeps it that way: a refusal is terminal, never retried at lower rigour, never converted into a probability and never dropped from the record \citep{Register}. The only path from \vF\ to \vC\ is a new exact argument --- a finer cover, a better extension, a different certifier, or, as Section~\ref{sec:math} shows, the bytes the claimant had not yet published. The three words are not probabilities and there is no fourth.

\subsubsection{Witnesses}
Every verdict carries the object that justifies it, and the object is what is published. A certification carries its \emph{certificate}: the inputs pinned by digest, the cover, the enclosures, and a statement of what would make it fail. The certificate constructor of the interval toolkit refuses to build a certificate whose falsifier is not stated and a \vC\ that carries no evidence, on the principle that a certificate that cannot go red is decoration. A refutation carries the falsifying object, printed --- a counterexample, an observation outside a region, a violated equation, a digit that is not the rounding of an enclosure. A split refusal carries its two witnesses. A certificate can be re-checked without any of this system's code: its facts are exact or outward-rounded, and any arithmetic that rounds outward can check them.

\subsubsection{Thresholds}
A split refusal is the most useful outcome the grammar has, because the question it leaves open has a size.

\begin{definition}[flip thresholds]\label{def:flip}
Let the envelope depend on a declared scalar $p$ --- an operating choice, or one end of an interval that a measurement could narrow --- through boxes $B(p)$, and let the verdict be monotone in $p$: if it is \vC\ at $p$ it is \vC\ at every $p' \le p$, and if it is \vR\ at $p$ it is \vR\ at every $p' \ge p$. The \emph{flip thresholds} are $p_{\mathrm C} = \sup\{p : \text{\vC\ over } B(p)\}$ and $p_{\mathrm R} = \inf\{p : \text{\vR\ over } B(p)\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:flip}
Under monotonicity, if the procedure has certified the claim over $B(\underline p)$ and refuted it over $B(\overline p)$, then the claim is \vC\ for every $p \le \underline p$ and \vR\ for every $p \ge \overline p$, and $\underline p \le p_{\mathrm C} \le p_{\mathrm R} \le \overline p$.
\end{proposition}

The proof is the definition. Monotonicity is a hypothesis of the proposition, and it is established in each use rather than assumed. When $p$ is one end of a declared interval, the boxes $B(p)$ are nested, and the verdict is monotone for every claim: a predicate proved over a box is proved over each of its sub-boxes, and one refuted at every point of a box is refuted on each sub-box (orient $p$ so that the boxes shrink in the direction of the definition). When $p$ is an operating choice, monotonicity is a property of the model and must be proved for it. Where neither argument is available, the procedure reports the two endpoints it decided and no threshold. The procedure computes $\underline p$ and $\overline p$ by bisection over rational $p$, each endpoint decided like any other claim, or in closed form where $p$ enters additively, the result rounded outward to the precision reported. The engineering demonstrator does both \citep{P2}: the pump discharge in closed form, monotone because the wellhead pressure is the discharge plus a term that does not depend on it, and the lower end of the roughness interval, which an inspection could narrow, by bisection, monotone because its boxes are nested. The threshold turns a refusal into a measurement or an operating change with a known effect on the verdict: at this discharge the rule is proved, at that roughness it is refuted.

\subsubsection{Compositions}
A receipt with several checks is \vR\ if any check is refuted, otherwise \vF\ if any is refused, otherwise \vC; by Proposition~\ref{prop:sound} this is sound for their conjunction. The public register composes the three verdicts into words that each say what they are made of (Table~\ref{tab:grammar}); none is a fourth verdict. Two consequences are enforced rather than recommended \citep{Register}. A scope sentence is load-bearing, because a \vC\ without the scope beside it can say more than the record, as when the computational fragment of a claim is certified and its analytic core is not touched. And the kinds of refusal are never summed: the instrument declining, the claimant having published nothing, a budget exhausted, a cell whose box did not close and an audit stating its reach are different behaviours with different denominators.

\subsection{Certification implementations: the arithmetic}\label{sec:arith}

Proposition~\ref{prop:sound} has one hypothesis, the inclusion property, and the arithmetic exists to secure it. Two kinds are used, and the choice is made per quantity: exact rationals where a tie must be decided or the object is rational, outward-rounded intervals where the quantity is transcendental or the data are long.

\paragraph{Exact rationals} Numbers are reduced fractions of arbitrary-precision integers. Doubles convert to them losslessly; linear systems are solved by exact elimination, which reports a singular matrix rather than a small pivot; conversion back to a double is for display only. Rational arithmetic decides every sign it is asked, and it is used for the corners of multilinear forms (Section~\ref{sec:multilinear}), the expected counts of a benchmark, polynomial identities, and the ends of enclosures whose comparison must be exact. Decimal literals --- the digits a claimant printed --- are read as the rationals they denote and kept as strings beside their double images, so that a vertex printed to three decimals and an observation printed to four can be found to coincide \citep{P1}.

\paragraph{Exact integers in floating-point hardware} A product of integer vectors is itself an integer, and floating-point hardware computes it exactly when no partial sum can leave the range the format holds exactly: $2^{24}$ for single precision, $2^{53}$ for double, $2^{11}$ for the half precision of tensor cores. The bound used is the Cauchy--Schwarz one --- every partial sum of products of two vectors is at most the product of their lengths --- checked against the data before anything runs and written into the record. This is what lets $10^{15}$ pairs of integer points be decided exactly on a GPU (Section~\ref{sec:math}): the arithmetic is floating point, the result is an integer held exactly, and the test applied to it is the exact integer test.

\paragraph{Outward rounding on doubles} IEEE-754 arithmetic in round-to-nearest returns, for each of $+, -, \times, \div$, the double nearest the exact result, which therefore lies within half a unit in the last place of the computed one \citep{IEEE754,Goldberg91}. JavaScript cannot set the rounding mode, so directed rounding is emulated: each computed endpoint is moved one unit in the last place outward by stepping its bit pattern. The trust base of this emulation is stated once: the correctly rounded $+, -, \times, \div, \sqrt{\ }$ that IEEE-754 mandates and V8 implements, V8's arbitrary-precision integers, and nothing in \texttt{Math.*}, which ECMA-262 leaves ``implementation-approximated'' (a 2017 proposal to require one-ulp accuracy was closed without adoption); the Node version that produced every record is written into the record (\texttt{v24.14.1}, V8 13.6, whose \texttt{Math.*} is the fdlibm port, faithful to within one ulp and not correctly rounded; V8 moved these functions to a correctly rounded library in May 2026, which is why the version is material; correctly rounded libraries are a recent engineering achievement \citep{CoreMath22}). The operations are those of the basic set of the interval standard \citep{IEEE1788}; the implementation is not a conforming one and carries no decorations.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:ulp}
Let $\circ \in \{+,-,\times,\div\}$, let $A$, $B$ be intervals of doubles --- pairs of non-NaN doubles with lower end at most upper end, an infinite endpoint allowed as a bound --- with $0 \notin B$ when $\circ = \div$, and let $\ell$ and $h$ be the round-to-nearest values of the smallest and largest of the endpoint combinations $a \circ b$. If no endpoint combination is indeterminate ($0 \cdot \infty$, $\infty - \infty$), then $A \circ B \subseteq [\mathrm{down}(\ell), \mathrm{up}(h)]$, where $\mathrm{down}$ and $\mathrm{up}$ step one double toward $\mp\infty$ and fix $\mp\infty$; an exact result beyond the largest double rounds to $\pm\infty$, which the step fixes, so overflow is enclosed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For these operations the extremes of $a \circ b$ over a product of intervals are attained at endpoint combinations (monotonicity in each argument on each sign region, with $0 \notin B$ for division). The exact extreme lies within half the spacing of the doubles on its side of its round-to-nearest value, and the adjacent double on that side is a whole spacing away (subnormal spacing included), so it lies on the correct side of $\mathrm{down}(\ell)$ and $\mathrm{up}(h)$.
\end{proof}

The domain of the proposition is enforced, not assumed: a malformed operand (a NaN, an inverted pair) or an indeterminate result is refused by exception and never decided, because a consumer written as ``not proved above the bar, hence below it'' would otherwise certify on a NaN (until 2026-10-09 nothing checked this, and the batteries skipped non-finite results; enforcing it exposed one battery check that had been passing on malformed boxes). Division by an interval that contains zero is refused, not extended; a square is clamped at zero; a non-integer power is refused, and so is an integer exponent of $2^{31}$ or more, where the implementation's exponent arithmetic would wrap. The widening costs about one bit per operation and nothing at the accuracy the claims need; the domain checks cost a few comparisons per operation (ten million operations in 0.18 s). Where more precision is required --- a constant to be certified to twelve digits, a Catalan or Li$_2$ value --- a separate arbitrary-precision arithmetic carries a binary mantissa of arbitrary length with true directed rounding (floor and ceiling of the shifted integer), and converts its results to doubles outward with an exact check.

The elementary and special functions, long sums and derivatives that the certifiers also need are built on these operations; how each is enclosed, and the margins that make the series tails safe, are in \ref{app:arith}.

\subsubsection{A zero, exactly one}
\begin{theorem}[Krawczyk {\citep{Krawczyk69,Neumaier90,Rump10}}]\label{thm:krawczyk}
Let $f : D \subseteq \R^n \to \R^n$ be continuously differentiable, $X \subseteq D$ a box, $\hat x \in X$, $Y$ a nonsingular matrix and $F'(X)$ an interval extension of the Jacobian over $X$. If
\[ K(X) = \hat x - Y f(\hat x) + \bigl(I - Y F'(X)\bigr)(X - \hat x) \subseteq \operatorname{int} X, \]
then $f$ has exactly one zero in $X$.
\end{theorem}
The certifier takes $\hat x$ from a floating-point search, $Y$ as an approximate inverse of the Jacobian at $\hat x$, evaluates $K(X)$ with every datum enclosed by its neighbouring doubles, and inflates $X$ for a bounded number of rounds; if the inclusion never holds the zero is \vF\ with the reason ``no contraction''. A sibling certifier for fixed points in weighted sequence spaces uses the radii-polynomial form of the same argument and requires the contraction factor to be proved below one before it states uniqueness.

\subsubsection{A maximum in a box}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:max}
Let $\ell$ be twice continuously differentiable on a convex box $X$, let $\nabla\ell$ have exactly one zero $\theta^\ast$ in $X$, and let the Hessian of $\ell$ be negative definite at every point of $X$. Then $\theta^\ast$ is the unique maximiser of $\ell$ on $X$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A function whose Hessian is negative definite on a convex set is strictly concave there, and a critical point of a strictly concave function is its unique maximiser on the set.
\end{proof}
Negative definiteness over the box is proved one of two ways: by Sylvester's criterion on the interval Hessian, its leading principal minors enclosed and proved of alternating sign over all of $X$ (for up to three parameters, with the two enclosures of each symmetric entry intersected); or, where the box's minors are too wide, at the candidate point together with a proof that every Hessian over $X$ is nonsingular --- $|I - A H(X)|\,u < u$ componentwise for a positive vector $u$ and an approximate inverse $A$ --- so that, $X$ being connected and the eigenvalues continuous, no Hessian over $X$ can change its inertia. This is how a maximum-likelihood fit becomes a certificate \citep{P1}. The statement is local: that no better maximum exists elsewhere in the family remains the search's claim, and every fit is stated with that scope.

\subsubsection{A supremum at a limit}
Some families have no interior maximum on some data: the likelihood rises toward a limit member, and an optimiser prints the last iterate as the fit.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:limit}
Let $\ell(\mu, \sigma, Q)$ be continuous on $B \times [0, q_1]$ and differentiable in $Q$ on $B \times (0, q_1]$, and let $\partial\ell/\partial Q < 0$ there. Then for every $(\mu, \sigma) \in B$ and every $Q \in (0, q_1]$, $\ell(\mu, \sigma, Q) < \ell(\mu, \sigma, 0)$.
\end{proposition}
The proof is the mean value theorem. With $Q = 1/\sqrt\alpha$ the generalized gamma reaches the lognormal at $Q = 0$ \citep{Prentice74}, and in these coordinates $Q = 0$ is an ordinary point; a proof that the $Q$-score is negative over a neighbourhood of the lognormal fit refuses the family at its edge \emph{with the proof} that every member there is less likely than its limit. A threshold on $\alpha$ in place of this proof hands a different family the verdict at \ANaiveDiff{} of \ABlocks{} cell-blocks of the extreme-value atlas \citep{P1}, which is the cost of a threshold measured.

\subsubsection{A statement for every input in a box}\label{sec:multilinear}
Four reductions decide a universal statement over a box.

\emph{A finite box, exhaustively.} Where the box is a finite set --- the pairs of a point configuration, the cases of a theorem up to a bound --- every element is decided, and the only question is the arithmetic: exact integers, or floating point under a bound that makes it exact (Section~\ref{sec:arith}). The work is proportional to the set, and a set too large for the budget is a refusal, never a sample.

\emph{Bisection with a proposer.} The box is split until each piece's enclosure decides, a floating-point heuristic choosing the coordinate to split and never a verdict; a depth budget bounds the work, and an exhausted budget is an unresolved refusal. To publish a split refusal, the corners are evaluated as thin boxes and one proved witness is kept on each side.

\emph{Monotone implicit functions.} Where a model quantity is the root of an equation monotone in its parameters, the root's range over the box is bracketed by the roots at two corners, and each end counts only when the equation's sign just beyond it is proved. Colebrook's friction law is handled this way: with $x = 1/\sqrt f$ the root of $h(x) = x + (2/\ln 10)\ln(a + bx)$, increasing in $x$, $a$ and $b$, every root over the box lies in $[x^\ast(a_+, b_+), x^\ast(a_-, b_-)]$, and a floating-point Newton iteration only locates the ends \citep{P2}.

\emph{Multilinear forms.}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:multilinear}
Let $f$ be multilinear on a box $X \subset \R^n$ (affine in each variable when the others are fixed). Then $f(X) = [\min_v f(v), \max_v f(v)]$, the minimum and maximum taken over the $2^n$ vertices of $X$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix all variables but one; $f$ is affine in it, so its extremes over that side are at the side's ends. Induct over the variables to reach a vertex for each extreme. $f$ is continuous and $X$ connected, so every value between is attained.
\end{proof}
An emissions-abatement estimate, a sum of products of premises each declared as an interval, is such a form. The kernel checks multilinearity syntactically --- every term a monomial in distinct variables, or the corner rule refuses to apply --- and evaluates the $2^n$ corners in exact rationals; the enclosure is then the exact range, not an outer bound, and the two corners at its ends are the witnesses of a split refusal.

The three remaining certifiers of Table~\ref{tab:pathways} --- an exact sign, a nonnegative polynomial and the minimum of a cosine polynomial --- play no part in the exercises and are described in \ref{app:arith}.

\subsubsection{A formal proof, built and read}
A formal certificate is two claims. That the proof is accepted is decided by building it at the pinned commit, with the toolchain and library versions the claimant pinned, and asking the kernel which axioms the result depends on; where the claimant's checker allows it, the proof is replayed by a second kernel written independently of the first \citep{Comparator,Nanoda}. That the statement proved is the one claimed is not a computation: the statement is read hypothesis by hypothesis against the published theorem and the library's definitions, the reading is recorded with its slips and weak points, and it never issues a verdict of its own --- the verdict is the kernel's, scoped by what the reading found absent. Section~\ref{sec:ns} is the worked case. The readings reported in this paper were produced by AI agents working from a written brief and consolidated into the records cited (Section~\ref{sec:limits}); they are recorded as readings and carry no verdict.

\subsubsection{Proposers}
Every certifier above takes its candidate from a floating-point search --- Nelder--Mead and Levenberg--Marquardt for a likelihood, Newton for a root, sampling for a minimum, a semidefinite solver for a decomposition, a language model for a construction --- and none of these is ever relied on for a verdict. A proposer may be wrong, slow or adversarial; it can cost a refusal, never a false verdict. The same rule governs forecasts in the system's applications: a forecaster enters as a proposer of boxes, is graded against what is later observed, and stops being admitted when it misses its declared coverage.

\subsection{Evidence infrastructure: the verification discipline}\label{sec:discipline}

Proposition~\ref{prop:sound} makes the verdicts as sound as the code that computes the extensions. The discipline is how the system guards that code. Its rules are stated as engineering because each is a line of code that refuses --- a battery that exits non-zero, a page builder that will not write its page, a numbers generator that will not write a paper's macros --- and this section says where each refusal sits.

\paragraph{Screens prune, never admit} Floating point decides only what is worth certifying. Every family of the generation loop declares a cheap screen that may only discard candidates and a certifier that is the only authority; the loop can skip on a screen and can record a hit only from the certifier. A screen is itself a claim that it drops nothing it should keep, so the campaign runner plants known answers that must pass every stage and certify before a run may start, refuses a run whose filters lose one, and requires a deliberately simple baseline beside every campaign so that a clever proposer is measured against something \citep[the methods note]{CMArchive}.

\paragraph{Red controls must fire} A check that has never gone red is decoration. A red control is a deliberately wrong variant of a test that must be rejected, and a battery whose red controls do not all fire fails, whatever its other checks say. Examples: a determinant whose double-precision value is $+3.6\times10^{-15}$ and whose exact sign is negative, which the filter must send to exact arithmetic; a ``gate'' that evaluates the floating-point model at the centre of the box, which is what a point pipeline does and which the corners must catch; a missing inner certificate, which must refuse a theorem; a bar on the wrong side of an endpoint, which must be refused by name; a seventh point of equal norm in the plane, which the pairwise test must find. The test target runs \MtBatteries{} batteries, \MtBatteriesNode{} in JavaScript and \MtBatteriesPy{} in Python, and a wiring check refuses a tree in which that target and the control build's list differ. In the six-lane audit of machine-written manuscripts, \AiMutations{} mutation controls stand beside \AiChecks{} named checks, and the campaign runner ships \CheatsN{} ways to cheat its own reward signal, each caught by a named control \citep{Register}. The methods note records \BugsN{} defects the project shipped and then found: \BugsByRunning{} caught by an impossible number, a calibration, a control, a byte pin or an outside read, and \BugsByCodeRead{} by the adversarial read of the code made for this paper (Section~\ref{sec:limits}), counted apart \citep[the methods note]{CMArchive}.

\paragraph{Calibrate before you decide} A new path --- a new machine, a new arithmetic, a new kernel --- decides nothing until it has reproduced a decision already made by an old one. The GPU kernel of Section~\ref{sec:math} was required to return the laptop's exact contact counts on two smaller configurations before it was allowed to decide the seven large ones; the Leech lattice's 196,560 and E$_8$'s 240 are re-derived at the start of every kissing run; and a pre-registration states, before a paid run, what will be measured, what counts as a result, and at what cost the run stops.

\paragraph{Pin the bytes} Every claim is decided against a held byte sequence named by path and SHA-256 and re-hashed at decision time: the claimant's file, the dataset, the printed table transcribed digit for digit, and the certifying modules themselves, whose digests a ledger records and whose workers refuse other bytes. A page that reports a record refuses to build when a pinned input has changed, when the record was written by a different version of its verifier, or when its battery does not pass; an application build that regenerates a record and finds it different from the committed one refuses and leaves the committed record in place. Files lifted from the laboratory this system grew out of come through a manifest of their source paths and digests.

\paragraph{Independence from the claimant} The claimant's program is never relied on: the decision is re-derived from the published statement and the published bytes, and where a claimant's script was read to settle which identity a manuscript meant, the record says so and the script was not executed. An unqualified ``no shared code'' would not survive a check, because two of the system's own instruments reuse its own code across a producer--checker line; the precise form adopted is that the system \emph{shares no code with the claimant}, and every certificate re-runs with no engine present \citep[the positioning notes]{CMArchive}.

\paragraph{Same-author second implementations} Where a verdict is the product, a second program written from the specification, in another language and arithmetic and sharing no code, re-derives it (Table~\ref{tab:second}). These are N-version checks by the same author from the same specification on the same inputs: they catch a slip in one arithmetic and cannot catch a shared misreading, and they are counted apart from re-derivations by other parties (Section~\ref{sec:independence}), of which the record holds \MtOutsideReruns{}. Not every certifier has one; Section~\ref{sec:limits} names those that do not.

\paragraph{Records re-run} A record is not believed because it was written; it is re-derived. Published records re-run in the reader's browser by the bytes that wrote them and are compared with the record --- by digest for a day of wave-window decisions and a cell of the extreme-value atlas, field by field for an engineering receipt and an abatement registry. A rerun kit lists \MtKitCommands{} commands that re-derive the register's records or verify them with the standard library alone; its one recorded execution, on \MtRunDate{}, ran \MtRunCommands{} of them in about \MtRunMinutes{} minutes on a laptop and compared \MtRunRows{} register rows before and after: \MtRunMoved{} moved, \MtRunFailed{} failed. And the system's own results are offered to outside parties to re-certify without its code; \MtOutsideReruns{} such rerun is recorded so far, the $\lambda(4)$ audit of Section~\ref{sec:wider}.

\paragraph{Honest counting} Every row is derived from a record; a claim with no record gets no row. An aggregate audit counts once and names its most frequent defect. A correction the system adds beside a refuted claim is never counted among the audited corpus's rows; a queued row is not a decided row; refusal kinds are never merged into one total; and every number in this paper and its companions is a macro written by a generator that refuses when a record no longer supports the sentence that quotes it \citep{Register}.

\section{Benchmark exercise}\label{sec:bench}

Three exercises test the method on claims that machines produced, each a different kind of claim with a different outcome: a configuration of points, decided pair by pair; the certificates behind a registry's unverified bounds; and the formal proofs and challenges of one release. Each is told in the same order: the claim, how it is read, how it is decided, the verdict and its witness, the cost, and what the claimant did next. Every number is read from the record that decided it.

\subsection{Mathematics: nineteen kissing-number bounds from an autonomous discovery system}\label{sec:math}

\paragraph{The claim} In September 2026 a group at Zhejiang University published new lower bounds for the kissing number \citep{ConwaySloane99} in \MtKwRows{} dimensions (\MtKwDims), found by an autonomous multi-agent research system \citep{Yang26}. Each bound is a configuration of unit vectors, no two closer than sixty degrees, larger than the previously recorded one. The configurations are built from lattices, codes, sections and projections. Their sizes are proved by moment identities and the angular condition by structure, and the release's own verification files record that the pairwise inner products were not checked.

\paragraph{The reading} A kissing configuration is the simplest universal claim there is: a finite set of points and one inequality for every pair. In the terms of Definition~\ref{def:claim}, the box is the set of pairs and the predicate is $\langle x, y\rangle \le \tfrac12 |x|\,|y|$. With integer coordinates the test is exact. For rows of squared norms $A$ and $B$ with inner product $p$, the angle is at least sixty degrees if and only if $p \le 0$ or $4p^2 \le AB$; the same test rejects a repeated or parallel row, so it also checks that the directions are distinct. A contact, at exactly sixty degrees, is $4p^2 = AB$.

\paragraph{The decision} Two configurations, in dimensions 25 and 27, were published with the paper as exact objects and were decided at once. The other seventeen had no published points, so they were marked \textsc{needs data}, with the missing files named. We asked for them on the repository's public thread. The claimant published the points for dimensions 43 and 45 five days later and, once those were decided, every remaining configuration within hours \citep{QiushiRelease}. Each file is pinned by the digest that the claimant's manifest and the hosting platform report, and the tool refuses a file that does not match.

For every configuration, the row count and the counts per squared norm are checked against the claimant's, the rows are shown to span exactly $d$ dimensions (the rank over $\mathbb Q$ of the exact Gram matrix $X^{\mathsf T}X$), and every pair is decided. Three arithmetics are used, each exact on its data. On the laptop, single-precision BLAS tiles are exact because every partial sum is bounded by the largest squared norm (at most 192, against $2^{24}$). In dimension 38, where \MtKwAxisRows{} rows have coordinates of \MtKwAxisDigits-digit squared norm, those rows are decided in exact 64-bit integers (every partial sum is below $\sqrt{48N} < 2^{63}$) and among themselves in Python integers. In dimensions 49--55, about $1.4\times10^{15}$ pairs each, a fused kernel on one rented GPU computes and reduces each $128\times128$ block of products in registers, with half-precision inputs and single-precision accumulation; every value is an integer below $2^{11}$. Before deciding anything, the GPU path had to reproduce the laptop's contact counts on dimensions 32 and 39 (\MtKwCalContacts), and it did. In every dimension a seeded sample of rows was decided again in plain Python integers and by a separate JavaScript implementation in arbitrary-precision integers; the three agree on every contact count. Dimensions 43 and 45 were also decided in the form the construction states --- integer coefficients over the claimant's lattice basis, with its exact Gram matrix --- with the same contacts.

\subsection{A curated record: the certificates of an optimization-constants registry}\label{sec:registry}

\paragraph{The claim} The optimization-constants registry \citep{OptConst} keeps the best known bounds on \MtRgConstants{} constants of analysis and combinatorics, each with its source. Many recent entries come from automated discovery and AI-assisted contributors \citep{GGSWT25}: two of the rows below carry the registry's own note that they were prepared with an AI system, and a third producer signs its archive ``AI Autoresearch'' \citep{NumaroC71}. The registry marks with an asterisk a bound ``whose verification is currently minimal'', meaning one too large to check by hand and available only as a self-archived artifact. On \MtRgRuleDate{} its maintainer stated how the mark comes off: it ``is removed when that changes --- when the paper is refereed and published, or when the argument is formalized or independently replayed'', and a pull request removing it on those grounds would be taken \citep{OptConstRule}.

Three asterisked lower bounds rest on finite certificates. Two are constants of the sums--differences problem \citep{KatzTao99}, $C_{3b} \ge \MtRgCbBound$ and $C_{3c} \ge \MtRgCcBound$, each certified by an entropy law, on \MtRgCbPoints{} and \MtRgCcPoints{} points of $\mathbb Z^2$ \citep{MosaicC3b,LinC3c}. The third is the Fourier entropy--influence constant \citep{FriedgutKalai96}, $C_{71} > \MtRgFeiBound$, certified by a Boolean function on \MtRgFeiN{} variables \citep{NumaroC71}. Beside them, the registry's open pull requests of 2026-10-15 (\MtRqPrs) offered \MtRqRows{} new bounds: two step functions, five one-line constructions, a nine-dimensional counterexample, a two-law entropy certificate \citep{JaswalC3c}, and a ``certified record package'' for the de Bruijn--Newman constant, $\Lambda < 0.1595$ \citep{MosaicLambda}, against the recorded $0 \le \Lambda \le 0.2$ \citep{RodgersTao20,PlattTrudgian21}.

\paragraph{The reading} An entropy certificate is a finite law $\mu$ on $\mathbb Z^2$ with rational weights. For $(X, Y) \sim \mu$ it claims $C \ge H(X-Y)/\max\{H(X), H(Y), H(X+Y)\}$, with $H(X+2Y)$ added to the maximum for $C_{3c}$. That is a universal claim over one finite object, decided by the exact pushforward of $\mu$ under each linear form and an outward-rounded enclosure of each entropy. The two-law certificate draws $10^{20}$ independent base-55 digits from two laws, and its entropies add digit by digit only if the map from digits to $L(X, Y)$ is injective. That holds when every form is at most $(55-1)/2 = 27$ in absolute value on both supports, a condition of the claim that is checked, not assumed. The entropy--influence bound is read from a truth table of $2^{\MtRgFeiN}$ entries. The integer Walsh--Hadamard transform gives the spectrum exactly, the influence $I$ is a rational, the entropy $H$ of the spectral weights is an enclosure, and the bound $H/(I-1)$ follows from an amplification rule that is cited, not re-proved \citep{ODonnellTan13}. The record package is read for what it is, a set of scripts and frozen files, and inventoried without running them.

\paragraph{The decision} Each certificate was decided from the bytes the registry cites, every file pinned by digest, without relying on any of the claimants' checkers. The entropy certificates were decided twice, by programs that share no code with each other or with the claimants: JavaScript over arbitrary-precision integers with dyadic intervals at 128 bits, and the Python standard library alone, its decimal arithmetic at 120 digits rounded outward (Table~\ref{tab:second}). The two asterisked ones took \MtRgSecSumdiff{} and \MtRgSecVerify{} seconds; the queue's two-law certificate was decided within the queue's run below. The truth table was decided once, in \MtRgSecFei{} seconds, by \MtRgFeiChecks{} named checks: the digest the artifact binds, balance, Parseval's identity, $I = \MtRgFeiI$ exactly, the bound, and the claimant's printed floor inside the enclosure. The queue was decided wherever a finite exact object exists, in \MtRqSeconds{} seconds for all of it. The record package was read file by file. Of its \MtRqLamFiles{} files, \MtRqLamFrozen{} are frozen tables and \MtRqLamScripts{} are scripts, and none of the scripts evaluates the zeta function, the heat-flow function $H_t$ or the Dirichlet polynomial whose zeros the bound is about. The analytic inputs enter as frozen outputs whose proofs cite \MtRqLamBanks{} numbered banks that the bundle does not contain.

\subsection{Formal proof: OpenAI's mathematics release, read against its claims}\label{sec:ns}\label{sec:openai}

\paragraph{The claim} In September 2026 OpenAI announced a proof of finite-time blowup for the forced three-dimensional Navier--Stokes equations, one of the four alternatives of the Clay problem. It came with a Lean repository of \MtNsLeanFiles{} files and \MtNsLeanLines{} lines --- a Navier--Stokes library, an Euler library and the challenge statements --- proved against a statement that DeepMind's Formal Conjectures project had written months earlier \citep{OpenAINS,FormalConjectures}. The same release carries \MtOaManuscripts{} machine-written manuscripts in \MtOaFamilies{} families, with \MtOaChallenges{} Comparator challenges, Lean statements that a proof must satisfy, as its formal spine \citep{OpenAIMath}. The claim, for both, is that the mathematics has been machine-checked.

\paragraph{The reading} A formal certificate makes two claims (Section~\ref{sec:certifiers}): that the kernel accepts the proof, which a build decides, and that the statement proved is the theorem claimed, which no build decides. The second is read. Clay's wording \citep{Fefferman00}, the paper's Theorem 1.1 and the formal statement's hypotheses and conclusions were set side by side, and every difference was recorded. The reading issues no verdict of its own, so a weak point in a 166-page manuscript is recorded as a note, not a refutation. Each challenge of the release makes the same two claims: the kernel's, decided by replaying it, and the statement's, read against the theorem its manuscript claims. A manuscript whose headline rests on a finite object is a claim about that object, decided exactly wherever the object is published.

\paragraph{The decision} \MtNsFiles{} files were pinned on \MtNsFetched. The repository was built at the announced commit \NsCommit{}, over \MtNsJobsBuilt{} modules of its own (\MtNsJobsTotal{} with the library it imports), in \MtNsBuildMinutes{} minutes on the laptop, and the kernel reported only the standard axioms. The claimant's comparator accepted both challenge theorems, and a non-vacuity file of \MtNsNonVacuity{} declarations compiled against the result. The reading found that the formal statement is Clay's alternatives (C) and (D), with nothing added to the competitor class and nothing dropped from the data conditions. It re-derived \MtNsByHand{} steps of the manuscript, recorded \MtNsSlips{} slips, none affecting a conclusion, and named \MtNsWeakPoints{} weak points. It also found one clause of the paper's Theorem 1.1, the uniform energy bound $\sup_{0 \le t < 1}\|u(t)\|_{L^2} < \infty$, missing from the formal conclusions at that commit.

\section{The method evaluated}\label{sec:eval}

The exercises show what the method finds. This section measures the method itself, as a decision procedure, on the questions a checker must answer before it is trusted: does it accept what is false, decide what is malformed, or refuse what is true; how does its arithmetic compare with an independent library; how often does it fail to decide what a stronger method could; and what does it cost. A checker that refuses everything never accepts a false claim, so claims known to be true are planted beside the false ones, and a refusal of a true claim counts against the method.

\paragraph{The arithmetic against an independent library} The interval kernel was run on \MtArbCases{} seeded cases across \MtArbOps{} operations --- up to \MtArbPerOp{} each, drawn from full-precision doubles, subnormals, arguments near overflow and underflow, and boxes that straddle a function's critical points --- and every enclosure it returned was compared with the exact result, or for a box the exact range, computed in Arb \citep{Johansson17} at \MtArbPrec{} bits and more (Table~\ref{tab:arb}). Every enclosure contained the exact value; none of the \MtArbMalformed{} malformed inputs (a NaN, an inverted interval, division through zero, a non-integer or overflowing power, a logarithm at or below zero) was decided; and the comparison caught two planted wrong kernels, a round-to-nearest addition in \MtArbRedAdd{} of \MtArbRedAddOf{} sums and the platform's exponential taken as an enclosure in \MtArbRedExp{} of \MtArbRedExpOf{} cases, so it can fail. The \MtArbInDomainRefused{} refusals inside the domain are exponentials near underflow, which the kernel refuses rather than enclose. The audit ran in \MtArbSeconds{} seconds and runs in reduced form in every build. It is a test of the primitives on sampled inputs against code written by other people, not a proof that every path computes an interval extension, and it does not replace a review of the code by a specialist.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the three exercises show}

\paragraph{The defects are the verifier's kind} No bound was found false and no theorem was false. In each exercise the gap is between what was claimed and what was checked: a pairwise condition proved by structure and never checked pair by pair; a certificate's checker that compares in double precision what it encloses at a hundred digits; a package that certifies its own assembly and not the analysis it assembles; a clause of a theorem that the formal statement leaves out, and formal statements narrower than the theorems beside them. These are not the errors a referee reads for, and each was closed, or named, by a finite computation whose only hard part was deciding to make it.

\paragraph{The producer's checker shares the producer's blind spots} In every case the claimant had a checker, and in every case it stopped where the claim exceeded it. An independent decision is not a better checker; it is a checker that was not written by the claimant and so does not know where to stop.

\paragraph{Refusal is a result, and it has a size} Seventeen kissing rows were \textsc{needs data} for a month, each naming the files that would decide it; the request was answered and the rows closed, an opacity measured and then removed. The record package is \vF{} with the three inputs that would decide it named. The Navier--Stokes reading issues no verdict and defers to the kernel, so the missing clause became a scope sentence rather than an accusation, and the next commit closed it. Every refusal says what would convert it, and the kinds are never summed (Section~\ref{sec:eval}). The cost of exactness, by contrast, is small (Table~\ref{tab:cost}): the objects are integers, rationals and bounds.

\paragraph{A decision changes the record} Each exercise ends outside the system. The kissing claimant published its points in the form the check needed and linked the decision from its release. The registry's maintainer, who had written down beforehand what removes the mark of an unverified bound, removed it from three bounds on the replay and cites the replay on their pages. OpenAI's next commit proved the clause the reading found missing. A checker written by the producer cannot have these outcomes; they are what the grammar is for, since a \vC{} with its certificate can be cited, a \vF{} that names its files can be answered, and a scope sentence can be closed.

\subsection{Relation to existing practice}\label{sec:related}

Validated numerics and computer-assisted proof are the certifiers' ancestry: interval arithmetic \citep{Moore09,IEEE1788}, interval methods for nonlinear systems \citep{Neumaier90}, verification methods in floating point \citep{Rump10,Rump99}, correctly rounded and ball arithmetic \citep{Fousse07,Johansson17}, rigorous integration \citep{Kapela21} and the proofs they made possible \citep{Tucker02,Hales17}. Formal proof checks a proof against a stated theorem \citep{Lean4,Mathlib20}, and a translation that compiles may still not be the theorem intended \citep{Bastounis26}. Certifying algorithms return a certificate that a small independent checker verifies \citep{McConnell11}, as SAT solvers do with proofs that DRAT-trim checks \citep{Wetzler14}, and proof-carrying code ships a proof of a safety policy with the program \citep{Necula97}. Reproducibility initiatives re-run published computations \citep{Rougier17}. Table~\ref{tab:related} sets them side by side on the properties this system enforces. What it adds is the combination: the computations are aimed at other parties' claims, the outcome is one of three recorded words with a witness, a failure to decide is a result, and the inputs and code that decided each claim are pinned and re-derived. Each property exists elsewhere; this system enforces them together, by code that refuses. Its own weakest point is the last column: its semantics has a paper proof and its implementation is tested, not formally verified.

\subsection{What is not claimed, and the limits}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Not claimed} Interval arithmetic, the Krawczyk operator, floating-point filters, sums of squares and Sturm sequences are not claimed; they are cited. The validity of a declared model is not claimed: a \textsc{proved} engineering number holds for the declared model over the declared envelope, not for the sea or the reservoir. A kissing bound ``decided here'' is a statement about one published configuration, not about the record in that dimension. Nothing is certified in the regulatory sense; the word is used in the mathematical sense of Definition~\ref{def:verdicts} only. Nothing is a probability.

\paragraph{Locality and incompleteness} A certified maximum is the maximum in its box; a global statement over a family would need a branch-and-bound over the family's whole parameter space, which the system does not yet run. The system refuses what it cannot decide, and the refusal rate is a property of each campaign, published with it.

\paragraph{Readings are not decisions} Three results of this paper rest in part on readings rather than computations: the clause found absent from the Navier--Stokes formal conclusions, the statements of the mathematics release found short of their claims (Table~\ref{tab:gaps}), and the inventory of the record package of Section~\ref{sec:registry}. Each reading was produced by AI agents from a written brief; the second readings were made the same way, so they are a check on consistency and not an independent review; and no human specialist in the fields concerned has confirmed them. The clause is the one upstream later proved, which is consistent with the reading and does not confirm it. Until a specialist does, the gaps are reported as what the release does not show, never as errors.

\paragraph{Soundness rests on code} Proposition~\ref{prop:sound} is a theorem about interval extensions; whether a program computes one is a fact about the program. The defences --- second implementations, red controls, pinned bytes, calibrations, outside re-runs (Section~\ref{sec:discipline}) --- reduce that risk and do not remove it: a red control shows that one named failure is caught, not that the code is free of others, and two implementations written from one specification can agree and both be wrong; and they are not uniform: some certifiers have a second implementation that shares no code, and some, among them the point-in-polygon decider, the cosine-minimum certifier and the likelihood certifier, are re-run in the browser by the same bytes, which checks the record and not the code; and the entropy--influence replay of Section~\ref{sec:registry} has one implementation, guarded by its named checks.

\paragraph{What a read of the code for this paper found, and what was closed} An adversarial read of the code on 2026-10-09 found defects under the system's headline sentence, and they were closed the same day, with the correction published beside the old numbers (\url{certs/erratum-2026-10-09.json} pins the numbers, the commit and the pages that carried them). The most serious: the generation loop's closed-form hunt compared floating-point candidates with certified enclosures and counted a miss as a refutation, so that 54.6 million of its ``refutations'' were float comparisons; every candidate is now an exact rational or an enclosure, and a refutation is a proved disjointness. A word that covered both a proved failure and a straddle was split, with a straddle now \vF; a comparison of lower end with lower end was replaced by the champion's enclosure; the kernel gained its NaN and overflow refusals; and the build stopped writing pages with a red battery. None of this moved a verdict reported in the companion papers. \emph{Open, and named:} the older helper that encloses the platform's cosine by a derived pad, which contained the exact value in every case of Table~\ref{tab:arb} but is wide, is still called from six files, among them the radii-polynomial certifier, whose records are re-run when it is retired; one quadrature routine rigorous on dyadic cells of $[0,1]$ is called on another interval by one instrument; one battery labels some positive assertions as red controls; in the engineering demonstrator a \textsc{proved} consistency check means that the claim meets an outer enclosure, and the discharge thresholds are rounded outward to a tenth of a bar in floating point rather than in directed rounding; the transcendental tails are safe by a margin of twenty orders rather than by directed rounding; the same-author second implementations are not independent re-derivations (Table~\ref{tab:evidence}); and only the exact-class certificates are in a form a program other than this system's can check. Each is in the repository's debt register, and the system's own rule applies to them: a check that has not been made is not a check.

\section{Conclusions and recommendations}\label{sec:conclusions}

cert-machine decides the numbers and claims that machines propose with three verdicts, each carrying the object that justifies it, sound under one hypothesis that the arithmetic is built to secure. Its certifiers reduce the questions that arrive --- a zero, a maximum, a statement for every input, a sign, a nonnegative polynomial, a minimum, a formal proof --- to finitely many exact or outward-rounded decisions; its discipline keeps them honest by code that refuses; and its record is public and re-runs. The three exercises are what the method finds when it is pointed at machine-generated claims of three kinds: every one of \MtKwRows{} record configurations holds, pair by pair; the certificates behind a registry's unverified bounds hold, and the registry removed the marks on the replay, while a package that certifies only its own assembly is refused with the inputs that would decide it; and a formal proof is a proof of what it states and not of the clause it omits, until the clause is proved. Beside them, the graders that score machines on mathematics accept most of what is provably wrong. Measured as a decision procedure, the system refuted every planted false claim, certified every planted true one and decided no malformed input, and its arithmetic contained the exact value in all \MtArbCases{} cases checked against an independent library; what it could not decide, it refused, with the reason. What the system offers those who produce such claims is not a second opinion but a different object beside their number: a verdict with its witness, in which a refusal says what would decide it.

\paragraph{Recommendations} To producers of machine-generated mathematics: publish the object, not only its description --- the points of a configuration in one integer form, the witness a theorem rests on, the analytic inputs a certificate freezes, the data a fit was made from --- with a digest beside each file, because the finite check is cheap once the bytes exist and impossible until they do; state the scope of every check in the release itself (``pairwise inner products checked: false'' is the right sentence, and it was the one that made the exercise of Section~\ref{sec:math} possible); and when a formal statement is narrower than the theorem it accompanies, say so where the theorem is stated. To those who maintain the tables and records of a field: distinguish a bound that has been decided from outside from one that has only been claimed, write down what removes the distinction --- the registry of Section~\ref{sec:registry} does it with one mark and one sentence, and that sentence is what let an outside replay change its record --- and ask for the points. To those who build benchmarks and graders: where a reference answer has a certified enclosure, grade by the enclosure and not by a window, because the window's false positives can be minted without limit; where it has none, say which digits of the key are known; and plant red controls, because a grader that has never rejected a planted wrong answer has not been tested --- the same holds for a certificate's own checker, which should compare at the precision it encloses. To those who verify: refuse where you cannot decide, name the reason, and count the kinds of refusal apart --- a result that says what would decide it is worth more than a probability that nothing could.

\section{The arithmetic in detail}\label{app:arith}

\paragraph{Elementary and special functions} The certifiers of this paper trust no library function, because the platform's \texttt{Math.exp} and \texttt{Math.log} carry no accuracy guarantee in ECMAScript --- with one exception that Section~\ref{sec:limits} names: an older helper that encloses the platform's cosine by a derived pad rather than by a series is still called from six files, among them the radii-polynomial certifier of Section~\ref{sec:certifiers}, and its retirement for the series below is scheduled with the re-run of those records. Each function is a truncated series evaluated in interval arithmetic plus an explicit bound on the omitted tail: $\exp$ after reduction $x = k\ln 2 + r$, $|r| < 1$, by twenty-four Taylor terms and the tail $|r|^{25}/25!\,(1 - |r|/26)^{-1}$; $\log$ after $x = m\,2^e$ by the series of $\operatorname{atanh}$ in $v = (m-1)/(m+1) \in [0, 1/3)$, whose one-sided tail is valid because $v \ge 0$; $\sin$ and $\cos$ after reduction by an interval enclosure of $\pi/2$, with the box's enclosure forced to $\pm1$ wherever it may contain an extremum; $\ln 2$ and $\pi$ in exact rationals by $\operatorname{atanh}(1/3)$ and Machin's formula, converted outward. Monotone functions on wide boxes are evaluated at the endpoints. The special functions follow the same pattern: $\Gamma$ by Spouge's formula with its error bound taken ten times over; $\ln\Gamma$, $\psi$ and $\psi'$ by Binet's expansion after shifting the argument above twenty, the first omitted term as the bound; the regularized incomplete gamma function by its series with a geometric tail; Bessel functions by their power series with a certified ratio bound; $\operatorname{erfc}$ by Laplace's continued fraction, whose consecutive convergents bracket the value \citep{Abramowitz64}; and inverses ($\Phi^{-1}$, $P^{-1}$) by bisection that moves an end only on a certified sign. The tails are computed in round-to-nearest and are many orders below the one-ulp widening that absorbs them; that margin, not the tail's own rounding, is what makes them safe, and it is stated where it is used.

\paragraph{Long sums} A running interval sum rounds outward at each of $n$ additions, so its width grows linearly in $n$; at the $\RBuoyN$ hourly values of one buoy it is a thousand times what the terms carry. Each endpoint stream is instead summed by the compensated Sum2 algorithm \citep{Dekker71,Ogita05} and the error bound of \citet{Ogita05} is added outward with a factor of two to spare, which is what makes a likelihood over a hundred thousand data an enclosure narrow enough to rank families by \citep{P1}.

\paragraph{Derivatives} Gradients and Hessians that a certifier needs come from second-order forward differentiation over the same interval arithmetic (a value, a gradient and a Hessian carried through every operation), or from univariate Taylor jets, so that the derivative a proof uses is an extension of the function the proof is about. The remaining families carry hand-written scores and Hessians, evaluated in the same interval arithmetic.

\paragraph{Further certifiers} The certifiers of Table~\ref{tab:pathways} that the exercises do not use.

\subsection*{A sign, exactly}
Geometric predicates --- which side of an edge a point lies on --- are signs of small determinants. Each is evaluated first in double precision and trusted only when its magnitude clears a forward-error bound proved for the inputs \citep{Higham02,Fortune96}: with coordinates bounded by $M$ and $u = 2^{-53}$, the computed $2\times2$ orientation determinant is within $48uM^2$ of the exact one. Below the bound, the sign is recomputed exactly in integers, each decimal literal scaled to a fixed sixty-four places; a literal that would need more is refused rather than rounded. A point on an edge is reported \textsc{on}, an answer a floating-point test does not have. This is the static filter of exact geometric computation \citep{Shewchuk97,Yap97}, written against the literals a file contains rather than against their doubles. Over a contour benchmark it decided \EPredicatesSci{} predicates, \EExactPredicates{} of them in integers \citep{P1}.

\subsection*{A polynomial, nonnegative}
A claim $p \ge \gamma$ is certified by an identity $p - \gamma = \sum_i c_i q_i^2$ with rational polynomials $q_i$ and rational weights $c_i \ge 0$, checked by exact coefficient comparison; a negative weight or a nonzero residual refuses, and the residual is printed. Finding the decomposition is a semidefinite program \citep{Parrilo03,PeyrlParrilo08} that is never relied on: the solver proposes, the identity decides. A separate refuter evaluates $p$ at an exact witness point and refutes the claim if $p(w) < \gamma$.

\subsection*{A minimum of a cosine polynomial}
The minimum over $x$ of $\sum_{a \in A} \cos(ax)$ is the minimum over $y \in [-1, 1]$ of $P_A(y) = \sum_a T_a(y)$, a polynomial with integer Chebyshev coefficients. The certifier isolates the critical points with a Sturm chain of the square-free part of $P_A'$ on a slightly enlarged interval, refines each by interval Newton in rationals with outward balls around exact Horner values, encloses $P_A$ on each critical interval with the bound $a^2(a^2-1)/3$ of V.~Markov on the second derivative, adds the endpoints $\pm1$ exactly, and returns the hull of the smallest enclosures. A stronger certificate identifies the minimum as an algebraic number --- a resultant, a Sturm count of one inside the enclosure, irreducibility modulo a prime --- and is \vC\ only when all of these hold.
