\begin{abstract}
Search programs, surrogate models and large language models now propose constants, counterexamples, fitted parameters and operating points faster than they can be refereed, and each usually arrives with a checker written by its producer. We present cert-machine, a system that decides such claims independently of the claimant. A claim is read as a conjunction of inequalities over a declared box of inputs, under a universal or an epistemic reading, and receives one of three verdicts---certified, refuted or refused---each published with the object that justifies it: a certificate with its falsifier, a falsifying input, or, for a refusal, its reason, with, for a split refusal, two proved witnesses and, where a parameter is declared, the threshold at which the verdict would change. Verdicts are proved sound under the inclusion property of interval extensions, which the system secures with exact rational arithmetic and outward-rounded IEEE-754 interval arithmetic; the elementary and special functions it uses carry explicit tail bounds, stated as lemmas. Classical certifiers (the Krawczyk operator, second-order tests for maxima, boundary proofs, multilinear reductions, filtered exact geometric predicates, rational sums of squares and Chebyshev--Sturm minimisation) reduce analytic questions to finitely many such decisions, and a verification protocol (pinned inputs, mandatory adversarial controls, second implementations and in-browser re-execution) guards the code on which soundness rests. We evaluate the system on a pre-registered benchmark of \MtSaMutants{} claims of known truth, built by mutating \MtSaMutObjects{} published objects of its public register with an exact independent witness for each: \MtSaFalseCert{} false certifications and \MtSaFalseRef{} false refutations, \MtSaRefusalsOnFalse{} refusals, and two defects of the system's own found by the benchmark's rule and fixed on the record; on \MtArbCases{} comparisons of its interval kernel with an independent library, with no enclosure that failed to contain the exact value; and on a public register of \RegRows{} machine-generated claims from \RegClaimants{} claimants, whose refutations are the verifier's kind of defect. The contribution is the system and its semantics, not interval arithmetic, which is standard.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}

\item Three verdicts, each published with the object that justifies it; a refusal names its reason.

\item A pre-registered benchmark of \MtSaMutants{} claims of known truth: no false verdict; two defects found and fixed.

\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Context}
Machines now propose mathematics and engineering numbers at a rate no referee can follow. Conjectures on fundamental constants are generated from numerical coincidences~\citep{Raayoni21}; constructions in combinatorics and analysis are found by program search guided by language models~\citep{RomeraParedes24,Novikov25,GGSWT25} and by learned search~\citep{Wagner21,Davies21,Fawzi22}; autonomous research systems publish record configurations with their own verification files~\citep{Yang26}; long proofs arrive with machine formalisations~\citep{OpenAINS,OpenAIMath,Trinh24}; and in engineering, surrogate models, optimisers and language models propose operating points and design values~\citep{P2}. In every case the number reaches its reader together with a checker written by its producer.

\paragraph{Problem}
A checker written by the producer shares the producer's blind spots. The published record already shows the cost: a floating-point product printed as an exact constant, witnesses that hold only within the verifier's own tolerance, a checker that compares in double precision what it encloses at a hundred digits, a pairwise condition proved by structure and never checked pair by pair, and a clause present in a paper's theorem but absent from the formal statement that was proved~\citep{Register,Bastounis26}. What is missing is a decider that reads the claim from its published statement and bytes, never runs the claimant's program in its trust path, and returns an answer that a third party can re-check without trusting the decider either.

\paragraph{State of the art}
The ingredients of such a decider are decades old. Interval arithmetic with outward rounding provides enclosures with the inclusion property~\citep{Moore79,Moore09,Neumaier90}, now standardised~\citep{IEEE1788std,Revol17} and tested for conformance~\citep{Benet24,Revol23testing}; the Krawczyk operator proves existence and uniqueness of zeros~\citep{Krawczyk69,Rump10}; verification methods in floating point~\citep{Rump10,Rump99,Rump16fixed}, ball arithmetic~\citep{Johansson17,Johansson19} and interval global optimisation~\citep{HansenWalster04,Kearfott96,Neumaier04,Rump18global} made computer-assisted proofs routine~\citep{Tucker02,Hales17,Tucker11,vandenBergLessard15,GomezSerrano19,NakaoPlumWatanabe19}; and filtered exact predicates decide every sign in computational geometry~\citep{Fortune96,Shewchuk97,Yap97}. Closest to the present work in semantics are $\delta$-complete decision procedures for nonlinear real arithmetic~\citep{GaoAvigadClarke12,dReal13,Kong18}, which answer ``unsatisfiable'' or ``$\delta$-satisfiable'' with a witness box and from whose traces checkable proofs can be generated~\citep{Gao14proof}, and interval constraint propagation solvers that return boxes of unknown status~\citep{Loup13}. Proof assistants check nonlinear inequalities with Taylor-interval and sum-of-squares certificates inside a trusted kernel~\citep{SolovyevHales13,Magron15,MartinDorel16,Mahboubi19}, but they cannot certify that a formal statement is the one intended~\citep{Bastounis26,Kallel26}. For outputs of language-model pipelines, certificate architectures that treat abstention as a first-class outcome have recently been proposed~\citep{Koomullil26}, and test batteries sound by construction, whose reported defects are never spurious, have been built~\citep{Li26}.

\paragraph{Gap}
These tools answer a question posed by their own user about their own model. None of them, to our knowledge, is organised around deciding \emph{other parties'} published numbers: reading a printed digit string as the box it denotes, distinguishing a claim about every input in a box from a claim about an unknown true input inside it, and turning a failure to decide into a published result that states what would decide it. In engineering practice this second, epistemic reading is the usual one, and neither verification and validation nor uncertainty quantification~\citep{OberkampfRoy10} answers it: the former qualifies a model, the latter gives a dispersion, whereas the engineer asks whether a given rule holds for every input that the declared evidence allows.

\paragraph{Objective}
This paper asks three questions. (Q1)~Can a single three-valued decision grammar, proved sound under one hypothesis, cover claims arriving from mathematics, from engineering and from metocean statistics? (Q2)~What must the arithmetic and the software process guarantee for that hypothesis to hold in an implementation? (Q3)~On claims whose truth is known, how often does the system certify, refute or refuse, and at what cost?

\paragraph{Contributions}
The contribution is a system, not a numerical technique:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a three-valued decision grammar with witnesses, refusal as a result and flip thresholds, with a universal and an epistemic reading, and its soundness theorem (\cref{sec:grammar});
\item an arithmetic layer that secures the theorem's single hypothesis on IEEE-754 doubles and exact rationals, with a lemma for each elementary and special function it uses (\cref{sec:intervals}, \ref{app:arith});
\item a catalogue of standard certifiers, each mapped to the grammar with its witness and its refusal reasons (\cref{sec:certifiers});
\item a verification protocol whose rules are executable gates rather than guidelines, with the gates that are not yet enforced listed as such (\cref{sec:protocol}); and
\item a pre-registered evaluation of the system against claims of known truth, built by mutating its own public register with independent exact witnesses, which found two defects of the system by its rule and measures what soundness promises (\cref{sec:metrics}, \cref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}
Interval arithmetic, the Krawczyk operator, filtered predicates, sums of squares and Sturm sequences are cited, not claimed.

\paragraph{Outline}
\Cref{sec:method} describes the method: its foundations in the literature, the mathematical formulation (arithmetic, verdicts, soundness and termination, certifiers), the comparison metrics and decision rules, and the software architecture with its verification protocol. \Cref{sec:results} reports the benchmark with known truth, the kernel's comparison with an independent library and the public register with its use cases; \cref{sec:discussion} discusses what the results show, counts the independence the system can claim, places it beside existing practice and states the threats to validity and the limits; \cref{sec:conclusions} concludes. The appendices hold the reproduction table, the arithmetic in detail with the function lemmas, the thresholds, the certifiers in detail and the decision loop.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
%% =====================================================================

The methodology is presented in four layers, from the mathematics on which a verdict rests to the code that computes it. \Cref{sec:foundations} reviews the results the system is built on. \Cref{sec:math} states the mathematical formulation: the arithmetic, the claims and their verdicts, the soundness and termination results, and the certifiers. \Cref{sec:metrics} defines the metrics by which verdicts are compared with ground truth and with other tools, and the decision rules, each with its mathematical basis. Only then does \cref{sec:architecture} describe the software architecture and the protocol that guards it.

Four implementations recur below as running instances. (a)~The \emph{flow-line gate} of the engineering demonstrator~\citep{P2} decides, under the epistemic reading, whether the wellhead pressure of a water-injection line stays below a rule over a declared envelope of roughness, viscosity, diameter, density, elevation and length, with Colebrook's friction law solved inside the enclosure; the pump pressure is its operating choice. (b)~The \emph{rock-physics engine}~\citep{P3} decides, over declared boxes of rock and fluid properties, whether a time-lapse seismic change is guaranteed to be detectable. (c)~The \emph{likelihood certifier} of the metocean study~\citep{P1} proves the maximum-likelihood fits of extreme-value families to a buoy's hourly record and ranks the families. (d)~A \emph{universal branch-and-bound} decides a statement for every input in a box by bisection on the widest coordinate; its instance in the register bounds, for every monic polynomial of a given degree with all roots in $[-1,1]$, the measure of the set where the polynomial is below one in absolute value, by subdividing the box of root configurations until a certified upper bound falls below the bar~\citep{Register}.

%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Foundations}\label{sec:foundations}
%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

The system rests on interval analysis. Since Moore's formalisation of arithmetic on intervals~\citep{Moore79,Moore09}, its central result---the \emph{fundamental theorem of interval arithmetic}, by which the interval evaluation of an expression encloses the range of the function it denotes---has turned finite floating-point computation into proof~\citep{Neumaier90,Moore09}. The arithmetic is standardised as IEEE~1788-2015~\citep{IEEE1788std}, test suites check the conformance of libraries to it~\citep{Benet24}, and directed rounding can be emulated when the rounding mode cannot be set~\citep{Rump16fixed}, the situation of the implementation described here (\cref{prop:outward}); the interval layer is not claimed conformant, and no conformance suite has been run on it (\cref{sec:limits}). Verification methods carry the theorem into floating point with directed rounding~\citep{Rump10,Rump99,Johansson17}, interval branch-and-bound turns enclosures into rigorous global optimisation~\citep{HansenWalster04,Kearfott96,Neumaier04,Rump18global}, and the same arithmetic underlies computer-assisted proofs from dynamics to the Kepler conjecture and partial differential equations~\citep{Tucker02,Immler18,Hales17,GomezSerrano19,NakaoPlumWatanabe19}. A parallel tradition, exact geometric computation~\citep{Yap97}, decides discrete predicates exactly by filtering a floating-point evaluation through a proved error bound and falling back to exact arithmetic only when the filter fails~\citep{Fortune96,Shewchuk97}. Both traditions give the same guarantee: a computed sign or bound is a proved statement about the real numbers, not an approximation of one.

The second foundation concerns what cannot be decided. The first-order theory of real closed fields is decidable~\citep{Tarski51}, but adding the elementary functions makes it undecidable in general whether an expression in one real variable is nonnegative everywhere, or identically zero~\citep{Richardson68}. A sound procedure over expressions with $\exp$, $\log$ or $\sin$ must therefore be allowed to return no answer, and the existing tools differ in how they treat that answer: $\delta$-complete decision procedures weaken the question, returning ``unsatisfiable'' or ``$\delta$-satisfiable'' for a perturbation $\delta$ chosen by the user~\citep{GaoAvigadClarke12,dReal13}; interval constraint propagation returns boxes of unknown status~\citep{Loup13}. Three-valued logic gives the undetermined answer a semantics: in Kleene's strong logic~\citep{Kleene38} a conjunction is false if any conjunct is false, undetermined if none is false and some is undetermined, and true otherwise. The verdict lattice of \cref{sec:grammar} adopts this semantics, with refusal in the role of the undetermined value. The analogy is operational, not semantic: a refusal records that the procedure did not decide, not that the claim lacks a truth value.

The third foundation is the separation between finding an answer and checking it. A \emph{certifying algorithm} returns, with each output, a witness from which a simple and independent checker can confirm the output without trusting the algorithm~\citep{McConnell11}; proof-carrying code applies the same idea to programs~\citep{Necula97}, and SAT solvers ship proofs that small verified checkers replay~\citep{Wetzler14,CakeLPR21}. Sums-of-squares certificates found by semidefinite programming and checked in exact rationals~\citep{Parrilo03,PeyrlParrilo08}, and Taylor-interval certificates replayed inside a proof assistant~\citep{SolovyevHales13,Magron15,MartinDorel16}, are instances in nonlinear real arithmetic. From statistical decision theory comes the last ingredient: a classifier that may abstain is evaluated by its risk on the cases it decides and by the fraction it decides, the error--reject tradeoff~\citep{Chow70,ElYaniv10,Geifman17,Franc21}. Closest in intent is recent work on language-model outputs that builds test batteries sound by construction, so that a reported defect is never spurious, and states their detection limits~\citep{Li26}; cert-machine asks the same of every verdict, not only of refutations. The system combines these three lines: soundness from the inclusion property, a three-valued answer in which refusal is a result, and a witness for every verdict that an independent checker can re-run.

\subsection{Mathematical formulation}\label{sec:math}

\subsubsection{Interval arithmetic and the inclusion property}\label{sec:intervals}

Let $\IR$ denote the set of closed bounded real intervals $X=[\underline{x},\overline{x}]$, with width $w(X)=\overline{x}-\underline{x}$ and midpoint $m(X)=(\underline{x}+\overline{x})/2$. A box is a vector $X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)\in\IR^n$, with $w(X)=\max_i w(X_i)$. For $\circ\in\{+,-,\times,\div\}$ the interval operations are the set images $X\circ Y=\{x\circ y : x\in X,\ y\in Y\}$ ($0\notin Y$ if $\circ=\div$), computed from the endpoints~\citep{Moore09,Neumaier90}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:endpoints}
\begin{aligned}
X+Y&=[\underline{x}+\underline{y},\ \overline{x}+\overline{y}], &
X-Y&=[\underline{x}-\overline{y},\ \overline{x}-\underline{y}],\\
X\times Y&=[\min S,\ \max S],\ S=\{\underline{x}\underline{y},\underline{x}\overline{y},\overline{x}\underline{y},\overline{x}\overline{y}\}, &
X\div Y&=X\times[1/\overline{y},\ 1/\underline{y}].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Interval extension]\label{def:ext}
Let $g:D\subseteq\R^n\to\R$. A map $G$ from boxes in $D$ to $\IR$ is an \emph{interval extension} of $g$ if $g(X)=\{g(x):x\in X\}\subseteq G(X)$ for every box $X\subseteq D$ (the \emph{inclusion property}), and \emph{inclusion isotone} if $X\subseteq Y$ implies $G(X)\subseteq G(Y)$. This is weaker than Moore's extension, which also asks $G([x,x])=g(x)$; it is what Neumaier~\citep{Neumaier90} calls an inclusion function, and nothing below needs more.
\end{definition}

The \emph{natural extension} of an expression replaces each real operation by its interval operation and each elementary function by an enclosure of its range.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental theorem of interval arithmetic~\citep{Moore09,Neumaier90}]\label{thm:ftia}
If every elementary function occurring in an expression for $g$ has an inclusion-isotone interval extension, then the natural extension $G$ of $g$ is an inclusion-isotone interval extension of $g$.
\end{theorem}

\Cref{thm:ftia} is the only property that the soundness of a verdict requires (\cref{prop:sound}). Its price is overestimation: $G(X)$ may be much wider than $g(X)$, because each occurrence of a variable is treated as independent. Writing $q(A,B)=\max(|\underline{a}-\underline{b}|,|\overline{a}-\overline{b}|)$ for the Hausdorff distance between intervals, an extension has \emph{convergence order}~\citep{Scholz12} $\omega$ on $B$ if there are $\kappa>0$ and $\eta\ge0$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order}
q\bigl(g(X),G(X)\bigr)\le\kappa\,w(X)^{\omega}+\eta\qquad\text{for every box }X\subseteq B.
\end{equation}
In exact arithmetic $\eta=0$. In floating point $\eta>0$: outward rounding adds at least one ulp per operation and each function enclosure adds its tail bound, neither of which shrinks with $w(X)$, so $\eta$ is the overestimation floor of the implementation on $B$. When every elementary function in the expression has a Lipschitz interval extension, the natural extension has order $\omega=1$, and the mean-value form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mvform}
G_{\mathrm{MV}}(X)=G\bigl([c,c]\bigr)+\nabla G(X)^{\top}(X-c),\qquad c\in X,
\end{equation}
with $c$ a double near $m(X)$, since $m(X)$ itself need not be representable, has order $\omega=2$~\citep{Neumaier90}, both with $\eta=0$ in exact arithmetic. Higher-order enclosures, such as Taylor models~\citep{BerzMakino98}, reduce the overestimation further. cert-machine uses the natural and mean-value forms, endpoint evaluation of monotone functions, the Krawczyk operator (\cref{thm:krawczyk}) and second-order Taylor remainders in quadrature; it uses no Taylor models or affine forms. The reason is the claims it meets: they are low-dimensional, and either exact or decided with a margin that bisection reaches; the one family where the enclosure straddles the bar at the budget (the cosine minima of \cref{sec:eval}) is the place a higher-order form would help, and it is listed as future work rather than built. The order governs how much subdivision a decision costs (\cref{prop:termination}), never whether the decision is sound.

\paragraph{Outward rounding on doubles}\label{sec:arith}
IEEE-754 arithmetic in round-to-nearest returns, for each of $+,-,\times,\div$, the double nearest the exact result~\citep{IEEE754,Goldberg91,Muller18}. Evaluating \cref{eq:endpoints} in round-to-nearest therefore does not enclose the exact endpoints, and the enclosure must be widened. JavaScript cannot set the rounding mode, so directed rounding is emulated by moving each computed endpoint one unit in the last place outward~\citep{Rump09,Rump16fixed}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:outward}
Let $\circ\in\{+,-,\times,\div\}$, let $A,B$ be intervals of doubles---pairs of non-NaN doubles with lower end at most upper end, an infinite endpoint allowed as a bound---with $0\notin B$ when $\circ=\div$, and let $y_-,y_+$ be the round-to-nearest values of the smallest and largest endpoint combinations $a\circ b$. If no endpoint combination is indeterminate ($0\cdot\infty$, $\infty-\infty$, $\infty/\infty$), then $A\circ B\subseteq[\mathrm{down}(y_-),\mathrm{up}(y_+)]$, where down and up step one double toward $\mp\infty$ and fix $\mp\infty$; an exact result beyond the largest double rounds to $\pm\infty$, which the step fixes, so overflow is enclosed.
\end{proposition}
The proof is in \ref{app:arith}. The domain of \cref{prop:outward} is enforced, not assumed: a malformed operand or an indeterminate result is refused by exception and never decided; division by an interval containing zero is refused, not extended; a non-integer or overflowing integer exponent is refused, and a real power of a positive base is $\exp(p\log x)$. The trust base is stated once: the correctly rounded $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$ and square root that IEEE-754 mandates and the engine implements, its arbitrary-precision integers, and nothing in the platform's mathematical library, which ECMAScript leaves ``implementation-approximated''. Where the quantity is rational or a tie must be decided, exact arithmetic is used instead: reduced fractions of arbitrary-precision integers, to which doubles convert losslessly, with exact elimination that reports a singular matrix rather than a small pivot; where more precision is required, a separate arbitrary-precision arithmetic with true directed rounding, converted to doubles outward with an exact check.

\paragraph{Elementary and special functions}
\Cref{thm:ftia} requires an enclosure for every elementary function, and no library function is trusted as an enclosure, because ECMAScript gives no accuracy guarantee for the platform's exponential or logarithm---with one exception at the time of writing: an older helper that encloses the platform's cosine and sine by a derived pad is still called by six modules, among them the radii-polynomial certifier, and is scheduled for retirement (\cref{sec:limits}). Each function $f$ is evaluated as a truncated series $S_N$ in interval arithmetic plus an explicit bound $R_N$ on the omitted tail, so that $f(x)\in S_N(x)+[-R_N,R_N]$, and the implementation must satisfy:

\begin{assumption}[Function enclosures]\label{ass:functions}
For every function $f$ of \cref{tab:functions} and every box $X$ in its declared domain, either the call is refused or the returned interval $F(X)$ satisfies $f(X)\subseteq F(X)$, including near zeros of $f$.
\end{assumption}

\ref{app:arith} states, for each function, the reduction, the series, the tail bound and the domain on which the bound holds (\cref{lem:exp,lem:log,lem:trig,lem:gamma,lem:lgamma,lem:gammaP,lem:bessel,lem:erf}), and how the tail is added: as an interval $[-R_N,R_N]$ (or $[0,R_N]$ for a one-sided tail) by an outward-rounded addition, the bound itself evaluated in interval arithmetic and its upper end taken, so that nothing is absorbed into the widening of \cref{prop:outward}. The kernel and its functions have been compared with Arb~\citep{Johansson17} on \MtArbCases{} seeded cases over \MtArbOps{} operations, with no enclosure that failed to contain the exact value (\cref{sec:arb}); that is evidence on sampled inputs, and the lemmas are the proof.

\paragraph{Long sums}
A running interval sum rounds outward at each of $n$ additions, so its width grows linearly in $n$; at the \RBuoyN{} hourly values of one buoy it is about a thousand times what the terms carry. Each endpoint stream $p_1,\dots,p_n$ is therefore summed by the compensated algorithm Sum2 of Ogita, Rump and Oishi~\citep[Proposition~4.5]{Ogita05}, whose result $\tilde s$ satisfies, also in the presence of underflow,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sum2}
\Bigl|\tilde s-\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\Bigr|\le u\,\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\Bigr| +\gamma_{n-1}^2\sum_{i=1}^n|p_i|,\qquad \gamma_k=\frac{ku}{1-ku},\quad u=2^{-53},
\end{equation}
provided $nu<1$ and no overflow occurs. The implementation evaluates the bound from computable quantities as $u|\tilde s|+2\gamma_n^2\sum|p_i|$, each step rounded upward, and adds it outward to each endpoint; the doubled term covers the difference between $u|\tilde s|$ and $u|\sum p_i|$ (supplement, S1), and both hypotheses are checked by name: a stream with $nu\ge1$ or an overflowed partial sum is refused.

\paragraph{Derivatives}
Gradients and Hessians come from second-order forward differentiation over the same interval arithmetic, or from univariate Taylor jets, so that the derivative a proof uses (e.g.\ $\nabla G$ in \cref{eq:mvform}) is an extension of the function the proof is about. Families with hand-written scores and Hessians evaluate them in the same arithmetic.

\subsubsection{Claims, readings and verdicts}\label{sec:grammar}

\paragraph{Boxes and claims}
A box with rational endpoints is the object a claim is decided on; a double is a rational, so a box of doubles is such a box. A \emph{declared envelope} $B$ is a box each of whose sides carries the source of its bounds (a survey, a data sheet, a chart). A printed number is the box its digits allow: a decimal $\hat d$ printed with $k$ fractional digits denotes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:printed}
\bigl[\hat d-\tfrac12 10^{-k},\ \hat d+\tfrac12 10^{-k}\bigr],
\end{equation}
so a printed $0.0634$ is $[0.06335,0.06345]$, and both endpoints are exact rationals. \Cref{eq:printed} assumes that the printed value was rounded to nearest. The claim schema fixes that convention for a printed \emph{value} and refuses any other box for it; a printed \emph{bound} ($\le$, $<$, $\ge$, $>$) is taken as the exact rational its digits denote, and a value that was truncated or given to significant figures must be declared as an explicit rational box. Declared envelope ends are read as the nearest double, widened outward by one ulp unless the conversion is exact.

\begin{definition}[Claim]\label{def:claim}
A \emph{claim} is a pair $(B,\varphi)$ of a domain $B$---a box, or a finite set of rational points, covered by its thin boxes---and a predicate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:claim}
\varphi(x)\equiv\bigwedge_{j=1}^m g_j(x)\le c_j
\end{equation}
whose functions have interval extensions $G_j$; an equality, or an exact predicate---the orientation of three points, or a Boolean combination of exact integer tests such as the angular test of a kissing configuration---is the special case decided in exact arithmetic. It is read either \emph{universally}, as $\forall x\in B:\varphi(x)$, or \emph{epistemically}, as $\varphi(x^\dagger)$ for an unknown true input $x^\dagger\in B$.
\end{definition}
The form is deliberately narrow: a conjunction of inequalities over one box, with disjunction allowed only among exact tests and no alternation of quantifiers. A statement outside it---an existential claim, an inequality quantified over a parameter that is itself unknown, a claim about an object that was not published---is not forced into the schema; it is refused with the reason \emph{the instrument declined}, or marked \emph{needs data} with the bytes that would decide it named (\cref{tab:words}). Under the universal reading a single proved counterexample refutes the claim; under the epistemic reading it shows only that the declared evidence does not settle the question.

\begin{definition}[Verdicts]\label{def:verdicts}
Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a finite cover of $B$ by boxes. The claim is \emph{certified} if $\sup G_j(X)\le c_j$ for every $j$ and every $X\in\mathcal{X}$; \emph{refuted}, under the universal reading, if some box $X^*\subseteq B$ has $\inf G_j(X^*)>c_j$ for some $j$, and under the epistemic reading if every $X\in\mathcal{X}$ has $\inf G_j(X)>c_j$ for some $j$; and \emph{refused} otherwise. An epistemic refusal is \emph{split} when two points $x^-,x^+\in B$ have been proved to violate and to satisfy $\varphi$, and \emph{unresolved} otherwise.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Soundness]\label{prop:sound}
If every $G_j$ is an interval extension of $g_j$, then \CERT{} implies $\varphi(x)$ for every $x\in B$; \REFT{} implies $\neg\varphi(x)$ for some $x\in B$ under the universal reading and for every $x\in B$ under the epistemic reading; and a split \REFS{} implies that neither $\forall x\in B\,\varphi(x)$ nor $\forall x\in B\,\neg\varphi(x)$ holds, so that $\varphi(x^\dagger)$ is not determined by the declared envelope.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $x\in B$ and $X\in\mathcal{X}$ contain it. By the inclusion property $g_j(x)\in g_j(X)\subseteq G_j(X)$, hence $g_j(x)\le\sup G_j(X)\le c_j$ for every $j$. The refutations follow by the same argument with the inequality reversed, on $X^*$ or on every member of the cover. For a split refusal, $x^+$ and $x^-$ are points of $B$ at which $\varphi$ is proved true and false; either could be $x^\dagger$.
\end{proof}
In the engineering gates the box decided is the double hull $B^+\supseteq B$ obtained by widening each declared end outward. Certification and epistemic refutation transfer from $B^+$ to $B$. A witness of a split refusal is a corner of $B$ itself: the declared decimal, enclosed by its two neighbouring doubles and decided as that thin box, so that a verdict proved over the box is proved at the exact corner, which is a point of $B$. A witness that a floating-point search proposes (the rock-physics engine) is a double of $B^+$, within one ulp of $B$, and is reported as such.

Soundness says nothing about how often the procedure decides. The following result, in the spirit of $\delta$-completeness~\citep{GaoAvigadClarke12}, states when bisection is guaranteed to certify and what it costs.

\begin{proposition}[Termination for robust claims]\label{prop:termination}
Let each $G_j$ have convergence order $\omega$ with constants $\kappa$ and $\eta$ on $B$ (\cref{eq:order}), and let the claim hold with margin $\varepsilon>\eta$: $g_j(x)\le c_j-\varepsilon$ for every $j$ and every $x\in B$. Then every box $X\subseteq B$ with $w(X)\le((\varepsilon-\eta)/\kappa)^{1/\omega}$ is certified, and bisection of the widest coordinate certifies the claim at depth at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:depth}
d^*=n\Bigl\lceil\log_2\Bigl(w(B)\,\bigl(\kappa/(\varepsilon-\eta)\bigr)^{1/\omega}\Bigr)\Bigr\rceil^{+},
\end{equation}
with at most $2^{d^*}$ boxes in the final cover.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{eq:order}, $\sup G_j(X)\le\sup g_j(X)+\kappa w(X)^\omega+\eta\le c_j-\varepsilon+\eta+\kappa w(X)^\omega\le c_j$ when $\kappa w(X)^\omega\le\varepsilon-\eta$. Under widest-coordinate bisection, every $n$ consecutive splits along a branch at least halve $w(X)$: a coordinate wider than half the current width is split before any narrower one, and at most $n$ such coordinates exist; after $d^*$ splits $w(X)\le 2^{-d^*/n}w(B)\le((\varepsilon-\eta)/\kappa)^{1/\omega}$.
\end{proof}

\Cref{prop:termination} explains the refusals the evaluation must expect. A claim whose margin does not exceed the floor ($\varepsilon\le\eta$; in particular $\varepsilon=0$, a bound attained at a point) is never certified by bisection alone and needs one of the certifiers of \cref{sec:certifiers} or a higher precision that lowers $\eta$; and the box count grows as $(w(B)(\kappa/(\varepsilon-\eta))^{1/\omega})^{n}$, so a depth budget $d_{\max}$ turns high-dimensional or nearly tight claims into refusals by budget rather than into wrong verdicts. The depth bound is proved for widest-coordinate bisection, the rule of the universal branch-and-bound. The engineering gates choose the coordinate by sensitivity instead (the largest floating-point variation of the model across the box; or, after the one-signed coordinates are fixed at the deciding end, the largest $|\partial g/\partial x_i(X)|\,w(X_i)$, the monotonicity test and a smear-type rule~\citep{HansenWalster04,RatzCsendes95}), and for those rules no depth bound is claimed. The rule changes cost and termination, never soundness.

\paragraph{Composition}
Order the verdicts as $\REFT\prec\REFS\prec\CERT$. A record with several checks $\varphi=\bigwedge_i\varphi_i$ receives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:compose}
v(\varphi)=\min_{\prec}\{v(\varphi_i)\},
\end{equation}
that is, \REFT{} if any check is refuted, otherwise \REFS{} if any is refused, otherwise \CERT---the strong conjunction of Kleene's three-valued logic~\citep{Kleene38}.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:compose}
Under \cref{prop:sound}, if all checks $\varphi_i$ are decided on the same box $B$ and under the same reading, the composition rule \cref{eq:compose} is sound for $\varphi$ under that reading.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
If every $\varphi_i$ is certified, $\varphi_i(x)$ holds on $B$ for every $i$, hence $\varphi$. Under the universal reading a refuted $\varphi_i$ gives $x\in B$ with $\neg\varphi_i(x)$, hence $\neg\varphi(x)$; under the epistemic reading it gives $\neg\varphi_i(x)$ for every $x\in B$, hence $\neg\varphi$ on all of $B$.
\end{proof}
A split refusal does not survive conjunction: if $\varphi_1$ holds only on one half of $B$ and $\varphi_2$ only on the other, each is split while $\varphi_1\wedge\varphi_2$ is false on all of $B$. A composed refusal is therefore unresolved unless its witnesses are proved for $\varphi$ itself. In the implementation the composition is computed from the checks' words alone and carries no witness, so a composed refusal is never labelled split; the split refusals of the record are issued by the two engineering gates at the level of one check, a single inequality, and a receipt that composes several checks carries that check's witnesses beside the composed word, never as its own. Every check of a record shares one box and one reading; items of one record that are decided on different objects are not composed by \cref{eq:compose} but reported as \verdict{mixed} with the counts of each verdict (\cref{tab:words}); a record with refuted and refused items composes to \REFT{} by \cref{eq:compose} and carries both counts.

A refusal is terminal: it is never retried at lower rigour, never converted into a probability and never dropped from the record. The only path from \REFS{} to \CERT{} is a new exact argument---a finer cover, an extension of higher order, another certifier, or the bytes the claimant had not yet published. The three verdicts are not probabilities, and there is no fourth.

\paragraph{Witnesses}
Every verdict carries the object that justifies it, in the sense of a certifying algorithm~\citep{McConnell11}. A certification carries its \emph{certificate}: the inputs pinned by digest, the hull of its enclosures with the counts of its cover, and a statement of what would make it fail (the \emph{falsifier}); the constructor refuses a certificate without a stated falsifier, a proved verdict without evidence, and a refusal without a reason. A refutation carries the falsifying object, printed; a split refusal its two witnesses, decided as thin boxes by the same enclosures. An exact certificate re-checks without the system's code; an interval certificate records the hull and the counts of its cover, not the cover, and is re-checked by re-deciding the claim from the pinned inputs (exporting the cover is future work).

The public register composes the three verdicts into words that say what they are made of (\cref{tab:words}); none is a fourth verdict. The register's builder refuses a row that carries no scope sentence or no kind, so a \CERT{} is never published without its scope; that the kinds of refusal (instrument declined, claimant published nothing, budget exhausted, box did not close, audit reach stated) are never summed into one total is a rule of reporting, not yet a check in code.

\subsubsection{Flip thresholds}\label{sec:thresholds}
A split refusal leaves open a question that has a size. Let the claim depend on a declared scalar $p$---an operating choice, or one end of an interval that a measurement could narrow---through boxes $B(p)$ and predicates $\varphi_p$, and write $\Tp(p)$ for ``$\varphi_p(x)$ holds for every $x\in B(p)$'' and $\Fp(p)$ for ``$\varphi_p(x)$ fails for every $x\in B(p)$''.

\begin{assumption}[Monotone truth]\label{ass:mono}
$\Tp(p)$ implies $\Tp(p')$ for every $p'\le p$, and $\Fp(p)$ implies $\Fp(p')$ for every $p'\ge p$. A sufficient condition is that the boxes do not depend on $p$, $B(p)=B$, and every $g_j(x;p)$ is nondecreasing in $p$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:thresholds}
Let \cref{ass:mono} and the hypothesis of \cref{prop:sound} hold, let $B(p)$ be nonempty, and define $p_T=\sup\{p:\Tp(p)\}$ and $p_F=\inf\{p:\Fp(p)\}$, so that $p_T\le p_F$. If the procedure, at a value $m$ of the parameter, certifies the claim, then $\Tp(p)$ holds for every $p\le m$ and $p_T\ge m$; if it refutes it epistemically, then $\Fp(p)$ holds for every $p\ge m$ and $p_F\le m$; if it returns a split refusal, then $p_T\le m\le p_F$.
\end{proposition}
The proof, the closed form where $p$ enters additively ($g(x;p)=p+h(x)$ on a fixed box: $\Tp(p)$ for every $p\le c-\overline h$ and $\Fp(p)$ for every $p>c-\underline h$, from one enclosure $[\underline h,\overline h]$ of $h$ over $B$, every step in directed rounding), the second case---a measurement that narrows one side of the box, where the half of \cref{ass:mono} for $\Tp$ fails and the nesting of \cref{prop:nested} replaces it---and the way each running instance verifies the hypotheses are in the supplementary material (S2). The threshold turns a refusal into a measurement or an operating change with a known effect on the verdict: at this pump pressure the rule is proved, and with the roughness shown to be at least this value it is proved too~\citep{P2}.

\paragraph{Proposers}
Every certifier takes its candidate from a floating-point search---Nelder--Mead and Levenberg--Marquardt for a likelihood, Newton for a root, sampling for a minimum, a semidefinite solver for a decomposition, a language model for a construction---and none of these is in the trust path. \subsubsection{Metrics}\label{sec:metricsdef}

\paragraph{Verdict--truth confusion matrix}
Each benchmark claim $i=1,\dots,N$ carries a ground-truth label $\tau_i\in\{\text{true},\text{false},\text{malformed}\}$---it holds on all of $B$, it fails at a proved witness, or it is not a well-formed instance of its claim schema---established by an oracle that is independent of cert-machine and itself exact (\cref{sec:evaluation}). It receives a verdict $v_i\in\{\CERT,\REFT,\REFS\}$, a refusal being split or unresolved. The confusion matrix $N_{v\tau}=\#\{i: v_i=v,\ \tau_i=\tau\}$ is the primary result. \Cref{prop:sound} predicts structural zeros in the cells that would be errors:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zeros}
N_{\CERT,\text{false}}=0,\quad N_{\REFT,\text{true}}=0,\quad N_{\CERT,\text{malf.}}=N_{\REFT,\text{malf.}}=0,
\end{equation}
the last pair because a malformed object must be refused by its shape check and never decided. Any nonzero entry in \cref{eq:zeros} is a defect in code or in a hypothesis, and is reported individually. Under the epistemic reading a fourth label, \emph{undetermined} (the predicate holds at one proved point of $B$ and fails at another), has the split refusal as its correct answer; the benchmark of \cref{sec:results} contains universal and exact claims only, so that label does not arise there.

\paragraph{Upper bound on the error rate}
Observing zero errors on $N_\text{F}$ false claims does not show that the error probability $\theta$ is zero. The one-sided Clopper--Pearson bound~\citep{ClopperPearson34} at level $1-\beta$ after $x$ errors is the beta quantile $\theta_U=\mathrm{Beta}^{-1}(1-\beta;\ x+1,\ N_\text{F}-x)$, which at $x=0$ is $1-\beta^{1/N_\text{F}}\approx 3/N_\text{F}$ for $\beta=0.05$, the ``rule of three''~\citep{Hanley83}. The same bound is reported for false refutations on true claims. The bound presumes independent draws from a population of claims, whereas the benchmark is assembled from chosen records and injected faults, and a defect in one code path affects every claim of its class; it is therefore read as a description of this benchmark, not as an error rate that transfers. \Cref{prop:sound} is what the system claims mathematically; the bound measures the gap between the code and the theorem on the claims tested.

\paragraph{Coverage and selective risk}
A sound three-valued decider is a selective classifier with a reject option~\citep{Chow70,ElYaniv10,Geifman17}, in which a split refusal is an informative answer and only an unresolved refusal is an abstention. Writing $D=N-N_{\REFS_{\mathrm{u}}\cdot}$ for the number of informative answers, its \emph{coverage} is $C=D/N$ and its \emph{selective risk} $R$ is the number of errors of \cref{eq:zeros} divided by $D$. Because the mix of labels is set by the benchmark's design, $C$ is also reported per label: the certification rate on true claims, the refutation rate on false claims and the refusal rate on malformed ones, each by claim class, with the unresolved refusals broken down by reason (never summed across reasons). Soundness predicts $R=0$; the measured $R$ tests the implementation against that prediction, and the performance of a sound implementation is then carried by $C$. A floating-point checker is placed on the same risk--coverage plane~\citep{ElYaniv10,Franc21}: it has $C=1$ and $R\ge0$; cert-machine is predicted to have $C\le1$ and $R=0$.

\paragraph{Expected cost of a decision}
With a cost $c_e$ for acting on a wrong verdict, $c_s$ for the measurement a split refusal asks for and $c_r$ for the argument an unresolved refusal asks for, the expected cost per claim is $L=c_e\,R\,C+c_s\,S+c_r\,(1-C)$, $S$ the fraction of split refusals, in the spirit of Chow's error--reject tradeoff~\citep{Chow70}: $c_sS+c_r(1-C)$ for cert-machine against $c_eR_\text{fp}$ for a floating-point checker, both measured in the benchmark, so the break-even ratio $c_e/c_r$ is reported as a function of the proportion of false claims---which the injected faults inflate---rather than assumed.

\paragraph{Tightness, cost and agreement}
For a certified enclosure $F$ the number of certified decimal digits is $\delta(F)=-\log_{10}(w(F)/|m(F)|)$ for $0\notin F$; for a split refusal the thresholds of \cref{sec:thresholds} say how far an operating change or a measurement must go. For each decision the harness records wall-clock time, CPU time and peak memory, cold and warm, under two profiles (a portable one with a wall-clock cap, an extended one without), and the certificates keep the boxes evaluated and the depth reached. Verdicts are deterministic, so agreement with a second implementation is exact agreement, field by field or by digest, and a single disagreement is a defect; against an established library the primary result is the table of containment per operation. A paired comparison with another decider (McNemar's test~\citep{McNemar47} on the discordant counts) is defined for the day one can express the same claims, and none does.

\subsubsection{Evaluation design}\label{sec:evaluation}
The evaluation answers Q3 with a benchmark of known truth and answers Q1 and Q2 with observational records.

\paragraph{Benchmark with known ground truth}
The benchmark is the system's own public register, mutated. Each register row that carries a published finite object---a tensor decomposition, a point configuration, a set of circles or points, a printed constant, a polynomial map, an entropy law, a truth table---is the \emph{original}; from it the benchmark builds \emph{mutants} by transformations of three kinds, each with a label known by construction: \emph{false} (a coefficient raised by one, a digit moved, a point displaced, a bar raised one unit past the certificate's own printed end), \emph{true} (an invariance of the claim: a translation, a rotation, a relabelling of coordinates), and \emph{malformed} (a missing exponent, a weight off a probability law, a digit beyond the alphabet). Every label is established by an \emph{oracle} written for the benchmark in the Python standard library---exact fractions, directed decimal rounding, integer convolutions---that shares no code with the deciders it judges, and a false mutant counts only when the oracle exhibits the violating element (a \emph{witnessed} false claim); a mutant the oracle cannot witness is discarded and counted as such. The design, the classes, the kill switch (the campaign stops if fewer than half the rows yield a witnessed false mutant) and the outcome words were pre-registered before the first mutant ran, the note is pinned by digest in every record, and the one amendment, dated, moved no definition. This is the planted-solution design of benchmarks for other verifiers~\citep{Troxell26} applied to the system's own record. Every claim is also decided by a baseline: (B0) a floating-point checker that evaluates the predicate in double precision at a tolerance $\tau\in\{10^{-6},10^{-9},10^{-12}\}$, as producers' checkers typically do. The metrics of \cref{sec:metricsdef} are reported for cert-machine and for B0.

\paragraph{The arithmetic against an independent library}
The interval kernel's operations and functions are compared, case by case on seeded inputs, with the exact value or range computed in Arb~\citep{Johansson17}: the rule is containment, and two planted wrong kernels must be caught. This answers Q2 on sampled inputs; the lemmas of \ref{app:arith} answer it on paper.

\paragraph{Coverage across domains, and the register}
Q1 asks whether one grammar covers claims from mathematics, engineering and metocean statistics. It is answered by reporting, for each domain, how many claims could be stated in the form of \cref{def:claim} and with which verdicts they were decided, and by listing the claims that could not be so stated. The register of machine-generated claims~\citep{Register} is reported as an observational study: how many claims of each source certify, refute or refuse, and what kinds of defect the refutations expose. It measures what independence finds in practice, not a false-certification rate, since the ground truth of its claims is not known independently; its three largest exercises are the subjects of the companion papers.

\subsubsection{System overview}\label{sec:overview}

The mathematics of \cref{sec:math} is implemented in four layers (\cref{fig:system}): an arithmetic layer (exact rationals, outward-rounded intervals, function enclosures, compensated sums and derivatives; \cref{sec:intervals}); a certifier layer (the certifiers of \cref{tab:certifiers} and the decision loop); a record layer (certificates, witnesses, digests and the register's builder); and a verification layer (the batteries, red controls and second implementations of \cref{sec:protocol}). The repository holds \MtRepoInstrumentDirs{} instrument directories, about \MtRepoJsKLines{} thousand lines of JavaScript and \MtRepoPyKLines{} thousand of Python, lifted code included. A \emph{proposer}---a search, a model, a language model or a printed table---is never trusted: its output is read as a claim over a box and decided by a \emph{certifier}. A floating-point \emph{screen} may discard candidates before certification but can never admit one. The verdict, its witness and the inputs pinned by SHA-256 form a \emph{record}: an exact record re-checks without the engine, and an interval record is re-checked by re-deciding the claim from its pinned inputs (\cref{sec:grammar}).

The decision loop that \cref{def:verdicts} describes---decide the corners of $B$ as thin boxes, build the cover by bisection, return the verdict with its witness---is sketched in the supplementary material (S4). \subsubsection{Verification protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
\Cref{prop:sound} makes the verdicts as sound as the code that computes the extensions. The protocol below is how that code is guarded. Each rule is an executable gate---a test battery that exits non-zero, a page builder that will not write its page, a numbers generator that will not write a paper's macros---and \cref{tab:protocol} states which gates are enforced at every level and which are not yet.

\paragraph{R1. Screens prune, never admit.}
A floating-point screen may discard candidates; only the certifier can record a hit, and the campaign runner plants known answers that must pass every stage before a run may start~\citep[the methods note]{CMArchive}.
\paragraph{R2. Adversarial controls must fire.}
A \emph{red control} is a deliberately wrong variant of a test that must be rejected; a battery whose red controls do not all fire fails. One example: a ``gate'' evaluating the model only at the centre of a box, which the corner rule must catch. Red controls are a form of mutation testing~\citep{DeMillo78,JiaHarman11,Petrovic18}, by which soundness defects have been found in SMT solvers~\citep{Winterer20,Mansur20}; of the \MtBatteries{} batteries the test target runs (\MtBatteriesNode{} in JavaScript, \MtBatteriesPy{} in Python), \MtEvRedBatteries{} state their red controls, \MtEvRedTotal{} in all, and the others check without one.
\paragraph{R3. Pin the bytes.}
Every claim is decided against a byte sequence named by path and SHA-256 and re-hashed at decision time; certifying modules are pinned in a ledger; a page refuses to build when a pinned input changed, when the record was written by another version of its verifier, or when its battery fails.
\paragraph{R4. Independence from the claimant.}
The claimant's program is never in the trust path; the decision is re-derived from the published statement and bytes. The precise form adopted is that the system \emph{shares no code with the claimant}, and every exact certificate re-checks with no engine present; the exceptions known at the time of writing are listed in \cref{tab:protocol}.
\paragraph{R5. Second implementations.}
Where a verdict is the product, a program written from the specification in another language and arithmetic, sharing no code, re-derives it: the benchmark's oracles, the kissing samples, the entropy and truth-table certificates, the step functions, the trace-constant and Zhang--Zagier certificates, $\delta_3$, three engineering decisions (supplement, S5). Sharing no code does not make failures independent: independently written programs fail together more often than independence predicts~\citep{KnightLeveson86}, and the same has been measured for programs generated by language models~\citep{Nogueira26,Ron26}, which drafted parts of cert-machine as well as many of the claimants' artefacts; agreement is evidence against defects the two do not share, not proof of their absence.
\paragraph{R6. Records re-run.}
Published records re-run in the reader's browser by the bytes that wrote them and are compared with the record by digest or field by field. A rerun kit of \MtKitCommands{} commands re-derives the register's records; run whole on \MtRunDate, it moved none of the \MtRunRows{} rows, and the pre-registered self-audit of \cref{sec:results} ran \MtSaRuns{} runs of its \MtSaRunCommands{} commands under two profiles and found every re-derived record identical to its pin outside declared time stamps (\MtSaRunRecordsExt{} records and \MtSaRunRowsExt{} rows without a wall-clock cap; \MtSaRunRecordsPort{} records and \MtSaRunRowsPort{} rows under a \MtSaRunCap-second cap, the other \MtSaRunRefused{} refused for resources).
\paragraph{R7. Honest counting.}
Every number in a paper is a macro written from a record by a generator that refuses when the record no longer supports the sentence quoting it; queued rows are not decided rows; refusal kinds are never merged.

\paragraph{The verdict--truth matrix}
\Cref{tab:selfaudit} is the result. Of the \MtSaFalseWitnessed{} witnessed false claims, \MtSaFalseRefuted{} were refuted, \MtSaRefusalsOnFalse{} were refused (a Gaussian coefficient bound too large for exact double summation in three tensor rows; three flat-polynomial rows whose coefficients, perturbed off the integers, lie outside the decider's schema), \MtSaRepaired{} were answered \textsc{repaired}---the register's composition of a refutation of the printed bound with a certification of the certificate's own value, which is a refutation of the claim as printed and not an error---and \MtSaUnwitnessed{} was answered \textsc{unwitnessed}, a refusal under the ledger's own word. Of the \MtSaTrue{} true mutants, \MtSaTrueCertified{} were certified, and so were all \MtSaMutObjects{} originals. Of the \MtSaMalformed{} malformed objects, \MtSaMalformedRefused{} were refused and \MtSaMalformedNotRefused{} was decided: the structural zeros of \cref{eq:zeros} hold for false certifications and false refutations and fail once for a malformed object, and that one case is reported below as an open finding. The selective risk is $R=0$ on the decided claims; the coverage on witnessed false claims is \MtSaFalseRefuted{} refuted and \MtSaRepaired{} repaired of \MtSaFalseWitnessed, and the unresolved refusals are \MtSaRefusalsOnFalse{} by resources and \MtSaUnwitnessed{} by the decider's own word. With zero false certifications on \MtSaFalseWitnessed{} witnessed false claims the one-sided Clopper--Pearson bound at $\beta=0.05$ is $\theta_U=\MtSaCpFalse$, and with zero false refutations on \MtSaTrueDecided{} true claims (mutants and originals) it is \MtSaCpTrue; both describe this benchmark and its classes, not other claim sets (\cref{sec:metricsdef}).

\paragraph{Two defects found by the benchmark's rule}
The campaign's first run, at commit \MtSaFixCommit{} on \MtSaFixDate, did not show this matrix. It showed \MtSaFixOneMutants{} \MtSaFixOneOutcome{}s and \MtSaFixTwoMutants{} malformed objects decided, and both were defects of the system. The first: the ledger of point-set configurations composed its word from whether the published bytes witnessed a value, while the agreement of the printed digits with that value was a field beside the word, gated only by the page build; a printed value moved one unit in its last digit past the bytes therefore stayed \CERT{} on every row with a printed value. The fix is one module that composes the word in one place---a witnessed object whose printed value the exact value does not reproduce under the row's convention is \textsc{misprinted}, the register's \REFT{} with the kind \emph{not from the published data}---and the whole ledger was re-derived under it with no row changing word. The second: the tensor and kissing deciders answered a malformed object (a tensor with the wrong number of slices, a configuration that is not $n$ vectors in $\R^d$, a weight row without its twenty weights) with the verdict word \REFT, the identity having failed; the fix makes every shape check answer \REFS{} with its reason, and the one identity that fails under both tensor layouts stays \REFT. Both fixes are pinned in the campaign's record with the commit each was found at and every mutant it covers, the campaign was re-run whole after them, and \cref{tab:selfaudit} is that re-run. The pre-registration's rule that a defect found by the campaign is fixed on the record and the campaign re-run, never the definition moved, was followed; the one amendment to the note is dated and lists what it does not change.

\paragraph{The conventional check, on the same claims}
The baseline B0 evaluates each predicate in double precision at a tolerance. At $\tau=10^{-6}$ it accepts \MtSaFloatFalseSix{} of the witnessed false claims (\MtSaFloatFalseNSix{} of \MtSaFalseWitnessed), at $10^{-9}$ \MtSaFloatFalseNine{} (\MtSaFloatFalseNNine) and at $10^{-12}$ \MtSaFloatFalseTwelve{} (\MtSaFloatFalseNTwelve). The tolerance cuts both ways: at the same three settings it confirms only \MtSaFloatOrigSix, \MtSaFloatOrigNine{} and \MtSaFloatOrigTwelve{} of the \MtSaMutObjects{} true originals, and \MtSaFloatTrueSix, \MtSaFloatTrueNine{} and \MtSaFloatTrueTwelve{} of the \MtSaTrue{} true mutants, because four of the nine polynomial maps are decided by no tolerance at all (their determinants are exact integers that double precision does not hold). On the risk--coverage plane of \cref{sec:metricsdef} the checker has $C=1$ and $R$ between $\MtSaFloatFalseTwelve$ and $\MtSaFloatFalseSix$ of the false claims at the tolerances producers use; the decider has $R=0$ and $C$ as above.

\paragraph{Concordance, and the hosted rows}
Where a register row is decided by two or more programs---the deciders under test, the benchmark's oracles, the rerun kit's standard-library verifiers, the second implementations of rule R5---the concordance record compares their answers field by field: \MtSaConcord{} comparisons on \MtSaConcordRows{} rows, \MtSaConcordField{} at the level of the enclosure or the violating count and \MtSaConcordWord{} at the level of the word, with no disagreement. The \MtSaHostedChallenges{} kernel replays of the mathematics release ran on hosted runners and are carried in as recorded, never merged into the laptop's table: \MtSaHostedCertified{} \CERT{} under two kernels, \MtSaHostedRefused{} \REFS{} for resources, wall-clock from \MtSaHostedSecondsMin{} to \MtSaHostedSecondsMax{} seconds (median \MtSaHostedSecondsMedian) and peak memory up to \MtSaHostedRssGiB~GiB.

\paragraph{Cost}
The mutation campaign took \MtSaMinutes{} minutes on the laptop (\MtSaRunMachine); the re-derivation of the register's records under the extended profile \MtSaRunWallMinutesExt{} minutes of wall-clock and under the portable profile \MtSaRunWallMinutesPort, with a peak resident memory of \MtSaRunPeakGiB~GiB, every run cold and warm with its wall, CPU and memory in the record. Nothing was rented.

\paragraph{What the benchmark does not cover}
Every claim in it is universal or exact, so the epistemic reading, the split refusal and the undetermined label are not measured; the engineering gate's planted faults (\GFaults{} faulty proposals, \GFaultsRefuted{} refuted and \GFaultsRefused{} refused~\citep{P2}) are the only ground truth of that kind and are targeted, not sampled. The mutation classes were chosen by the author, so the benchmark bounds the defects it can find by the classes it plants; a defect outside them is invisible to it. No second decider that can express these claims exists, so the paired comparison of \cref{sec:metricsdef} is not reported.

\subsection{The kernel against an independent library}\label{sec:arb}

The interval kernel was run on \MtArbCases{} seeded cases across \MtArbOps{} operations---up to \MtArbPerOp{} each, drawn from full-precision doubles, subnormals, arguments near overflow and underflow, and boxes that straddle a function's critical points---and every enclosure it returned was compared with the exact result, or for a box the exact range, computed in Arb~\citep{Johansson17} at \MtArbPrec{} bits and more (\cref{tab:arb}). Every one of the \MtArbContained{} enclosures it returned contained the exact value, and it refused the other \MtArbInDomainRefused; none of the \MtArbMalformed{} malformed inputs (a NaN, an inverted interval, division through zero, a non-integer or overflowing power, a logarithm at or below zero) was decided; and the comparison caught two planted wrong kernels, a round-to-nearest addition in \MtArbRedAdd{} of \MtArbRedAddOf{} cases and the platform's exponential at the ends in \MtArbRedExp{} of \MtArbRedExpOf. \paragraph{What the method refuses that a stronger method could decide}\label{sec:eval}
Incompleteness has a size, and it is reported. Of the \MtEvChallenges{} Comparator challenges of the mathematics release, \MtEvResources{} are refused for a stack, memory or time limit that more resources might clear. \subsection{The register, and its three largest exercises}\label{sec:register}

The public register holds \RegRows{} claims from \RegClaimants{} claimants, decided from \RegSources{} records between \RegFirstDate{} and \RegLastDate{} and frozen on \RegDate; decisions made after the freeze (the registry replays, the kissing configurations of the second exercise) are not register rows. Of its rows \RegDecided{} are decided and \VQueued{} is queued, not counted: \VCertified{} \CERT, \VPartial{} \verdict{partial}, \VRefuted{} \REFT, \VMixed{} \verdict{mixed}, \VRepaired{} \verdict{repaired} and \VNeedsData{} \verdict{needs data}. \RegDefects{} rows carry a kind of defect other than none; the most frequent is \emph{\KTopDefect} (\KTopDefectRows{} rows), a formal statement or a check that covers less than the claim beside it. Of the \AiRows{} rows whose claimant is a machine or a machine-assisted pipeline, \AiRowsDefects{} carry a defect; of the other \OtherRows, \OtherRowsDefects. The register measures what independence finds, not an error rate: no row's truth is known independently of the decision. Three exercises dominate it and are the subjects of the companion papers.

\paragraph{A configuration of points}
An autonomous discovery system published new lower bounds for the kissing number in \MtKwRows{} dimensions (\MtKwDims), each a configuration of integer or algebraic vectors, their sizes proved by moment identities and the angular condition by structure, with the release's own files recording that the pairwise inner products were not checked~\citep{Yang26}. Two configurations were published as exact objects and decided at once; the other seventeen were \verdict{needs data} with the missing files named, and the claimant published them within days~\citep{QiushiRelease}. Every pair was then decided exactly: $\MtKwPairsSci$ pairs, $\MtKwContactsSci$ at exactly sixty degrees and none closer, in three arithmetics each exact on its data---single-precision BLAS on the laptop, exact algebraic integers for the two closed-form constructions, and half-precision tensor cores on one rented GPU for the seven largest (\MtKwGpuHours{} GPU-hours, \$\MtKwGpuCost{} billed), the floating-point paths made exact by a Cauchy--Schwarz bound asserted on the data before any path runs. All \MtKwRows{} bounds hold; the claimant linked the check from its release. The companion paper gives the materials, the calibration, the second implementation and the limit of the GPU argument~\citep[the kissing companion]{CMArchive}.

\paragraph{A curated record}
An optimization-constants registry marks with an asterisk a bound whose verification is minimal and states what removes the mark~\citep{OptConst,OptConstRule}. Three asterisked bounds rest on finite certificates---two entropy laws for the sums--differences constants and a truth table for the Fourier entropy--influence constant---and were decided twice from the registry's bytes by programs that share no code with each other or with the claimants; all three are \CERT{} with their ratios enclosed. The registry merged the replay and removed the marks, and its pages cite the replay~\citep[the registry companion]{CMArchive}. The registry's open queue offered \MtRqRows{} further bounds: \MtRqComputedCertified{} are \CERT{} by a computation made here, one on an earlier two-kernel record, one was checked by reading alone and is counted as a reading, and \MtRqRefused{} is \REFS---a package that certifies its own assembly and not the analysis it assembles, with the three inputs that would decide it named. One bound was printed on the wrong side of its own certificate, $C_{1d}\le\MtAcDPrinted$ against the certificate's exact $\MtAcDExact\ldots$; the record holds the printed bound \REFT{} and the repaired one \CERT. The certificate's own checker had printed OK on all \MtEvCheckerRuns{} of the claims it was given, of which \MtEvCheckerFalse{} are refuted here.

\paragraph{A formal release}
A mathematics release of \MtOaManuscripts{} machine-written manuscripts came with \MtOaChallenges{} Lean challenge statements as its formal spine, and with a Navier--Stokes repository proved against a statement written months earlier~\citep{OpenAIMath,OpenAINS}. Every challenge was replayed under Lean's kernel and under a second kernel written independently of it: \MtOaCertified{} accepted by both, \MtOaRefused{} \REFS{} for resources, none rejected. The Navier--Stokes development built clean at its pinned commit with the standard axioms, and its formal statement, read hypothesis by hypothesis against the paper's theorem, lacks the energy clause of that theorem; the record is \verdict{partial} at \NsCommit{} with the clause named in its scope, and the next upstream commit, \NsHeadCommit{} of \NsHeadDate, proves it. Of the \MtOaStatements{} manuscript families whose statements were read against their theorems, \MtOaMatches{} match and \MtOaShort{} state less than, or other than, what they claim; the readings were made by AI agents and are not verdicts; their recall was measured once, on \MtRrPlants{} defects planted in short statements, \MtRrFlagged{} flagged. The companion paper gives the kernel lane, the readings and the \MtOaGaps{} gaps the audit pinned~\citep[the release companion]{CMArchive}; the Navier--Stokes reading has its own note.

\subsection{What the results show}

\paragraph{Soundness, measured}
\Cref{prop:sound} predicts zero false verdicts, and on \MtSaMutants{} claims of known truth the implementation produced zero, after two defects that the benchmark's own rule exposed were fixed on the record. Neither defect was in the arithmetic. One was in a word: a ledger that said \CERT{} about the bytes while the printed digits beside them disagreed. The other was a verdict word given to an object that was not a claim at all. Both are the verifier's kind of defect---a gap between what was checked and what the word said---and both were found by planting claims against the system's own register, which is what the protocol asks of claimants. The lemmas of \ref{app:arith}, written for this version, found three more of the same kind in the arithmetic (\cref{sec:limits}): a thin box of a double standing for $\pi$, a tail bound short by its own prefactor, a margin computed in the platform's floating point; each lay below the enclosures' widths and each was unproved until written down.

\paragraph{The defects in the record are the verifier's kind}
No bound of the three exercises was found false and no theorem was false. One registry bound is printed on the wrong side of its own certificate; a pairwise condition was proved by structure and never checked pair by pair; a certificate's checker compares in double precision what it encloses at a hundred digits; a package certifies its own assembly and not the analysis it assembles; a clause of a theorem is absent from the formal statement, and formal statements are narrower than the theorems beside them. Each was closed, or named, by a finite computation or a recorded reading whose only hard part was deciding to make it.

\paragraph{Refusal is a result, and it has a size}
Seventeen kissing rows were \verdict{needs data} from the request until the points were released, each naming the files that would decide it; the request was answered and the rows closed, an opacity measured and then removed. The gate's split refusal carries the pump pressure at which it would become a proof. The package is \REFS{} with its three missing inputs named. The Navier--Stokes reading issues no verdict and defers to the kernel, so the missing clause became a scope sentence rather than an accusation. Every refusal says what would convert it, and the kinds are never summed. The cost of exactness, by contrast, is small: the objects are integers, rationals and bounds, and the one expensive decision---$10^{15}$ pairs---spent its budget on the size of the set, not on rigour.

\paragraph{A decision changes the record}
Each exercise ends outside the system. The kissing claimant published its points in the form the check needed and linked the decision from its release. The registry's maintainer, who had written down beforehand what removes the mark of an unverified bound, removed it on the replays and cites them. The release's next commit, made the day after the audit pinned the repository, proves the clause the reading found missing. We draw no causal conclusion from these cases. They show what the grammar makes possible: a \CERT{} with its certificate can be cited, a \REFS{} that names its files can be answered, and a scope sentence can be closed.

\subsection{Relation to existing practice}\label{sec:related}

Validated numerics and computer-assisted proof are the certifiers' ancestry (\cref{sec:foundations}). Formal proof checks a proof against a stated theorem~\citep{Lean4,Mathlib20,Avigad24}, independent type checkers replay the kernel's work~\citep{Nanoda,Carneiro24}, and a translation that compiles may still not be the theorem intended~\citep{Bastounis26,Kallel26}; autoformalization is now a research area of its own~\citep{Wu22,Tao25}. Certifying algorithms return a certificate that a small independent checker verifies~\citep{McConnell11}, as SAT solvers do with proofs that verified checkers replay~\citep{Wetzler14,CakeLPR21}, and proof-carrying code ships a proof of a safety policy with the program~\citep{Necula97}. Decision procedures already treat a failure to decide as an answer~\citep{SMTLIB,Why3,dReal13,MetiTarski10}, and reproducibility initiatives re-run published computations~\citep{Rougier17,ReproBuilds22}. Benchmarks for machine mathematics grade answers against keys~\citep{Glazer24}; the system's companion on grading measures what tolerance rules accept~\citep[the certified-evaluation companion]{CMArchive}. Each of these enforces one of the properties the system combines---a recorded refusal, a certificate that a small checker verifies, a reproducible build, independence from the producer---and none is organised around other parties' published claims; the combination, enforced by code that refuses, is what is claimed.



\subsection{Threats to validity}\label{sec:threats}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Selection.} Every claim was chosen by the author: the register records every row as self-initiated, and the exercises were picked because they had finite objects to decide. The results describe these claims, not machine-generated mathematics at large.
\item \emph{The benchmark's classes.} The mutation classes were designed by the author, so the benchmark can find only the defects its classes reach; the Clopper--Pearson bounds describe this benchmark and do not transfer. Every claim in it is universal or exact, so the epistemic grammar is measured only by the gate's targeted faults.
\item \emph{The author in the outcomes.} The outcomes of \cref{sec:register}---the points published, the marks removed---followed requests and pull requests the author made. They are reported as what happened, and no causal claim rests on them.
\item \emph{Correlated implementations.} The deciders, their second implementations and the benchmark's oracles were written by AI agents under the author's direction, from shared briefs. N-version programs written that way can fail together~\citep{KnightLeveson86,Nogueira26,Ron26}, and level 2 of \cref{tab:evidence} is counted apart for that reason.
\item \emph{The arithmetic's evidence.} The comparison with Arb covers sampled inputs, not every input; the lemmas cover the functions, not the code that implements them; no IEEE 1788 conformance suite~\citep{Benet24} has been run on the interval layer.
\item \emph{The readings.} The statement readings and the Navier--Stokes reading were made by AI agents and checked only by second readings made the same way; their recall was measured once, on short statements under an alert brief, and not on the long ones. No human specialist has confirmed them.
\item \emph{The GPU argument.} The exactness of the seven largest kissing decisions rests on the documented single-precision accumulation of tensor cores~\citep{Fasi21,Blanchard20,HighamMary22}, characterised for earlier devices than the one rented, on the bound asserted before the run, and on a calibration that reproduced the laptop's exact contact counts on two configurations; the calibration is a check, not a proof.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What is not claimed, and the limits}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Not claimed} Interval arithmetic, the Krawczyk operator, floating-point filters, sums of squares and Sturm sequences are not claimed; they are cited. The validity of a declared model is not claimed: a \textsc{proved} engineering number holds for the declared model over the declared envelope, not for the sea or the reservoir. A kissing bound ``decided here'' is a statement about one published configuration, not about the record in that dimension. Nothing is certified in the regulatory sense; the word is used in the sense of \cref{def:verdicts} only. Nothing is a probability.

\paragraph{Locality and incompleteness} A certified maximum is the maximum in its box; a global statement over a family would need a branch-and-bound over the family's whole parameter space, which the system does not run. The system refuses what it cannot decide, and the refusal rate is a property of each campaign, published with it (\cref{sec:eval}).

\paragraph{Readings are not decisions} Three results of the register rest in part on readings rather than computations: the clause found absent from the Navier--Stokes formal conclusions, the statements of the mathematics release found short of their claims, and the inventory of the record package. Each reading was produced by AI agents from a written brief, and no human specialist has confirmed them. Until one does, the gaps are reported as what the readings found, with their recall as measured.

\paragraph{Soundness rests on code} \Cref{prop:sound} is a theorem about interval extensions; whether a program computes one is a fact about the program. The defences---second implementations, red controls, pinned bytes, calibrations, the planted-claim benchmark, the comparison with Arb, outside reruns---reduce that risk and do not remove it. What a read of the code found on 2026-10-09 was closed the same day with the correction published beside the old numbers (the generation loop's closed-form hunt had counted \MtErrFloatMillions{} million float comparisons as refutations; every refutation is now a proved disjointness). What the benchmark found on \MtSaFixDate{} is in \cref{sec:selfaudit}. What writing the lemmas of \ref{app:arith} found the same day was closed before this version was built, and every record that pins the changed modules was re-derived: the square root of $\pi$ in the error function was taken from a thin box of the double nearest $\pi$, which does not contain $\pi$; the error function's tail was padded by the bare next term, short of the alternating-series bound by its own factor $2/\sqrt\pi$; the gamma function's error margin was a platform float; the four transcendental tails were evaluated in round-to-nearest and covered only by the margin below the sum's ulp; the lower end of $\ln\Gamma$ across its minimum was a hard-coded double; the flip thresholds were rounded to nearest before the decimal rounding; the gate's witnesses were the widened doubles, one ulp outside the declared box; and the kissing runner recorded the exactness bound it did not assert. Each lay below the widths in play, and none moved a published verdict; each was a place where the code said less than the paper. What remains open, by the system's own rule that a check not made is not a check:
\begin{itemize}
\item the older helper that encloses the platform's cosine by a derived pad, contained in every case of \cref{tab:arb} but wide, is still called from six files, among them the radii-polynomial certifier, whose records are re-run when it is retired;
\item the partial path of one polynomial-map decider reads a term with a missing exponent silently and answers \verdict{partial} where its full path refuses (the one malformed object decided in \cref{tab:selfaudit});
\item one quadrature routine, rigorous on dyadic cells of $[0,1]$, is called on another interval by one instrument; one battery labels some positive assertions as red controls; in the engineering demonstrator a \textsc{proved} consistency check means that the claim meets an outer enclosure;
\item two of the system's own instruments reuse its code across a producer--checker line (\cref{tab:protocol}), and the same-author second implementations are not independent re-derivations (\cref{tab:evidence});
\item only the exact-class certificates are in a form a program other than this system's can check; an interval certificate records the hull and the counts of its cover, not the cover.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions and recommendations}\label{sec:conclusions}
%% =====================================================================

cert-machine decides the numbers and claims that machines propose with three verdicts, each carrying the object that justifies it; the interval and exact verdicts are sound under the inclusion property, which the arithmetic is built to secure and whose function enclosures now carry a stated lemma each; its certifiers reduce the questions that arrive---a zero, a maximum, a statement for every input, a sign, a nonnegative polynomial, a minimum, a formal proof---to finitely many exact or outward-rounded decisions; its protocol keeps them honest by code that refuses; and its record is public and re-runs. Measured against \MtSaMutants{} claims of known truth, it produced no false verdict and refused \MtSaRefusalsOnFalse; measured against an independent library on \MtArbCases{} cases, every enclosure held; pointed at \RegRows{} machine-generated claims, it found the verifier's kind of defect, and the claimants, the registry and the release moved on what it found. Two of its own defects were found by the same rule and are on the record. The three questions are answered as far as the record allows: one grammar covers the three domains (Q1); the hypothesis holds on the inputs tested and is proved on paper for the functions used (Q2); and on claims whose truth is known the system certifies what is true, refutes what is false and refuses a stated fraction by resources, at a cost of minutes on a laptop (Q3).

\paragraph{Recommendations} To producers of machine-generated mathematics: publish the object, not only its description---the points of a configuration in one integer form, the witness a theorem rests on, the analytic inputs a certificate freezes, the data a fit was made from---with a digest beside each file; state the scope of every check in the release itself; and when a formal statement is narrower than the theorem it accompanies, say so where the theorem is stated. To those who maintain the tables and records of a field: distinguish a bound that has been decided from outside from one that has only been claimed, and write down what removes the distinction. To those who build checkers: plant claims of known truth against your own record, with an oracle that shares no code with the decider, before anyone else does.

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{prop:outward}]
For these operations the extremes of $a\circ b$ over $A\times B$ are attained at endpoint combinations (monotonicity in each argument on each sign region, with $0\notin B$ for division). The exact extreme lies within half the spacing of the doubles on its side of the rounded value, and the adjacent double on that side is a whole spacing away---also when the rounded value is a power of two, where the spacing below is half the spacing above, and in the subnormal range---so it lies on the correct side of $\mathrm{down}(y_-)$ and $\mathrm{up}(y_+)$.
\end{proof}

\Cref{tab:functions} lists every elementary and special function the certifiers call, with the lemma that establishes \cref{ass:functions} for it; the lemmas are stated here and proved in the supplementary material (S1). Throughout, a series is evaluated by the natural extension on the enclosed argument, the tail bound is evaluated in interval arithmetic and its upper end $\overline{R}$ taken, and $[-\overline R,\overline R]$ (or $[0,\overline R]$) is added by an outward-rounded addition, so the bound is never absorbed into the widening of \cref{prop:outward}. The enclosures of $\pi$ and $\ln 2$ are exact rationals (Machin's formula; $\operatorname{atanh}(1/3)$) converted outward, and every constant below ($\sqrt\pi$, $\sqrt{2\pi}$, $\pi/2$) is derived from them.

\begin{lemma}[exp]\label{lem:exp}
Let $x$ be a double, $k$ an integer, and $R\ni x-k\ln 2$ the interval computed from $x$, $k$ and the enclosure of $\ln2$ by \cref{prop:outward}, with $\rho=\max_{r\in R}|r|<1$ and $-1074\le k\le1023$ (the call is refused otherwise). Then for every $r\in R$, $e^{r}\in\sum_{i=0}^{24}r^{i}/i!+[-T,T]$ with $T=\rho^{25}(25!)^{-1}(1-\rho/26)^{-1}$, and $e^{x}=2^{k}e^{x-k\ln2}$ exactly, $2^k$ being a double.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[log]\label{lem:log}
Let $x>0$ be a normal double, $x=\mathrm{m}\,2^{e}$ with $\mathrm{m}\in[1,2)$ (exact), and $V\ni v=(\mathrm{m}-1)/(\mathrm{m}+1)$ the interval computed outward, with $\nu=\max V<0.34$ (refused otherwise). Then $\ln x=e\ln2+2\operatorname{atanh}v$ and $\operatorname{atanh}v\in\sum_{k=0}^{39}v^{2k+1}/(2k+1)+[0,T]$ with $T=\nu^{81}/(81(1-\nu^{2}))$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[sin, cos]\label{lem:trig}
Let $x$ be a double, $H$ the enclosure of $\pi/2$, $n$ an integer and $R\ni x-n\pi/2$ the interval computed outward from $x$, $n$ and $H$, with $\rho=\max_{r\in R}|r|\le2$. Then $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are $\pm\sin r$ or $\pm\cos r$ according to $n\bmod4$, and for $|r|\le2$, $\sin r\in\sum_{j=0}^{15}(-1)^{j}r^{2j+1}/(2j+1)!+[-\rho^{33}/33!,\rho^{33}/33!]$ and $\cos r\in\sum_{j=0}^{15}(-1)^{j}r^{2j}/(2j)!+[-\rho^{34}/34!,\rho^{34}/34!]$. For a box $X$ of width below $2\pi$, $\sin(X)$ lies in the hull of the images of its ends, extended to $1$ (to $-1$) whenever some $\pi/2+2k\pi$ (some $3\pi/2+2k\pi$) may lie in $X$, the test made on the enclosures of these points and answering ``may'' whenever it cannot separate; a box of width at least $2\pi$ is enclosed by $[-1,1]$; likewise for $\cos$ with the critical points $2k\pi$ and $\pi+2k\pi$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[$\Gamma$ by Spouge's formula]\label{lem:gamma}
Let $a=20$ and $w\ge0$ real. With $c_0=\sqrt{2\pi}$ and $c_k=(-1)^{k-1}(a-k)^{k-1/2}e^{a-k}/(k-1)!$ for $1\le k\le a-1$,
$\Gamma(w+1)=(w+a)^{w+1/2}e^{-(w+a)}\bigl[c_0+\sum_{k=1}^{a-1}c_k/(w+k)+\varepsilon_a(w)\bigr]$ with $|\varepsilon_a(w)|\le a^{-1/2}(2\pi)^{-(a+1/2)}$~\citep{Spouge94}. The implementation encloses each $c_k$, the sum and the prefactor by the natural extension and widens the bracket by the upper end of $10\,a^{-1/2}(2\pi)^{-(a+1/2)}$ computed in interval arithmetic, a tenfold margin; for $0.4<z<1$ it uses $\Gamma(z)=\Gamma(z+1)/z$, and for a box of width above $10^{-3}$ the log-convexity of $\Gamma$ with its minimum in $[1.4616,1.4617]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[$\ln\Gamma$, $\psi$, $\psi'$]\label{lem:lgamma}
For real $z\ge20$ and $m=8$, with $B_{2k}$ the Bernoulli numbers,
\begin{align*}
\ln\Gamma(z)&=(z-\tfrac12)\ln z-z+\tfrac12\ln2\pi+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{B_{2k}}{2k(2k-1)z^{2k-1}}+R_0,\\
\psi(z)&=\ln z-\frac1{2z}-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{B_{2k}}{2k\,z^{2k}}+R_1,\qquad
\psi'(z)=\frac1z+\frac1{2z^2}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{B_{2k}}{z^{2k+1}}+R_2,
\end{align*}
with $|R_0|\le|B_{2m+2}|/((2m+2)(2m+1)z^{2m+1})$, $|R_1|\le|B_{2m+2}|/((2m+2)z^{2m+2})$ and $|R_2|\le|B_{2m+2}|/z^{2m+3}$. An argument below 20 is lifted by the recurrences in interval arithmetic. Over a box, $\psi$ is increasing and $\psi'$ decreasing; $\ln\Gamma$ is convex with its minimum at $x^*=1.4616\ldots$, and across $x^*$ the lower end is $\ln\Gamma(p)-|\psi(p)|\max(p-\underline x,\overline x-p)$ for any $p>0$, from the enclosures at $p$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[the regularized incomplete gamma]\label{lem:gammaP}
For $a>0$ and $z>0$, $P(a,z)=z^{a}e^{-z}\Gamma(a+1)^{-1}\sum_{n\ge0}t_n$ with $t_0=1$ and $t_{n+1}=t_n\,z/(a+n+1)$; if $r_N=z/(a+N+1)<1$ then $0\le\sum_{n>N}t_n\le t_N\,r_N/(1-r_N)$. Over a box $P$ increases in $z$ and decreases in $a$; $P^{-1}(a,p)$ increases in both.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Bessel $J_\nu$]\label{lem:bessel}
For $\nu>-0.6$ and $x>0$, $J_\nu(x)=\sum_{m\ge0}t_m$ with $t_0=(x/2)^{\nu}/\Gamma(\nu+1)$ and $t_{m+1}=-t_m(x/2)^2/((m+1)(\nu+m+1))$; if $q=(x/2)^2/((M+2)(\nu+M+2))<1$ then $|\sum_{m>M}t_m|\le|t_{M+1}|/(1-q)$. For $J_\nu'$, whose terms carry the factor $(2m+\nu)/2$, the ratios beyond $M=40$ are at most $1.026q$, and for $q\le0.9$ the tail is at most $2|t'_{M+1}|/(1-q)$. A box $[0,x]$ with $x\le1$ is enclosed by $[\max(0,1-x^2/4),1]$ for $\nu=0$ and by $[0,(x/2)^{\nu}/\Gamma(\nu+1)]$ for $\nu>0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[erf, erfc]\label{lem:erf}
For $|z|\le2$ and $N=40$,
\[
\operatorname{erf}z=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\Bigl[\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^k z^{2k+1}}{k!\,(2k+1)}+T\Bigr],\qquad |T|\le\frac{|z|^{2N+3}}{(N+1)!\,(2N+3)},
\]
because the terms decrease in magnitude from $k=2$ on. For $x\ge1$, $\sqrt\pi\,e^{x^2}\operatorname{erfc}x=1/(x+\tfrac12/(x+1/(x+\tfrac32/(x+\cdots))))$, Laplace's continued fraction~\citep[7.9.2]{DLMF}, whose consecutive convergents bracket its value~\citep{Cuyt08}; the implementation takes the hull of the 80th and 81st.
\end{lemma}

\section{The certifiers' results}\label{app:cert}

\begin{theorem}[Krawczyk~\citep{Krawczyk69,Neumaier90,Rump10}]\label{thm:krawczyk}
Let $f:D\subseteq\R^n\to\R^n$ be continuously differentiable, $X\subseteq D$ a box, $\hat x\in X$, $Y$ a nonsingular matrix and $F'(X)$ an interval extension of the Jacobian over $X$. If $K(X)=\hat x-Yf(\hat x)+(I-YF'(X))(X-\hat x)\subseteq\operatorname{int}X$, then $f$ has exactly one zero in $X$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:max}
Let $\ell$ be twice continuously differentiable on a convex box $X$, let $\nabla\ell$ have exactly one zero $\theta^*$ in $X$, and let the Hessian of $\ell$ be negative definite at every point of $X$. Then $\theta^*$ is the unique maximiser of $\ell$ on $X$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:limit}
Let $\ell(\mu,\sigma,Q)$ be continuous on $B\times[0,q_1]$ and differentiable in $Q$ on $B\times(0,q_1]$, with $\partial\ell/\partial Q<0$ there. Then $\ell(\mu,\sigma,Q)<\ell(\mu,\sigma,0)$ for every $(\mu,\sigma)\in B$ and $Q\in(0,q_1]$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:multilinear}
Let $f$ be multilinear on a box $X\subset\R^n$. Then $f(X)=[\min_v f(v),\max_v f(v)]$, the minimum and maximum taken over the $2^n$ vertices $v$ of $X$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:nested}
Let the hypothesis of \cref{prop:sound} hold and let the boxes be nested, $B(p')\subseteq B(p)$ for $p\le p'$. If the claim is certified over $B(p_0)$, then $\Tp(p)$ holds for every $p\ge p_0$ and $\inf\{p:\Tp(p)\}\le p_0$; the same holds for an epistemic refutation and $\Fp$.
\end{proposition}

How each is implemented---the Krawczyk rounds and radius cap, Sylvester's criterion and the nonsingularity proof, the series in the shape parameter at the lognormal limit, the monotone implicit functions, the filtered orientation predicate with its error bound~\citep[Thm.~2.2 and \S3.1]{Higham02}, the exact sum-of-squares identity, the Chebyshev--Sturm minimum, the two claims of a formal proof, the threshold procedures and the decision loop---is in the supplementary material (S2--S4), with the proofs of the propositions above and the reproduction table (S5).
