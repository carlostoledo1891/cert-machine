\begin{abstract}
A metocean design basis rests on two computations that are usually left to an optimiser or to a floating-point test: the marginal fit a return level is read from, and the environmental contour that sea states are counted against. This paper decides both. For the marginals it repeats the extreme-value method of \citet{reis2026} --- six families fitted by maximum likelihood to the unfiltered series and to daily, weekly and monthly maxima, ranked by four goodness-of-fit criteria, with 100- and 1000-year levels --- on the method's own public hindcast. A fit is accepted only when the Krawczyk operator proves that a box around it contains exactly one zero of the score and the Hessian is proved negative definite over the box, in outward-rounded interval arithmetic over every datum; criteria and levels are enclosures over the box, and a ranking is \vDecided\ only where the enclosures separate. Over \ACells\ open-sea cells, \ACertified\ of \AFits\ fits are certified maxima and \AGGLimit\ generalized-gamma fits are refused with the proof that the likelihood is highest at the lognormal limit; a threshold in place of that proof changes the selected family at \ANaiveDiff\ of \ABlocks\ cell-blocks. A scientific library's default call is the maximum it reports on none of \SFits\ fits, and \TOff\ of twenty rows of a published design table are off the maximum of the nearest public cell. For the contours it re-decides the environmental-contour benchmark whose three buoys are certified above: each of \EContours\ submitted contours is read as the exact polygon its digits denote and \EHoursTotal\ hourly sea states are classified inside, outside or on it with every sign decided. \EExact\ of \EComparisons\ printed counts are reproduced to the integer, two are traced to files rewritten after the count, \ENotSimple\ polygons cross themselves, and the expected numbers are certified enclosures. The novelty claimed is not interval arithmetic but the decision grammar --- a verdict with a witness, refusal as a result --- and a record any reader can re-run.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A metocean design basis states a significant wave height with a return period, and that number comes out of a chain: a record is blocked, a distribution is fitted to the blocks, one distribution is chosen by a goodness-of-fit statistic, and its tail is inverted at the return period \citep{coles2001,dnv2021,iso19901}. Each link is a computation, and each is usually carried out by a general-purpose optimiser whose stopping point is then called ``the fit''. \citet{reis2026} applied this chain over a global wave hindcast: six parametric families --- normal, lognormal, Weibull, exponentiated Weibull, generalized gamma and Gumbel --- fitted by maximum likelihood to the unfiltered 3-hourly series and to daily, weekly and monthly block maxima, ranked by the Kolmogorov--Smirnov, Anderson--Darling, chi-square and mean-squared-error criteria, with the Anderson--Darling statistic chosen as the selector and 100- and 1000-year levels read off the selected family. Their conclusion, that the choice of criterion and of block size both move the selected distribution and the long-period return level, is a statement about the arithmetic of the method as much as about the sea. Beside the marginal stands the joint description of the sea states: an environmental contour, a closed curve in the plane of two metocean variables along which a structure is analysed for a given return period \citep{winterstein1993,haselsteiner2017,mackay2021}. A contour is compared with data by counting the observations that fall outside it, and the benchmark that compared contour methods on common data \citep{haselsteiner2021} --- on three of the same buoys this paper certifies marginals for --- made that count with a floating-point point-in-polygon test.

This paper asks a narrower question of both computations: at each step, is the number what the method says it is? A maximum-likelihood estimate is a zero of the score equations at which the Hessian is negative definite; a goodness-of-fit statistic is a sum over the data; a ranking is a comparison of such sums; a return level is a quantile of the chosen law; and the number of observations outside a contour is an integer. Each is decidable in finite arithmetic with rigorous error bounds, and the tools for doing so --- interval arithmetic with outward rounding \citep{moore2009}, the Krawczyk operator for the existence and uniqueness of a zero \citep{krawczyk1969,rump2010}, and validated global optimisation \citep{hansen2004,tucker2011} --- are decades old. So is exact geometric computation behind a floating-point filter, which decides every sign of a geometric predicate \citep{fortune1996,shewchuk1997,yap1997}. What has not been done, to our knowledge, is to apply them to the whole chain of an extreme-value study and to record the result so that a reader can re-run it. The present work does that for the method of \citet{reis2026} on their own data and for the contour benchmark on its own files, and adds a grammar for the outcome: every fit, every ranking, every count and every printed number receives one of a small set of verdicts, each with the quantity that justifies it, and refusal is a result that is counted, not an error that is dropped. The grammar, the arithmetic and the verification discipline behind every verdict are those of the cert-machine method \citep{certmethod}; this paper describes only what is particular to extreme-value analysis.

Three motivations come from practice. First, the likelihood of several families used for $\Hs$ has no interior maximum on many series: the generalized gamma's supremum is at its lognormal limit \citep{prentice1974,stacy1962}, and the exponentiated Weibull's runs to a Gumbel-type regime or to a Fr\'echet corner; an optimiser then prints the last iterate as the fit, and nothing in its output says so. Second, the same library called two ways returns two different 100-year levels for the same data, because a location parameter makes the likelihood unbounded \citep{hill1963,harper2011}; which of the two is a maximum is a question of fact, and this paper decides it. Third, design tables are printed from hindcasts that cannot be re-run outside the organisation that produced them \citep{bhaskaran2023}; a certificate at the nearest public cell measures the distance between the two records and says which of the printed digits are a maximum-likelihood fit of anything. Fourth, a floating-point point-in-polygon test has no answer for a point on the boundary, answers wrongly near degeneracy without saying so \citep{kettner2008}, and presumes that the file it is given denotes a polygon; whether a benchmark's printed counts are the counts of the files on record is therefore a question of fact, and this paper decides it.

The contribution is therefore not a new distribution, a new contour construction or a new estimate of the 100-year wave. It is (i) a certificate per fit, (ii) a decision rule for rankings in which refusal is explicit, (iii) proofs, rather than thresholds, for the two families whose supremum is at a limit, (iv) a verdict vocabulary for numbers printed elsewhere, (v) an exact count per contour, with every submitted file read as the polygon it denotes and \textsc{on} as a third answer, and (vi) the public, re-runnable record of all of this, with the method paper's own regional claims and the benchmark's printed tables decided against it. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data; Sections~\ref{sec:method} and \ref{sec:contourmethod} the certificate of a fit and the decision of a contour count; Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what a certificate does and does not say, and what a standard for marginal fits and contours could take from it.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The hindcast}
The reference method was applied by \citet{reis2026} to the Ifremer WAVEWATCH III hindcast GLOBMULTI\_ERA5\_GLOBCUR\_01 \citep{accensi,alday2021}: a global 0.5$^\circ$ grid at 3-hourly resolution, 1993 to 2024, forced by ERA5 winds and the global currents of the same system, distributed under CC BY-SA 4.0. The whole significant-wave-height field was read from the monthly files by byte range, each chunk hashed as read, and two cuts were kept. The first is a lattice of \ANodes\ nodes --- a 4$^\circ$ global lattice, a 1$^\circ$ lattice of the Brazilian margin from 6$^\circ$N to 36$^\circ$S, and thirteen named nodes --- at each of which the largest 3-hourly value of every UTC day was kept, \ADays\ days from \AFirst\ to \ALast. Of these nodes, \AIce\ are touched by the model's own sea-ice field at some step of the \AYears\ years; under ice the model damps the waves to millimetres rather than leaving a gap, so those series are partly the ice's and are recorded but not certified. The remaining \ACells\ open-sea cells (\AGlobalFour\ on the global lattice, \ABrazil\ on the Brazilian margin) are the atlas of Section~\ref{sec:atlas}. The second cut is the full 3-hourly series, \RNodeN\ values each, at \RNodes\ nodes chosen for the regions the method paper names: the Campos, Santos, Esp\'irito Santo, Pelotas, Potiguar and Foz do Amazonas basins on the Brazilian margin; the Drake Passage, the central Antarctic Circumpolar Current, the high southern latitudes and the northern sub-Antarctic; the Arabian Sea, the western Pacific south of Japan and the extratropical North Atlantic. A cell is the series at its node, not an area mean; nothing is interpolated.

\subsection{Three buoys}
To decide numbers other people printed for the same data, the three NDBC buoys of the environmental-contour benchmark of \citet{haselsteiner2021} --- its datasets A, B and C, hourly, the provided and the retained years together, \RBuoyN\ values each over \RBuoyYears\ years --- are certified at five block sizes (hourly, daily, weekly, monthly, annual). The benchmark's appendix prints the $\Hs$ marginal that each contributing team fitted to the provided ten years, and a scientific library's fits to the same series were recorded as printed (Section~\ref{sec:printed}).

\subsection{The contour benchmark}\label{sec:ecdata}
Exercise~1 of the benchmark \citep{haselsteiner2021} holds six hourly datasets: A, B and C are the sea states $(\Hs, T_z)$ of the three buoys above, D, E and F the wind--wave states $(u_{10}, \Hs)$ at three points of the coastDat hindcast. Participants received a ``provided'' part of each (ten years for A--C, twenty-five for D--F), the organizers kept a ``retained'' part of the same length, and nine groups submitted 1- and 20-year contours for A--C and 1- and 50-year contours for D--F as lists of vertices --- \EContributions\ contour sets and \EContours\ files in all, one group having submitted three constructions. Together the provided and retained parts hold \EHoursABC\ hourly states for each of A--C and \EHoursDEF\ for each of D--F, \EHoursTotal\ in all. The organizers counted, for every contour, the observations outside it and printed the counts in two tables beside the number expected for the contour's definition, \EComparisons\ printed numbers. The contour files, the organizers' scripts, the datasets' pin and the preprint's tables (transcribed digit for digit) are held at the benchmark repository's commit \texttt{\ECommit}, \ECorpusFiles\ files verified by SHA-256.

\subsection{The block rule}
A block maximum is the largest value in its calendar block, with the UTC day, the ISO 8601 week, the calendar month and the calendar year as the blocks; the rule lives in one module used by the ledger, the atlas and the browser so that it cannot drift between them. The $T$-year return level of a block of $b$ hours is $F^{-1}(1 - b/(8766\,T))$, with $365.25 \times 24 = 8766$ hours in a year, as the method paper defines it; it is left undefined where fewer than one block falls in the return period.

\section{The certificate of a fit}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Families and criteria}
The six families are those of \citet[\S2.1]{reis2026}, written in the same parameterisation: the normal and the lognormal in $(\mu, \sigma)$; the Weibull $F(x) = 1 - \exp[-(x/\lambda)^k]$; the exponentiated Weibull $F(x) = (1 - \exp[-(x/\lambda)^k])^\alpha$ \citep{mudholkar1993}; the generalized gamma $F(x) = P(\alpha, (x/\lambda)^c)$ with $P$ the regularized incomplete gamma function \citep{stacy1962}; and the Gumbel $F(x) = \exp(-\exp[-(x-\mu)/\beta])$. None carries a location parameter except the Gumbel's own; this is the form the method paper prints and, as Section~\ref{sec:scipy} shows, the form a library's default call does not use. The four criteria are the Anderson--Darling statistic $A^2 = -n - \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (2i-1)[\ln F(x_{(i)}) + \ln S(x_{(n+1-i)})]$ \citep{anderson1954}, the Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance $D = \max_i \max(i/n - F(x_{(i)}),\, F(x_{(i)}) - (i-1)/n)$, the mean squared error $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i [F(x_i) - F_n(x_i)]^2$ against the empirical distribution function with ties, and a chi-square statistic on $\max(8, \lceil \log_2 n \rceil + 1)$ equal-width bins over $[\min x, \max x]$ with expected counts $E_j = n[F(e_{j+1}) - F(e_j)]$ kept where $E_j \ge 5$ and undefined below two kept bins, as \citet[\S2.2]{reis2026} describe. Lower is better for all four. A seventh family, the generalized extreme value law \citep{jenkinson1955}, of which the Gumbel is the $\xi = 0$ member, is certified beside the six and never ranked among them (Section~\ref{sec:gev}).

\subsection{A fit as a box}\label{sec:box}
A maximum-likelihood estimate is a zero of the score $\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta)$. The certifier finds a candidate in floating point --- Nelder--Mead on the log-likelihood followed by a Levenberg--Marquardt ascent --- and then proves two things about a box $X$ around it, both in outward-rounded interval arithmetic in which every datum is enclosed by its two neighbouring doubles and every elementary operation rounds outward \citep{moore2009}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the Krawczyk operator $K(X) = \hat\theta - Y\,\nabla\ell(\hat\theta) + (I - Y\,H(X))(X - \hat\theta)$, with $Y$ an approximate inverse of the Hessian at the centre and $H(X)$ the interval Hessian over $X$, maps $X$ strictly into its interior, so that $X$ contains exactly one zero of the score \citep{krawczyk1969,rump2010};
\item the Hessian is negative definite over $X$: the leading principal minors of $-H(X)$ are enclosed by Sylvester's criterion and proved positive over the whole box, or, where the box's Hessian is too ill-conditioned for its minors, positive at the candidate with every Hessian over the box proved nonsingular, which gives the same conclusion on a connected box.
\end{enumerate}
The zero is then the likelihood's one maximum in $X$, and $X$ is the certificate: its half-width is recorded (at most \RMaxBox\ in the parameters on the 3-hourly ledger, where the widest 100-year level enclosure is \RWidestLevel\ m). Everything downstream is an interval extension over $X$: each criterion, each return level, and the log-likelihood itself, so that two families' values on the same data can be compared. The arithmetic is that of \citet[\S3]{certmethod}; what is particular to likelihoods is the length of the sums, since a running interval sum rounds outward at every one of $n$ additions and at $n = \RBuoyN$ its width is a thousand times what the terms carry, so each endpoint stream is summed by the Sum2 algorithm with the error bound of \citet{ogita2005} added outward. The gamma-function family ($\ln\Gamma$, $\psi$, $\psi'$, $P$, $P^{-1}$) is enclosed by Binet's formula with the first omitted term as the bound, the series of $P$ with a geometric tail, and bisection with certain sign for $P^{-1}$; the score and Hessian come from second-order forward differentiation over the same arithmetic, so that no derivative is written by hand.

What this does not prove is that no better maximum exists elsewhere in the family: that is the search's claim, as it is any optimiser's, and the paper says so wherever a fit is stated. It also does not say that the family is the law of the sea, nor how far another record of the same length would move the number; the sampling width is a statistical statement and is kept apart (Section~\ref{sec:stat}).

\subsection{Rankings as decisions}\label{sec:rank}
With every statistic an enclosure, a ranking of families under a criterion is \vDecided\ when the lowest enclosure lies wholly below every other, and \vRefused\ otherwise, naming the overlap. A family without a certified fit refuses the ranking --- its maximum might exist and win --- with one exception: the generalized gamma whose likelihood is proved highest at its lognormal limit (Section~\ref{sec:limits}) is left out, named, and the lognormal, which is what its likelihood reaches, is ranked in its stead. A climb stopped at a family's declared boundary proves nothing and blocks the ranking; so does a statistic the arithmetic cannot enclose, and a chi-square bin whose expected count straddles five. \vDecided\ ranks the criterion's arithmetic on this sample; it is not a test between models. The rule is one function, called by the ledger and by the browser, and the atlas's regional claims are decided through it.

\subsection{Families whose supremum is at a limit}\label{sec:limits}
As $\alpha \to \infty$ with $c \to 0$ the generalized gamma tends to the lognormal \citep{prentice1974}, so its likelihood's supremum is at least the lognormal's maximum, and on many series the likelihood rises all the way there. In Stacy's coordinates the ridge is too flat for any contraction argument. In Prentice's $(\mu, \sigma, Q)$, with $Q = 1/\sqrt{\alpha}$ and the likelihood written as a power series in $Q$ with proved tails, $Q = 0$ is an ordinary point, and a maximum beside the limit contracts (\APrentice\ such fits are certified in these coordinates on the atlas, \RPrentice\ on the 3-hourly ledger). Where no maximum is found the family is refused at its edge only with a proof: over a box $B \times (0, q_1]$, with $B$ a neighbourhood of the lognormal fit of the form $\hat\mu \pm k\hat\sigma/\sqrt n$, $\hat\sigma(1 \pm k/\sqrt{2n})$ for the widest $k$ among $3, 1, 0.3, 0.1$ that proves it, the $Q$-component of the Prentice score is proved negative, so every member there is less likely than the lognormal at the same $(\mu, \sigma)$ and hence than the lognormal's maximum. Like a certified maximum this is a local fact, and its neighbourhood is recorded. On buoy A's hourly series, for instance, the proof holds with $k = \RGGABoundaryK$ and $q_1 = \RGGABoundaryQ$, the score's $Q$-component at most $\RGGADqHi$ over the box.

The exponentiated Weibull's $\alpha$ runs away on another kind of series. With $\theta = \lambda^k$ and $\beta = \theta \ln\alpha$ the family reads $F(x) = \exp\{e^{\beta/\theta}\ln(1 - e^{-x^k/\theta})\}$, a Gumbel law of $\Hs^k$ with location $\beta$ and scale $\theta$ once $e^{-x^k/\theta}$ is small over the data; the runaway $\alpha$ is a regime that the $(\alpha, k, \lambda)$ coordinates place in the millions or beyond, not an edge, and in $(k, \theta, \beta)$ the maximum is an ordinary point, certified there at \AEwG\ atlas cell-blocks. Where the climb runs on even in those coordinates, toward $k \to 0$ --- a Gumbel law of $\ln\Hs$, which is a Fr\'echet law --- the family is refused with that reason (\AEwStop\ atlas cell-blocks), and the ranking with it, because the family's supremum there is not yet proved to be the Fr\'echet limit itself. An earlier version of the ledger used a threshold ($\alpha > 500$ for the generalized gamma) in place of the boundary proof; on six 3-hourly series the family's maximum sits beside the limit at $\alpha$ between \RNearAlphaLo\ and \RNearAlphaHi, certified there, and the threshold rule handed \RNearMoves\ of their \RNearChoices\ choices to another family. The present ledger keeps that rule's outcome beside the certificate's at every cell-block, so that the difference between a threshold and a proof can be counted (Section~\ref{sec:atlas}).

\subsection{The seventh family}\label{sec:gev}
The method paper's six hold one extreme-value law, the Gumbel. The generalized extreme value law $F(x) = \exp\{-[1 + \xi(x-\mu)/\sigma]^{-1/\xi}\}$ is certified beside them at every cell and block, written so that $\xi = 0$ is an ordinary point and with the support condition $1 + \xi(x-\mu)/\sigma > 0$ enforced on every datum. Its likelihood has no global maximum --- below $\xi = -1$ it is unbounded as the support's upper end reaches the largest datum --- so its fit is the certified maximum in the region where the estimator is regular, $\xi > -0.5$ \citep{smith1985}, and a box that reaches $-0.5$ is refused. The GEV is never ranked among the six: the tables that read against the method paper are the paper's; a separate column records what the Anderson--Darling statistic decides among the seven by the same rule.

\subsection{Statistical, not certified}\label{sec:stat}
A certificate says what a fit \emph{is} on these \AYears\ years; it does not say how far another \AYears\ years of the same sea could move it. The ledger carries that width beside the records, labelled: the delta method's 95\% intervals on the decided family's 100- and 1000-year levels, taken on the logarithm of the level so that they stay positive, from the observed information at the fitted point in the certificate's own coordinates and the level's gradient by central differences --- asymptotic, with successive blocks taken as independent and the family as right \citep{coles2001}; the runs estimator of the extremal index of the daily maxima \citep{smith1994}; and, on request in the browser, a profile-likelihood interval. These are statements of another kind and are drawn apart from the certified ones in every figure of this paper.

\subsection{Printed fits}\label{sec:printedmethod}
A number someone printed is decided over the box its digits allow: a printed ``0.0634'' is the interval $[0.06335, 0.06345]$, and the full log-likelihood and the levels are evaluated over that box against the certified fit on the same values. The verdict is \textsc{reproduced} when the certified box lies inside the printed digits (they are its rounding); \textsc{consistent} when the digits lie inside the certified box; \textsc{off the maximum} when the two are apart and the printed likelihood lies below the certified maximum by a proved margin; \textsc{not the certified fit} when they are apart without such a margin; \textsc{outside its support} when the printed location leaves data with zero density; \textsc{not a member} when a printed parameter lies outside the family; and \textsc{not decided} otherwise. The words are the same for the benchmark's marginals, the library's fits and the design table.

\subsection{The record and its controls}\label{sec:controls}
The discipline is the method's \citep[\S5]{certmethod}; its instances here are these. Every certifying module is pinned by its SHA-256 in the atlas ledger (\ACodeModules\ modules), every worker of the atlas run refuses other bytes, and the page that serves the atlas refuses to build over a ledger its own bundle did not write; the corpus is pinned the same way (digest \ACorpusSha). A battery of \HsevaChecks\ checks runs before any page is written, among them red controls that must fire: logarithms skewed to the right refuse the generalized gamma at its limit with the proof; a 40,000-point generalized gamma with $\alpha \approx 900$ is certified beside its limit, where a stop at $\alpha = 500$ would miss it; a family stopped at its boundary blocks the ranking; a saddle point is refused by the second-order test; and the printed-fit decision has \PrintedChecks\ checks with \PrintedReds\ red controls of its own. Any cell can be re-certified in a browser from the pinned data by the same bytes, and the result is compared with the ledger's record by digest. The atlas took \ASecondsHours\ hours on seven worker threads of a laptop; the 3-hourly ledger \RSeconds\ seconds.

\section{The decision of a contour count}\label{sec:contourmethod}

\subsection{The count the benchmark made}
For each contour set and dataset, the organizers' script concatenates the provided and retained hours, reads the contour file in a column order hand-coded per participant, and asks matplotlib's \texttt{Path.contains\_points} \citep{hunter2007} about every hour: a crossing-number (even-odd) test in double precision on the polygon closed implicitly from its last vertex to its first, with no defined answer on the boundary. An observation is outside when the test says it is not inside; a second count keeps those with $\Hs > 1$ m (A--C), or $u_{10} > 1$ m/s and $\Hs > 1$ m (D--F). The tables print, for the long return period, the mean over three datasets and the three integers, and for the one-year contours the three-dataset means to one decimal; beside each, the number expected for the contour's definition. That count is the benchmark's metric, and it is a finite, decidable quantity.

\subsection{Literals and the polygon a file denotes}
Every number in the benchmark is a decimal literal, and each is read as the rational its digits denote, beside its double image. A file's vertices define a polygon closed from the last vertex to the first, as the organizers' test closes it; for each polygon the record holds, exactly, whether any two non-adjacent edges cross properly, touch or fold back, which edges cross, whether the implicit closing edge is among them and how long it is, the signed area, and the maximum of $\Hs$ along the contour, which is attained at a vertex because $\Hs$ is linear on an edge. A polygon with no crossing, touch or fold is \emph{simple}; the others are reported and counted all the same, because they were counted.

\subsection{Inside, outside, on}
A point is classified by the even-odd crossing number of a horizontal ray \citep{shimrat1962,hormann2001}, in the half-open convention of the organizers' library. Every predicate is a sign --- of the $2\times 2$ determinant that orients a point against an edge, or of a difference of two literals --- and each is evaluated first in double precision and trusted only when its magnitude clears a forward-error bound proved for the inputs \citep{higham2002,fortune1996}: with every coordinate of magnitude at most $M$ and $u = 2^{-53}$, the determinant's computed value is within $48uM^2$ of the true one, which is $8.7\times10^{-11}$ at the $M = 128$ that covers the benchmark, so a computed determinant above $10^{-10}$ in magnitude has the right sign and one below is re-decided in arbitrary-precision integers, each literal scaled to sixty-four decimal places. A point on an edge or at a vertex is \textsc{on}, a third answer the float test does not have; the nonzero-winding count is kept beside the even-odd one. A printed integer \emph{agrees} with the record when it lies in $[n_{\mathrm{out}}, n_{\mathrm{out}} + n_{\mathrm{on}}]$ and is \emph{exact} when it equals $n_{\mathrm{out}}$; a printed mean is read the same way through its rounding; a printed number that agrees under neither reading is \emph{not the count of the file on record}.

\subsection{The expected numbers}
Beside each count the benchmark prints $E = n\,\alpha_t$, with $\alpha = 1/(T \times 8766)$ the hourly exceedance probability of a $T$-year contour and $\alpha_t$ the probability that the contour is exceeded anywhere: $\alpha$ itself for a total-exceedance construction, an exact rational, and for IFORM $\exp(-\beta^2/2)$ with $\beta = \Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha)$ \citep{mackay2021}. The latter is enclosed: $\operatorname{erfc}$ by Laplace's continued fraction \citep[\S7.1.14]{abramowitz1964}, whose consecutive convergents bracket the value, $\beta$ by bisection that tightens only where the sign is certain, and the rest in interval arithmetic. The printed value is then read as a rounding of the enclosure, or not.

\subsection{Controls}
The decider is one module with no dependencies, so the page on which a reader classifies any sea state against any submitted contour runs the bytes that wrote the ledger. Its battery holds \EBatteryChecks\ checks, \EBatteryReds\ of them red controls that must fire: among them a determinant, found by random search, whose double-precision value is $+3.6\times10^{-15}$ and whose exact sign is negative; a bow-tie that must be reported as one crossing; a square traced twice, on which the even-odd and winding rules must part; the two columns swapped, which must move a planted point across the contour; and a point $10^{-20}$ outside an edge, which must not be \textsc{on}. The full re-decision is in \citet{toledo2026ec}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What a certificate says}
For each fit the record holds the digest of the data, the choices made (the family, the block rule, the coordinates), a box proved to contain the likelihood's one maximum, the enclosure of each statistic and level over it, and the digests of the code; for each ranking it holds the verdict and, where refused, the overlap; for each printed number it holds the box the digits allow and the verdict; and for each contour it holds the file's digest, the polygon the file denotes with its diagnostics, the rule and its boundary convention, the counts under both rules, and the hours of any boundary point. Anyone can check these claims from the record alone, in any arithmetic that rounds outward, and the atlas does so in a browser from the pinned data. In the regulatory sense nothing here certifies anything: offshore units are approved by classification societies under national rules, and a design basis is theirs to accept \citep{dnv2021,iso19901}. The word is used in this paper in its mathematical sense only, for a statement with a machine-checkable proof, and the objects offered are such proofs.

\paragraph{What it does not say}
A certificate does not rank models: \vDecided\ means that one criterion's arithmetic separates the families on this sample, and the Arabian Sea node shows a decided choice that no one would design to. It does not say that a better maximum is absent elsewhere in a family, which remains the search's claim; a branch-and-bound proof over the family would close that gap and is the natural next step. It does not give the sampling width of a level, which is a statistical statement made beside the record and labelled; nor the hindcast's own error against the sea it simulates. And a decided regional claim depends on the box drawn for it; the atlas lets the method paper's authors redraw their own. On the contour side, no contour is called right or wrong: a count of exceedances is the benchmark's own metric, taken on its own terms; a self-crossing polygon is a property of a file as submitted, not necessarily of the construction that produced it; the enclosures certify the benchmark's formula for the expected number, not the independence it assumes; and the printed tables read are those of the preprint held in the benchmark's repository.

\paragraph{What it changes}
Two practical consequences follow. The first is for software: the same library returns two 100-year levels for the same data depending on a default, neither of which announces whether it is a maximum, and the generalized gamma's likelihood has no interior maximum on \AGGLimit\ of \AFits\ atlas fits, where any optimiser prints a point on the way. A pipeline that fixes the location and uses the boundary proof agrees with the certificate; one that does not can be a factor of two off at the 100-year level with no warning. The second is for standards. A standard for marginal fits --- which the operators of the Brazilian margin's fields have reason to want, since every contractor builds its own model \citep{reis2026} --- can be read off the atlas: which choices it must fix (the location, the block rule, the coordinates in which a family is fitted, the treatment of a family at its limit, the criterion), and where a certificate rather than an optimiser has to say what the fit is. The certificate is the second half of such a standard: the data stay where they are, and the proof travels. The same holds for contours, at no cost to whoever draws them: a contour submitted or delivered as an explicit polygon --- closed, with no vertex repeated consecutively, no coordinate that is not a number, a header naming the columns --- with the rule for ``inside'', the convention for a point on the boundary and the thresholds declared, and the count published as an integer per contour and dataset beside the file's digest, is decidable by construction; an exact point-in-polygon decider is a few hundred lines in any language and re-decides the whole benchmark in seconds. The expected number beside each count should be printed exactly where it is a rational and as an enclosure where it is not.

\paragraph{Cost}
The atlas's \AFits\ fits took \ASecondsHours\ hours on a laptop's seven worker threads, with the generalized gamma's three climbs and the Prentice fallback the largest part; six fits of a \RNodeN-value 3-hourly series take minutes each. Re-certifying one cell in a browser takes seconds. The contour benchmark is re-decided in \ESeconds\ s on a laptop: \EPredicatesSci\ predicates, of which \EExactPredicates\ (\EExactPredicatesPerMillion\ per million) fell below the filter's bound and were decided in integers. The arithmetic is JavaScript on IEEE-754 doubles with simulated directed rounding (one ulp outward per operation), which is conservative by a factor of about two in the last bit and costs nothing at the accuracy needed here.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
The extreme-value chain of \citet{reis2026} was repeated on its own public hindcast with every fit certified as the likelihood's one maximum in a recorded box, every statistic and level an enclosure, every ranking a decision or a refusal with its reason, and the two families whose supremum lies at a limit treated by proof rather than by threshold. Over \ACells\ open-sea cells, \ACertified\ of \AFits\ fits are certified and \AGGLimit\ are refused at the generalized gamma's lognormal limit with the proof; a threshold in place of that proof changes the selected family at \ANaiveDiff\ cell-blocks and the verdict of \AClaimsMoved\ of the method paper's ten regional claims. The same decision applied to other people's numbers finds that a library's default call is the maximum it reports on none of \SFits\ fits, that the printed marginals of a benchmark span a factor of nearly three in the hourly 100-year level on one buoy, and that \TOff\ of \TRows\ rows of a published design table are off the maximum of the nearest public cell by a measured amount. On the contour side, the \EContours\ contours of the benchmark on the same buoys were read as the exact polygons their files denote and \EHoursTotal\ hourly sea states classified inside, outside or on each with every sign decided: the organizers' procedure reproduces \EExact\ of their \EComparisons\ printed numbers to the integer, one more within two observations that lie exactly on a contour, and two are traced to files rewritten after the count; \ENotSimple\ polygons cross themselves and \ENotClosed\ are closed by an edge nobody listed; the expected numbers are certified. What is proposed to the metocean community is not a different estimate of the 100-year wave or a different contour but a different object to accompany them: a record that any reader can re-run, in which refusal is a verdict.
