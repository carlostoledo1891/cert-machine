## Why Anthropic? (Carlos's draft of 2026-11-03, refined; 338 words)

I build things to be honest. Over the past few months I went from reproducing one manufacturing-lab result to an independent engine that verifies public claims, most of them generated by AI. It is not obliged to return a binary verdict: it can decline to decide, and when it does it names what is missing. With it I audited OpenAI's releases on the Navier–Stokes equations and on mathematics, METR's time-horizon fits, and the answer key of GSM8K. I also reproduced Anthropic's Lean refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis from anthropics/formal-math: I checked the refutation against the statement it was written for, then built a bridge of 51 theorems connecting it to ETH as the literature states it, claiming nothing beyond what the kernel checks validated. My first result accepted outside was on Erdős problem 1186, and Terence Tao's optimization-constants registry credits me as the author of the independent reproduction of several of its records.

That work showed me where, in my view, the hard problem in AI now sits. As models become more capable, the bottleneck moves from generating answers to knowing which answers to trust, including the answers used as the basis for a reward. I want to work on that at a lab that treats it as a safety problem rather than a marketing one. Anthropic publishes its formal claims in a verifiable form; that is what made my replication of the ETH work possible at all, and it is the kind of culture I want to work inside.

I am a self-taught, independent developer with a design background. What I bring beyond the engine is the ability to make rigorous results understandable and usable: every verdict I publish carries its reason, its witness and the command that re-runs it, drawn in a visual grammar that says what each number rests on. I would like to apply that to how Anthropic communicates the results of its evaluations, and to learn how the people who think about this every day frame the question from inside.

## What research would you most like to work on, and why? (Carlos's draft, refined; 322 words)

Three things, in order of how ready they are.

First, reward channels that cannot be exploited. I have built four environments on Prime Intellect's Hub aimed at the robustness of verifiers: verify-gym, break-the-grader, lattice-claims and blind-spot. In each, the model is rewarded for a precise decision or for refusing with a clear reason, and adversarial submissions are planted to detect reward hacking before anything is scored. The measurement behind them: a tolerance grader admits 89.55% of 4,000 provably wrong answers; a certificate grader admits none. I would extend this to evaluation systems whose own robustness is certified, so that a policy cannot raise its reward without actually getting closer to the truth, and measure what a policy trained that way learns that a policy trained on a gameable reward does not.

Second, certified oversight of claims about neural-network weights. Frontier models are starting to read and write weights directly, as in MIT's weight-space programming result, which I audited within a day of its release. Today such claims rest on sampling: "succeeded in 1,024 episodes". I would use interval methods to turn mechanistic claims about small networks into formal proofs or concrete counterexamples, giving interpretability a verdict channel and not only an explanation channel.

Third, calibrated refusal as a measurable behaviour: benchmarks in which "I cannot decide this, because X" is a correct and valid answer, scored as such, with the forged version of X refused. My environments already score it apart; the open question is whether a model can be trained toward it without collapsing into refusing everything, which is what I observed on a 9B model in a pre-registered run.

Why these three: they are the places where my own work found "checked" and "trusted" coming apart, and each has a public, re-runnable record behind it that a mentor can inspect before deciding whether the work is worth a desk.
