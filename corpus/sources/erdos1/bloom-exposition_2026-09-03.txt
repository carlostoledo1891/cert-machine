I will sketch the new construction of GPT, which provides a counterexample. In my efforts to understand this construction it ended up quite far from the original GPT writeup, but is (I believe) essentially the same construction, in slightly different language. This is just a sketch, but hopefully suffices to demonstrate the main ideas at play; I also wanted to highlight the connections with Siegel's lemma and geometry of numbers, which I discovered while writing this exposition and were obscured in the original GPT proof, and which should be fleshed out in the 'final form' of this proof.
Let\[f(n)=\inf\frac{\max A}{2^{n-1}},\]where the infimum is taken over all dissociated sets of positive integers $A$. This problem then asks whether $f(n)\gg 1$, and the current lower bounds have the shape $f(n)\gg n^{-1/2}$. The upper bound $f(n)\leq 1$ is trivial, and the best upper bound previously was $f(n) \leq 0.44004$ for all large $n$, by Bohman 
[Bo98b]
. 
The proof we sketch here provides a much better upper bound: for any $\epsilon>0$, there are infinitely many $n$ such that $f(n)<\epsilon$. The proof is currently non-quantitative, but just due to the non-effectiveness of the part where we approximate an arbitrary lattice by a primitive lattice. It seems likely that using a suitably effective version of this could prove something like, for infinitely many $n$,\[f(n) \leq n^{-\frac{c}{\log\log n}}\]for some constant $c>0$.
The same construction also likely gives a new lower bound for the strength of Siegel's lemma, so before dissociated sets I'll briefly digress.
Siegel's lemma and dissociated sets
(If you only care about the dissociated sets problem, skip this section.) Siegel's lemma, proved by Siegel 
[Si29]
 in 1929, proves that, for any $d\geq 2$, for any non-zero integral vector $a\in\mathbb{Z}^d$, there exists a non-zero $x\in\mathbb{Z}^d$ such that $a\cdot x=0$ and\[\| x\|_{\infty}^{d-1}\ll_d \| a\|_\infty.\](This is actually a special case of the more general Siegel's lemma.) Let $C_d$ be the minimal implicit constant on the right-hand side. Bombieri and Vaaler 
[BoVa83]
 proved that $C_d\ll \sqrt{d}$, while Schinzel 
[Sc02]
 proved that $C_d\geq 1$. It may have been Schinzel who first observed (as recorded by Aliev 
[Al08]
) that $C_d$ has a close relationship to dissociated sets.
One direction, that $f(d)\geq C_d^{-1}$, is easy to prove: if $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_d)$ is a vector of a dissociated set of integers, then whenever $a\cdot x=0$ we have $\| x\|_\infty \geq 2$, whence\[2^{d-1}\leq C_d\| a\|_\infty\]and so $f(d)\geq C_d^{-1}$.
The GPT construction goes in the opposite direction (at least for infinitely many $d$): essentially it constructs a sequences of lattices which prove that $C_d$ can get arbitrarily large, and constructs corresponding dissociated sets which show $f(d)$ can get arbitrarily small.
Note that this requires the converse of the simple inequality above; it would be desirable to make this connection more explicit, and prove that $f(d) \asymp C_d^{-1}$ for all $d$. (Some form of the argument below may work.) Those interested in this connection should start with 
[Al08]
 or Section 6 of 
[AlHe23]
. For now though, we put Siegel's lemma to one side and return to dissociated sets.
From $Q$-dissociated to dissociated
The first observation is that it suffices to find a large set which is $Q$-dissociated, which means that if $\sum c_iu_i=0$ and $\lvert c_i\rvert<Q$ for all $i$ then $c_i\equiv 0$. If $U$ is such a set in $[1,Y]$ of size $m+1$, and $Q=2^l$, then we can create a genuinely dissociated set $A\subset [1,X]$ with $X\ll QY$ and $\lvert A\rvert=l(m+1)$ by setting $A=\{1,2,4,\ldots,2^{l-1}\}\cdot U$, so that\[\frac{X}{2^{\lvert A\rvert}}\leq \frac{QY}{2^{lm+l}}= \frac{Y}{Q^{m}}.\]In other words, if we define $f_Q(n)$ to be the minimum of $\frac{\max a_n}{Q^{n-1}}$ over all $Q$-dissociated sequences of positive integers $a_1<\cdots<a_n$, then (whenever $Q=2^l$)\[f(l(n+1))\leq f_Q(n).\]So it suffices to make $f_Q(n)$ arbitrarily small.
$Q$-dissociated sets from lattices
A codimension $1$ primitive lattice is one of the shape $L=u^\perp\cap \mathbb{Z}^d$, where $u$ is a primitive integer vector. The connection to dissociativity is the observation that if $u$ generates such a lattice with $L\cap (-Q,Q)^d=\{0\}$ then the coordinates of $u$ form a $Q$-dissociated set: indeed, if\[\sum c_iu_i=0\]then this means precisely that $c\cdot u=0$ so $c\in L$, whence either $c=0$ or there is some coordinate of $c$ outside $(-Q,Q)$. 
So the task is now to find a primitive lattice with a 'small' primitive vector. The study of primitive lattices has a long history (in particular heavily investigated by Cassels and Schmidt). The key fact that we need (see e.g. the results of Horesh and Karasik 
[HoKa23]
, who actually prove a stronger equidistribution theorem) is that codimension $1$ primitive lattices, after rescaling, are dense in the space of all codimension $1$ lattices. (In other words, the 'distance' of a primitive lattice from a given lattice can be made arbitrarily small after a suitably large dilation.)
(This is a little vague and perhaps overkill as a result, but suffices for this heuristic sketch; the original GPT argument proved directly what was required going via Smith normal form, but I suspect this is all well-trodden ground in the lattice literature, and I leave further discussion to those with a better understand of lattices.)
Let $V_d\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{d}$ be the hyperplane given by the equation $\sum x_i=0$, and let $\Lambda\subset V_d$ be a lattice of full rank, such that $\Lambda\cap (-1,1)^d=\{0\}$. Note that, for large integer $Q$, the lattice $Q\cdot \Lambda\subset V_d$ is a lattice of full rank such that $\Lambda\cap (-Q,Q)^d=\{0\}$. We therefore choose $L_Q$ some primitive lattice arbitrarily 'close' to $Q\cdot \Lambda$. This has the property (perhaps after replacing $Q$ by $(1+o(1))Q$) that $L_Q\cap (-Q,Q)^d=\{0\}$, and so it remains to show that, if $u$ is a primitive integer vector with $L_Q=u^\perp cap \mathbb{Z}^d$, then $u$ has small entries.
Since $Q\cdot \Lambda\subset V_d$, the unit normal of $L_Q$ converges to the unit normal of $V_d$, that is,\[\frac{u}{\| u\|_2}\to \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}(1,\ldots,1).\]On the other hand, the covolume of $L_Q$ is equal to $\| u\|_2$, and converges to $Q^{d-1}\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda)$. Therefore, if $\Delta=d^{-1/2}\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda)$, every coordinate of $u$ is equal to $(\Delta+o(1))Q^{d-1}$, where the $o(1)$ term $\to 0$ as $Q\to \infty$. 
Combined with the earlier observation, this implies that\[f_Q(d)\leq \Delta+o_{Q\to \infty}(1),\]and so $f(d) \leq \Delta$. 
In summary, if there is a lattice $\Lambda\subset V_d$ with covolume $\sqrt{d}\Delta$, such that $\Lambda \cap (-1,1)^d=\{0\}$, then\[f(d)\leq \Delta.\]It remains to construct such lattices with arbitrarily small $\Delta$.
Constructing the lattices
We construct lattices via an inductive procedure, for which it is useful to actually construct something slightly stronger. Call a pair $(\Lambda,v)$ admissible if $\Lambda$ is a lattice in $V_d$ of full rank, such that $\Lambda \cap (-1,1)^d=\{0\}$, and $v\in \mathbb{R}^d\backslash V_d$ is such that whenever $tv\in \Lambda+(-1,1)^d$ we have $\lvert t\rvert<1$. We write $h=\sum_i v_i$ for the 'height' of $v$. 
We then set $\Gamma=\Lambda+v\cdot \mathbb{Z}$, which is a lattice of rank $d$, and satisfies\[\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma)=\frac{h}{\sqrt{d}}\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda)=h\Delta.\]The goal is now to construct admissible pairs $(\Lambda,v)$ with $\frac{\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma)}{h}\to 0$.
There are two parts to this: constructing an initial admissible pair, and then iterating this. Both parts are elementary, and where the real novelty of the proof lies, so I'll try to spell this out carefully.
Let $b\geq 3$ be an odd integer. We start by constructing an admissible pair $(\Lambda_1,v_1)$ in $\mathbb{R}^b$. Consider the cyclic shift linear transformation given by $(Tz)_i=z_i+\frac{1}{2}z_{i-1}$ (with the indices interpreted cyclically). Note that this maps $V_b\to V_b$. Let $\Lambda_0=\mathbb{Z}^b\cap V_b$, and $\Lambda_1=T(\Lambda_0)\subset V_b$. Let\[v_1=T(1,0,\ldots,0)=(1,0,\ldots,0,1/2),\]so that $h_1=3/2$.
We claim that $(\Lambda_1,v_1)$ is admissible. Let's first show that $\Lambda\cap (-1,1)^d=\{0\}$. Suppose that $T(z)\in (-1,1)^d$ for some $z\in \Lambda_0$. This means that\[\lvert z_i+\tfrac{1}{2}z_{i-1}\rvert <1\]for all $0\leq i<b$, which implies that $\lvert z_i\rvert <2$ for all $0\leq i<b$. Since all $z_i$ are integers, they must all be in $\{-1,0,1\}$. Now if $z_i=1$ this further implies that $z_{i-1}=-1$, and then $z_{i-2}=1$, and so on. But since this goes round all coordinates cyclically, and there are an odd number of coordinates, we get $z_i=-1$, a contradiction. Therefore $z_i=0$ for all $z$, so $T(z)=0$ as required.
We also need to show that if $tv\in \Lambda+(-1,1)^d$ then $\lvert t\rvert<1$. This is shown by a similar argument: suppose there exists $z'\in \Lambda_0$ such that if $z=z'+t(1,0,\ldots,0)$ then $T(z)\in (-1,1)^b$. All coordinates of $z$, except possibly the first, are integral, so by the argument in the preceding paragraph, coupled with the fact that $z=z'+t(1,0,\ldots,0)$ for some $z'\in V_b$,\[z=(z_0,z_1,\ldots,z_{b-1})=\left(t-\sum_{i\geq 1}z_i, 1,-1,1,-1,\ldots,0,0,\ldots,0\right)\](or the same with the signs flipped). 
There are now a number of cases. If $z_i=0$ for $i\geq 1$ then this implies $z_0=t$ and $\lvert t\rvert=\lvert z_0+z_{b-1}/2\rvert<1$ and we are done. 
Otherwise, if $z_{b-1}=0$ then $\lvert z_0\rvert=\lvert z_0+\frac{1}{2}z_{b-1}\rvert <1$, but also $\lvert z_1+\frac{1}{2}z_0\rvert<1$ and $z_1=1$ so $z_0<0$, and hence\[z_0=t-\sum_{i\geq 1}z_i\in (-1,0).\]The sum here is a (possibly empty) alternating sum of $\pm 1$, beginning with $+1$, so is either $0$ or $1$, and thus we again have $\lvert t\rvert <1$. 
Finally, if $z_{b-1}=-1$ (the only other possibility, since $b$ is odd) then $\sum_{i\geq 1}z_i=0$ and so $z_0=t$. Using the cyclic inequality twice we have $\lvert 1+t/2\rvert<1$, so and so $t<0$, and\[\lvert t-1/2\rvert=\lvert z_0+\tfrac{1}{2}z_{b-1}\rvert <1,\]whence $t>-1/2$ and again $\lvert t\rvert<1$.
This constructs an admissible pair $(\Lambda,v)$ in $\mathbb{R}^b$, with $h=3/2$, and if $\Gamma_1=\Lambda_1+v_1\cdot \mathbb{Z}=M\mathbb{Z}^b$, where $M=I+\frac{1}{2}P$ where $P$ is the cyclic shift $z_i\mapsto z_{i-1}$, and hence\[\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_1)=\lvert \det M\rvert=1+2^{-b}.\]By itself, this has\[\Delta=\frac{1+2^{-b}}{3/2}.\]Taking $b\to \infty$ yields $f(b)\leq (1+o(1))\frac{2}{3}$, which does not even beat Bohman's bound. But this admissible definition is ripe for iteration.
The iteration
Given an admissible pair $(\Lambda_s,v_s)$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$, define the admissible pair $(\Lambda_{s+1},v_{s+1})$ in $\mathbb{R}^{db}$ by\[\Lambda_{s+1}=\{(\lambda_0+\beta_0v_s,\ldots,\lambda_{b-1}+\beta_{b-1}v_s) : \lambda_j \in \Lambda_s\textrm{ and }\beta\in \Lambda_1\}\]and\[v_{s+1}=(v_s,0,\ldots,0,v_s/2).\]It is easy to check that $\Lambda_{s+1}$ is a lattice of rank $bd-1$ in $V_{bd}$ (note that the parameterisation is injective, since summing the coordinates in the $j$th block gives $\beta_jh_s$, whence $\beta$ can be recovered as $h_s\neq 0$).
Furthermore, this pair is indeed admissible. Firstly, suppose $y\in \Lambda_{s+1}\cap (-1,1)^{bd}$. Each 'block' of $y$ is in $(\Lambda_s+\beta_iv_s)\cap (-1,1)^{d}$, so by admissibility of $(\Lambda_s,v_s)$ we have $\lvert \beta_i\rvert <1$ for all $i$. Therefore, since $\beta\in \Lambda_1 \cap (-1,1)^b$, by admissibility of $(\Lambda_1,v_1)$ we have $\beta=0$. Now each block is in $\Lambda_s\cap (-1,1)^d$, and so by admissibility again vanishes everywhere, so $y=0$.
Similarly, if\[y\in (\Lambda_{s+1}+tv_{s+1})\cap (-1,1)^{bd}\]then the $j$th block looks like $\lambda_j+\gamma_j v_s$ for some $\lambda_j\in \Lambda_s$, where $\gamma=\beta+tv_1$ for some $\beta\in \Lambda_1$. By admissibility of $(\Lambda_s,v_s)$ we have $\lvert \gamma_j\rvert <1$, and so\[\gamma=\beta+tv_1\in (\Lambda_1+tv_1)\cap (-1,1)^b,\]so by admissibility of $(\Lambda_1,v_1)$ we have $\lvert t\rvert<1$ as required. 
The heights multiply as $h_{s+1}=h_1h_s$, and so $h_s=(3/2)^s$. Finally, since elements of $\Gamma_{s+1}=\Lambda_{s+1}+v_{s+1}\cdot \mathbb{Z}$ have the shape\[(\lambda_0+\beta_0v_s,\ldots,\lambda_{b-1}+\beta_{b-1}v_s)\]where $\lambda_j\in \Lambda_s$ and $\beta\in \Gamma_1$,
the covolumes similarly scale as\[\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_{s+1})=\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_{1})\left(\frac{h_s}{\sqrt{d}}\mathrm{covol}(\Lambda_s)\right)^b=\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_{1})\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_{s})^b.\](To see this, note that in each block of the shape $(\lambda_j+\beta_jv_s)$, one unit in the \(\beta\)-direction contributes perpendicular length \(h_s/\sqrt d\), so the corresponding covolume factor is $(h_s/\sqrt d)\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_s)$.)
Putting all this together, after $s$ iterations, we have (in $d=b^s$ dimensions)\[\Delta\leq \frac{\mathrm{covol}(\Gamma_s)}{h_s}= \frac{(1+2^{-b})^{1+b+\cdots+b^{s-1}}}{(3/2)^s}.\]For fixed $s$ the numerator $\to 1$ as $b\to \infty$, and so $\Delta$ can be made arbitrarily small by first choosing $s$ very large and then $b$ very large.
