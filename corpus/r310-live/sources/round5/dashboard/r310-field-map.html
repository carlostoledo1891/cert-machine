Is it 40 or 41?

Take 40 points. Join some pairs with a line. Two rules: no three points all joined to each other, and no ten points with no line among them. If no drawing can obey both, the Ramsey number R(3,10) is 40. If one can, it is 41. Nobody knows.

The object

Two rules that pull against each other

Lines kill unconnected sets. But add too many lines and three of them close a triangle. The smaller cases below are real: 5, 8 and 13 points can be done, and one more point cannot. Click a point to stand on it; click a second point to add or remove the line between them.

What to notice. Stand on any point of the 13-point graph: its 4 neighbours have no lines among them (they could not, or a triangle would close). The 8 points it does not touch form, by themselves, a correct solution of the 8-point problem. That is the whole structure of the problem: every point sees a smaller copy of the same question.

The nested structure

Inside the 40-point question sits every smaller one

Pick j points with no lines among them. The points none of them touches must obey the same two rules with 10 − j in place of 10. So their number is capped by a smaller Ramsey number, and those are all known exactly.

Why 40 is different

The slack is 13. No closed case had more than 5.

In the hard case every point has the same number of neighbours, so the untouched part has a fixed number of lines. Counting works when that number falls below the fewest lines such a graph can have. The chart shows how far above the minimum it lands.

What a method cannot see

Three phantoms explain almost every failure

A phantom is something that passes all of a method's checks without being a graph on 40 points. If a method accepts a phantom, it can never prove that 40 is impossible. Today produced three, each for a different blindness.

✕blind to agreement

The one-view phantom

Take one real graph on 30 points and pretend it is what every one of the 40 points sees outside its neighbours. Every count made from a single point is then consistent, at any depth.

It forgets that the views of two points overlap and must agree where they do. This is why ten methods stop at 43. Sharper methods are fooled the same way by a blend of about seven real graphs. 2 sources

✕blind to triples

The pair phantom

A table of how many pairs of each kind exist, with every pair consistent in itself. It satisfies 35 exact equations in whole numbers.

Pairs are never asked to agree on a third point. The same phantom, one level up. Found at the stratum with largest codegree 4. 1 source

✕blind to size

The scale phantom

A real graph with the same proportions as ours, 320 points with 72 neighbours each, where every neighbourhood is a largest unconnected set. Built from a 2010 paper's recipe.

Any argument that only uses ratios would exclude it too, and it exists. So ratios alone can never work. 2 sources

A fourth fact, not a phantom. Real graphs on 40 points obey every local rule (9 neighbours each, no triangle, 1 to 3 shared neighbours) and still contain ten unconnected points. So nothing built from local rules alone can finish the proof. What is not fooled. Three kinds of input survive all three phantoms: an exact small Ramsey number applied to a region of the graph (18 points untouched by 4 chosen ones must contain 6 unconnected, since R(3,6) = 18); whole-number constraints; and statements that force two or more points' views to agree. Two of the three gates that passed today used the first kind; the third showed that at 18 points the pieces simply do not fit together.

Where an idea could enter

Six questions that are open in the plain sense

None of these needs the machinery. Each is a place where a new way of looking would change what gets tried next.

What flips between 22 and 23?

Ten regular graphs exist on 22 points; none on 23. Every count we can write is satisfied on both sides. Something that is not a count separates them. At 18 points the answer is now known: the counts are forced to a single table, every local piece is a real graph, and still the pieces cannot be assembled into one graph, even ignoring the ten-set rule. At 23 they can be assembled, so something more breaks there. Name it, and it is the thing to carry up to 40.

What is the smallest statement about two views agreeing?

Each point sees 30 others outside its neighbours. Two joined points share 22 of those. A proof must use that overlap. Listing all overlaps is the brute force we ruled out; a single inequality about them is what is missing.

Why do extreme graphs end 8, 5, 2, 0?

In every solved case, a graph at the limit contains unconnected sets whose untouched parts are a fixed 8-point graph, then a 5-cycle, then a single line. All 477,142 graphs one rung down do. At 40 only the last step is proved. Counting cannot prove the next; it would need 45 points.

Is the regular case the whole problem?

In every catalogue, whenever a graph exists, one with the maximum possible number of lines exists too. If that held at 40, a counterexample would exist only if a 9-regular one did, and half the map would vanish. No proof is known at any size.

Can rigid spots be forced, then chained?

When a region is exactly as large as a Ramsey number allows, its shape is one of a handful. One such spot (three points fully joined to four) fixes seven whole neighbourhoods. The open question is whether such spots must exist, and whether two of them can coexist.

Where do the 13 extra lines go?

Each untouched part has 13 more lines than the leanest possible graph of its size. The leanest graphs are understood, built from a few patterns. Nothing is known 13 lines above them. A description of what the surplus must look like would be new mathematics.
