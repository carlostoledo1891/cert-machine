chapter 1 · three words

A claim is an inequality. A verdict is one of three words.

Strip any machine-made mathematical claim down to what a computer can decide and you get a sentence like this quantity is at most this number, for every input in this box. A record kissing configuration says every pair of points is at least sixty degrees apart. A bound on a constant says a certain ratio never exceeds a printed decimal. The machine reads the claim as exactly that, over the exact bytes the claimant published, and answers with one of three words.

certified

The inequality holds over the whole box, proved by exact arithmetic or by outward-rounded intervals. Shipped with the certificate and the thing that would have falsified it.

refuted

A specific input violates the inequality. Shipped with that input, so anyone can check the refutation in one line.

refused

The claim cannot be decided from the bytes: a file is missing, a number is not printed, a function has no exact decider. Shipped with the reason and, where there is one, the threshold that would change the word.

never

A probability, a confidence, a "looks right". A floating-point comparison on its own is a screen, not a verdict: it may only prune what is not worth certifying, never admit.

The third word is the one that makes the other two honest. A checker that must answer yes or no will guess when it cannot decide. This one says why it cannot, and what would convert a refusal into a decision. That turns the machine into an incentive: publish the file, print the digit, and the word changes.

chapter 2 · one string of digits, two numbers

The same point, printed, is "too close" or "apart" depending on how many digits you read.

Touch it: print √3/2 to k digits

Two unit vectors at exactly sixty degrees: (1, 0) and (½, √3/2). A claimant prints the second coordinate as a decimal. Read it back three ways and decide, exactly, whether the pair is touching, too close, or apart.

Every decimal a machine prints is an exact rational: 0.8660254037844386 means that fraction over ten to the sixteenth, no more and no less. But the number the machine meant is usually a binary double, and the double nearest to √3/2 is not that decimal; it is 0.86602540378443859659…. Two readings of one string of digits, and they differ in the seventeenth place.

That is not a curiosity. In 2026 a public arena published a 605-point candidate for the kissing number in dimension eleven, with its points printed to fifteen digits. Read as printed digits, {{SIX_VIOL_DIGITS}} of its {{SIX_PAIRS}} pairs are closer than sixty degrees. Read as the doubles those digits denote, {{SIX_VIOL_DOUBLES}}. Both readings refute the record; the counts differ because the reading is part of the verdict.

So every object the machine decides carries a sentence saying how the bytes were read, and when a platform's own verifier reads them another way, that is written down too. A verdict without its reading is a number without a unit.

chapter 3 · we tried to fool it {{SA_MUTANTS}} times

How do you test a judge? Plant errors and see which ones it misses.

Before the method paper was written, its benchmark was pre-registered: take {{SA_OBJECTS}} published objects the machine had already decided, mutate each one in ways whose truth is known by an independent exact witness, and count how often the machine says certified to something false or refuted to something true. A kill switch was declared in advance: if fewer than half the rows could be given a witnessed false mutant, the benchmark would be reported as failed.

chapter 4 · exact arithmetic at ten quadrillion pairs

Floating point can be exact. You just have to prove when.

In September 2026 an autonomous research system published {{KW_ROWS}} new kissing-number lower bounds in dimensions {{KW_DIMS}}, the largest with {{KW_MAXPOINTS}} points in dimension {{KW_MAXDIM}}, and its own verification files said, honestly, that the pairwise angles had not been checked. Checking them is {{KW_PAIRS_SCI}} pairs. Exactly.

The trick is old and elementary. The coordinates are small integers, so every inner product is an integer, and a floating-point unit computes an integer product exactly as long as no partial sum can leave the range the format holds exactly: 2¹¹ for half precision, 2²⁴ for single. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality every partial sum is at most the largest squared norm in the configuration. Check that bound against the data before the run, assert it in the code, and the tensor cores of a rented GPU become an exact integer engine.

chapter 5 · a famous proof, and the ten sentences a machine can check

Most of a celebrated result has no finite layer. The machine says exactly which part does.

In 2026 a language model found a proof that more than two thirds of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function are simple and on the critical line; two mathematicians verified it and it was formalised in Lean. A translator with a closed grammar read every sentence the story's sources print, the manuscripts, the Lean files, the research post, even a video's captions, and either reduced it to a decidable claim or refused it with a rule.

What is read is the finite layer underneath: the formalisation's one numerical certificate, 256 integer enclosures at scale 2{{RfKPow}}, and the constants printed to their digits. The certificate's three inequalities hold over its integers, decided by two programs that share no code with Lean. And its one real-number hypothesis, that the enclosures enclose anything, rests on a file the release describes as "available from the authors". No published byte decides it. We asked for the file, in public.

Then something small and new

Once the constants were rational arithmetic, they could be optimised. The proportion of simple zeros of ξ′ depends on a window function through a ratio of two quadratic forms, so the best polynomial window of each degree is exact linear algebra. The paper's quartic sits {{RF_QUARTIC_GAP}} below its own family's optimum, and the certificate's nine-term series truncation costs more than the whole window gap: {{RF_EPS9}}. A degree-six window and fifteen terms raise the formalisation's constants from {{RfLeanP}} to {{RfNewP}}, with an exact certificate that no even polynomial window of degree up to twenty does better. As a calibration, the same machinery with the ζ kernel returns the Montgomery–Taylor constant to {{RfMtAgreeDigits}} digits.

chapter 6 · what it cannot do, and why that is the point

A referee that never guesses must say, out loud, where it stops.

Theorems

A statement about every integer, or about a limit, is not a finite claim. The machine refuses it with the rule and leaves it to proofs and proof assistants.

Hypotheses nobody can check

A certificate kernel-checked downstream of a file the public cannot read is conditional, for the kernel and for the machine alike. The word is refused, and the ask is the file.

Closed forms it has no decider for

A tangent or a logarithm is admitted and left undecided until someone reduces it to rational arithmetic, as the Montgomery–Taylor constant was, by two alternating series.

Its own code

Soundness rests on arithmetic; the arithmetic is held to an independent library on {{ARB_CASES}} cases of {{ARB_OPS}} operations, to a second implementation on every decision, and to the {{SA_MUTANTS}} mutants above. When it fails, the failure is on the record first.

The register this page is read from holds {{REG_ROWS}} claims from {{REG_CLAIMANTS}} claimants across {{REG_SOURCES}} sources: {{REG_HOLDS}} hold, {{REG_DEFECTS}} have a defect on record, and every row carries the object that justifies its word. The machine has never improved a theorem. It has moved an asterisk, counted a wrong digit, published the threshold that would turn a refusal green, and once in a while, when the arithmetic allowed it, raised a constant by a unit of the sixth digit with a certificate attached. That is the job.
