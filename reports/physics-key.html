The physics answer key: sixty simulator runs decided in exact arithmetic

A physics engine writes its state as floating-point numbers, and every one of those is an exact fraction. So whether a recorded run kept its energy, to a stated tolerance, can be decided with whole numbers and no rounding in the decision. With Claude (Anthropic’s AI model), I recorded 60 runs of MuJoCo 4.0.1 on three scenes whose physics is known exactly and decided each recorded file that way. The engine’s own documentation already describes what the runs show. What is added here is the exact decision, the step at which each tolerance first fails, and a page that decides the same bytes again in a browser tab.

MuJoCo is Google DeepMind’s engine. Nothing on this page is a fault in it: an integrator of finite order is expected to move away from the true motion, and the documentation says so. What is still open

  • It is not a discovery about the engine. MuJoCo’s documentation says that integrating a spinning free body explicitly can gain energy, and that its implicit integrators damp a tumble instead. Its own tests check the energy of a tumbling free body, in floating point with a tolerance. Free fall and the same tumble were compared across four other engines in 2014 and 2015 (the Gazebo “boxes” benchmark), and energy and momentum across five engines in 2015 (Erez, Tassa and Todorov). All of those plot or assert floating-point errors. I looked for an earlier case of conservation laws decided on an engine’s recorded output in exact arithmetic and found none; that is a search, not a proof that none exists.
  • One engine, one version, one machine. The bytes were written by MuJoCo 4.0.1 on one arm64 Mac. Another platform may write different last bits. The verdicts are about the pinned bytes.
  • The tolerances are mine. Six per law, from 1% (or 1 cm) down to exactly zero, fixed before the first run. No sentence of the engine’s documentation promises any of them.
  • Only conserved quantities, and nothing a robot touches. The pendulum’s timing and the tumbling body’s attitude are not compared with a closed form; only energy and angular momentum are. There is no contact and no friction in any scene, and those are where robots spend their time and where a single right answer stops existing.
  • Between the recorded steps, nothing is said. A law that holds at every recorded step holds at those instants.
  • One law was added after the runs were decided. The rules were committed before any simulation step. The law “the spin energy never rises” was added afterwards, when I read that the documentation states it; I had by then seen the other verdicts and an informal count. It is marked as an amendment in corpus/physics-key/RULES.md and is not pre-registered.
  • The second decider is independent by procedure only. It was written by a separate session of the same AI model, given the rules and nothing of the first decider. Both ran under one person. Exact programs decide here, not a model; you should still know that a model wrote both.
  • The fifth integrator is new. The documentation calls “discrete” new and subject to change. In all three scenes it wrote the same bytes as implicitfast.
the idea

A float is a fraction

A 64-bit floating-point number is a whole number times a power of two. A simulator’s state is a list of them: the time, the positions, the velocities. Kinetic energy and angular momentum are sums of products of those numbers, so for a recorded step they are fractions too, with no error at all. “The energy at step 4,812 is within 1% of the energy at step 0” is then a comparison of two whole numbers.

That removes one layer of doubt. When a floating-point check says an error is 1.9%, the check itself carries rounding. Here the only rounding is the engine’s own, which is the thing being measured. The pendulum needs a cosine, which is not a fraction; it is trapped between two fractions, as tightly as each comparison needs.

the scenes

Three motions with a known answer

  • A falling body. Dropped from rest, nothing in its way, for 2 seconds. Its height at every instant is the starting height minus half of g times the time squared, and its speed is g times the time. Two laws: position and speed against those formulas.
  • A pendulum. One weight on a frictionless hinge, let go from rest, for 10 seconds. One law: its total energy stays what it was.
  • A tumbling body. A flat body in empty space, spinning about its middle axis with a small nudge, so it flips over and over (the tennis racket effect), for 10 seconds. Four laws: its spin energy stays what it was; its spin energy never rises; its angular momentum keeps its size; its angular momentum stays the same vector.
  • The runs. Each scene with each of the five integrators MuJoCo 4.0.1 names (Euler, which is the default; implicitfast, which the documentation recommends; implicit; discrete; RK4) at four timesteps: 0.01, 0.005, 0.002 (the default) and 0.001 seconds. 60 files, each named by its sha256.

a published sentence, decided

The documentation’s own property holds exactly

The kinetic energy of a spinning free body is non-increasing in the absence of applied force. Steady spins about principal axes are conserved almost exactly; tumbling motion is mildly damped […]. Systems requiring long-horizon energy conservation of tumbling bodies should use the RK4 integrator.MuJoCo documentation, Computation, Numerical integration, “Gyroscopic derivatives for free bodies”, read 17 November 2026
  • What it says. Under the implicit and implicitfast integrators, the spin energy of a free body with no force on it never increases.
  • What was decided. On each of the 8 recorded runs it covers, the spin energy at every step is less than or equal to the step before, as fractions, with no tolerance. CERTIFIED The engine’s own test of the same property allows a small tolerance for rounding; on these runs none is needed.
  • What it does not say. That energy is kept. It is not: these are the runs that lose it. And it covers the bodies and settings recorded here, not every free body.
  • For contrast. Under Euler the same statement fails at step 1 of every run. REFUTED
written before running

What I expected, and where I was wrong

  • The falling body. I expected the position to fail at 1 cm within 2 seconds at the default timestep while the speed held, and RK4 to hold the position to rounding. That is what the bytes say.
  • The tumbling body. I expected the integrators that treat the spinning term implicitly to change the energy less than Euler does. They do not: at the default timestep they lose 1.83% where Euler gains 1.88%. What differs is the direction, which is the property the documentation states. My expectation came from an older version of the documentation.
how it was checked

Rules first, two deciders, planted files

  • The rules came first. corpus/physics-key/RULES.md was committed before the generator existed: the scenes, the laws, the six tolerances, the three verdict words, the exact form of every printed number, and what I expected. One law was added afterwards and is marked.
  • Reading the engine’s conventions. Before any verdict was read, three floating-point checks of my reading of the engine: a second run with the engine’s own energy and angular-momentum outputs switched on wrote identical bytes (60 of 60 runs); my angular momentum agrees with the engine’s to 7 × 10⁻¹⁵ of its size, and a deliberately wrong reading of the velocity frame disagrees by more than 2 times its size; my energy change agrees with the engine’s to 9 × 10⁻¹⁶.
  • Decider 1. instruments/physicskey/decide.js: JavaScript, whole numbers only. The same file runs in the browser on the instrument page.
  • Decider 2. instruments/physicskey/decide2.py: Python fractions, written from the rules alone by a session that never saw decider 1.
  • They agree. On all 70 files, every verdict, every first failing step and every printed digit is the same. The record is written only then. CERTIFIED
  • Planted files. 10 synthetic runs with a known answer: an exact parabola, a constant spin, one step nudged by two parts in a million, a single planted rise, a number that is not a number, a cut file, a file with one bit flipped, and three pendulum cases that exercise the cosine. Each was decided as planted by both.

check it yourself

How to re-run it

  • In a browser. The instrument page fetches any of the 60 files, checks its sha256 and decides it again in your tab with decider 1.
  • The record. node tools/record-physics-key.js --check runs both deciders on every file again and refuses on any difference from certs/physics-key.json. About two minutes; Python’s standard library and Node, nothing else.
  • The bytes. python instruments/physicskey/generate.py --check runs the engine again and compares every file’s sha256 with corpus/physics-key/runs/runs.json. It needs MuJoCo 4.0.1; on another platform the bytes may differ.
  • The scenes. corpus/physics-key/scenes: three short model files.
