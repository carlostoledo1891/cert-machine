Integer multiplication below n log n: two Lean proofs, replayed by a second party

Two groups have published Lean proofs that two n-bit integers can be multiplied in fewer than n log n steps on a Turing machine, a result OpenAI announced in a manuscript: Piaoyang Cui, Yiming Liu and David Woodruff (Google and CMU), and Dan Brown and Kenny Daniel (jdb19937 and platypii on GitHub, a community project). The mathematics and the proofs are theirs. With Claude (Anthropic’s AI model), I rebuilt both from source on a rented machine, under questions written down before the run. The Google/CMU proof is accepted by three independent checkers. The community proof builds and proves its own statement, but no second checker finished on it: each ran out of memory. I also proved, in Lean, that the community proof gives the Google/CMU statement.

What is still open

  • The community proof has no second kernel. nanoda was stopped on memory twice (114,017 MiB of 122,070 the first time, 114.8 GiB the second). Lean’s own kernel, replaying the whole closure afresh, was stopped at 115.2 GiB after 6 h 42 min. A third checker, con-leche, alone on the machine, stayed under 10 GiB for 54 minutes, then grew by more than 10 GiB a minute and was stopped at 100.7 GiB. Nothing rejected the proof. The cause of the memory growth was not found.
  • Some planted statements got no verdict. 5 of the 15 statements planted against the Google/CMU proof got no verdict from the intake: its check ran into the elaborator’s recursion limit under the 2^46 power. Every one that changes the saving is among them. The sentence written before the run is met only as amended afterwards. What those statements were meant to show comes from two checks added after the run.
  • The theorem joining the two statements is partial. It goes one way, was accepted by one kernel, and rebuilds the community machine instead of opening the one inside their theorem.
  • The statements were read, not decided. Neither formal statement is its paper’s theorem as written: both are about Mathlib’s multi-stack machine, and the step to a multitape machine is a classical argument that neither release formalizes. That the statements say what the papers say is a reading, made twice.
  • A third proof was not audited. A third Lean development of the same result exists (Prove2Me, prove2me/formalpedia). It was not audited here, and who was first is not decided here.
  • Nothing here is about practical speed. The saving reaches a factor of 2 only at sizes no computer will hold, the constants are not given, and the fast algorithm itself is not executable at any feasible size.
  • The comparator runs are not the planned ones. They used the release’s own statement and configuration for Google/CMU and a challenge module written here for the community proof, not a statement typed from the paper as the plan said. Comparator’s sandbox ran in best-effort mode on this machine.
  • The source is not judged. OpenAI’s manuscript, which both proofs point to, is not judged here.
the claim

Below n log n, by a factor that grows very slowly

Since 2021 the fastest known way to multiply two n-bit integers on a Turing machine has taken about n log n steps (Harvey and van der Hoeven), and many expected that to be the end. The new claim is a bound of n·(log n)^(1 − κ) for a fixed κ > 0: n log n divided by (log n)^κ. The three public numbers for κ are OpenAI’s 2^-182, Google/CMU’s 2^-46, and the community’s 4222422970/2^46 (about 2^-14.02). A larger κ is a stronger theorem.

Both proofs audited here state the result on Mathlib’s multi-stack Turing machine. A third Lean development, on Prove2Me, uses a model of its own and was not audited.

1 · the Google/CMU proof

Accepted by three independent checkers

  • Built from source. The release at its pinned commit, with its own Lean 4.33.1: 10,447 build steps, no error. The headline theorem SubNLogN.Assembly.intMul_challenge has the type SubNLogN.Challenge.IntMulSubNLogN, the proposition of the release’s 80-line statement file, and rests on propext, Quot.sound, Classical.choice and nothing else.
  • Lean’s kernel. It checked every module of the release as it was built. Afterwards a fresh replay of the whole proof, Mathlib included, also passed (1 h 24 min).
  • nanoda. An independent kernel written in Rust: it accepts all 124,416 declarations of the exported proof.
  • con-leche. A third checker, run in its default mode on the same export: accepted. Its count of declarations (120,324) is not nanoda’s; the two tools count differently and I did not reconcile them.
  • The authors’ own check, repeated. The release ships a configuration for Comparator, the tool that checks a proof against a separately written statement. Run here on another machine, it accepts under both of its kernels. This repeats the authors’ check; it is not a different kind of evidence. Comparator’s first attempt here ended in a stack overflow inside nanoda, with the nanoda build I had made for the intake; the run that counts used the nanoda commit the release names.
  • One checker gave no verdict. lean4lean, a kernel written in Lean, stopped at its own recursion limit after 30 min on the fresh replay. That is its limit, not a rejection.
  • The word. CERTIFIED with one amendment, stated under “planted errors” below: five of my planted statements got no verdict, so the sentence I wrote before the run is met only in an amended reading.
2 · the community proof

Proves its statement; no second kernel finished

  • What holds. The release at its pinned commit builds from source with its own Lean 4.34.1: 14,288 build steps, no error, so Lean’s kernel accepted every module. The theorem IntegerMultBounds.MathlibTM2.mulInTime proves IntegerMultBounds.MathlibTM2.MulInTime (4222422970 / 2 ^ 46) on the three standard axioms. All 13 of my planted statements came out as predicted, and the three planted wrong proofs were caught.
  • What did not finish: nanoda. The second kernel I had named before the run was stopped on memory twice. The first time the run’s own watchdog stopped it, 76 minutes in, while a tool build of mine shared the machine: how much of that memory was mine cannot be separated from the log. I ran the same step again by hand, from the same binary, configuration and export. It stayed small for about 70 minutes, then passed 100 GiB within ten, and was stopped at 114.8 GiB.
  • What did not finish: Lean’s own kernel, afresh. A fresh replay of the whole proof by Lean’s kernel stayed under 12 GiB for hours, then grew to 115.2 GiB in under half an hour; I stopped it after 6 h 42 min. The machine’s limit was 119 GiB.
  • What did not finish: con-leche. A third checker, run on the same export as the last optional step, this time alone on the machine. It stayed under 10 GiB for 54 minutes, then grew by more than 10 GiB a minute, and my guard stopped it at 100.7 GiB. It printed nothing.
  • Comparator. The release ships no statement file for Comparator, so I wrote one. With the saving raised by one unit in its numerator, Comparator refuses: the statements do not match. On the true statement it gave no verdict: it was still running after five hours when my memory guard stopped it, and I did not run it again.
  • The same pattern in three checkers. Memory grew without bound after a long quiet stretch in every run whose input contains this proof: nanoda on the proof, nanoda on the joined theorem below, Lean’s kernel on the fresh replay, and con-leche. The three checkers are written independently of each other. During the ordinary build, Lean’s kernel checked the same modules within the same memory. I did not find the cause. One guess, a very large number literal, was tested and not supported.
  • The word. REFUSED on resources: a check did not give its positive answer, and the reason is memory. Nothing rejected the proof. A machine with more memory, or the cause found, would settle it; I did not rent a larger one.
3 · the two statements

Read twice, then joined by a theorem

  • Two readings. Before the runs, each statement file was read against its paper twice, by readers who did not see each other’s notes (Claude sessions, under my direction). They agree. This is a reading, kept apart from the verdicts.
  • What the readings found. Neither proposition is its paper’s theorem as written. Both are about Mathlib’s multi-stack machine (FinTM2), on least-significant-bit-first words, with time measured in the total encoded length. The papers speak of a multitape machine. The step between the two is a classical simulation, argued in prose in both releases and formalized in neither; the Google/CMU paper says so.
  • The differences between the two. The input is written differently (a separator symbol against tagged halves), the bound is written differently (an inequality in whole numbers against big-O over the reals), the Google/CMU statement asks every stack alphabet to be finite and the community statement does not, and the savings differ.
  • The encodings, on small pairs. Each release’s encoder was evaluated in Lean against an encoder of mine on 328 pairs of numbers: all agree.
  • The joining theorem. IntMulBridge.community_proves_challenge : SubNLogN.Challenge.IntMulSubNLogN. The community proof gives the Google/CMU statement, as the Google/CMU statement file words it: compiled in the community release’s environment (Lean 4.34.1), importing the community’s theorem and a byte-identical copy of the Google/CMU statement file. It rests on the three standard axioms and was accepted by one kernel, Lean’s; nanoda on its export was stopped on memory, like the proof it imports. Under the words fixed before the run that is not CERTIFIED, which asks for two kernels.
  • Finite alphabets. To supply the clause the community statement lacks, one module rebuilds the community machine along the community’s own lemmas and carries the finiteness through. That an unreachable part of an alphabet cannot matter was already shown for this statement by ywz65 on 15 October 2026, with a kernel-checked lemma on definitions extracted from Mathlib; the module here is inside a real Mathlib import and specific to this machine.
What the joining theorem does not say
  • One direction only. That the Google/CMU proof gives the community statement at κ = 2^-46 is a written argument (second reading, section 3); it is not built. It cannot give the community theorem itself, whose saving is larger.
  • The finite-alphabets module rebuilds the community machine through the community’s own lemmas. It is not proved equal to the machine hidden inside their existential theorem, which cannot be taken out and asked.
  • The two releases pin different Lean and Mathlib versions. The statement proved here is the Google/CMU statement file compiled under the community’s versions: the same bytes, and four Mathlib files diffed at both pins (no definition differs); the definitions underneath (asymptotics, real powers, logarithms) were not diffed.
  • One kernel. Lean’s kernel accepted the five modules; nanoda on the export was stopped on memory. The theorem imports the community proof, so it inherits that proof’s missing second kernel.
Its four parts
  • IntMulBridge.natForm_iff_bigOForm: for one time function, the Google/CMU inequality in ℕ and the community’s =O form at κ = 2^-46 are equivalent.
  • IntMulBridge.community_to_natForm: the community’s bound at its κ gives the Google/CMU inequality.
  • IntMulEnc.reencode: a machine for the tagged input encoding, with finite alphabets, gives one for the separator encoding, with finite alphabets, at a linear extra cost.
  • IntMulBridge.mulInTimeFinite: the community machine rebuilt along the community’s own chain, carrying that every stack alphabet is finite.
4 · planted errors

What would have caught a wrong result

  • Planted statements. For each proof I wrote variants of its statement before the run, some reworded and some changed, each with a prediction: the proof must still prove a rewording and must fail to prove a change.
  • Google/CMU: 10 of 15 got a verdict. All 10 came out as predicted or, where I had made no prediction, were refused. The other 5 got no verdict: my check tried to unfold the 2^46 power and ran into Lean’s recursion limit. They include every variant that changes the saving, so as run, this check did not show that it tells κ = 2^-46 from κ = 2^-45.
  • The amendment. Before the run I wrote that every prediction would be met among the statements that reached the check. Those 5 reached it and got no verdict, so that sentence is not met as written. I now read it as “among the statements that got a verdict”, and I say so here and in the record.
  • Two checks added afterwards. First, every planted statement of both proofs was compared with its statement as an expression, with nothing unfolded: all 20 changed statements differ. Second, Comparator on the release’s own nine controls: its exponent-45 control is refused because the statement does not match.
  • Community: 13 of 13 got a verdict. All as predicted, the variants of the saving included.
  • Planted wrong proofs. In each release’s theorem file: the proof replaced by sorry, the theorem proved by an added axiom, and one step decided by native_decide. All 6 are caught by the list of axioms. One of them, the added axiom, was also exported and shown to nanoda, which fails on it.

5 · the released programs

Exact on every input tried, and what they are

  • The C program. 55 products, from 4,096 to 100,000,000 bits, in both of its modes, zero and one included: every one equals the product computed by Python’s integers. The C program implements the companion O(n log n) multiplier, not the algorithm below n log n, by its own README. “Exact” is a fact about that program; its arithmetic is GMP’s.
  • The Python reference. 236 products, each equal to Python’s own, with the library’s counters recorded for each row to show which path ran: 82 ran the new transform on test parameters, 70 fell back to the two-dimensional transform, and 48 returned Python’s own product through the small-input shortcut.
  • What neither shows. Algorithm 1 at the theorem’s parameters is not executable at any feasible size, by the release’s own statement. Nothing here measures speed, and no timing is quoted: the rows ran while the machine was building.
6 · in plain numbers

What the theorem buys

  • Google/CMU. The bound is n·(log2 n)^(1 − 2^-46): n log n divided by (log2 n)^(2^-46). That divisor equals 2 exactly when log2 n = 2^(2^46), that is for inputs of 2^(2^(2^46)) bits, and is below 2 for every shorter input.
  • Community. The bound is O(n·(ln n)^(1 − κ)): n ln n divided by (ln n)^κ. That divisor equals 2 exactly when ln n = 2^(1/κ), with 1/κ a little over 16,665. The saving is 2^-14.02, rounded.
  • The constants. In both statements the constant in front and the length from which the bound holds are asserted to exist and are not given.
  • So. This is a theorem about the shape of a bound at sizes far beyond any computer. Its interest is that n log n is not optimal, not that anything multiplies faster today.

Community: no verdict.   • A9 · Q3, plan P5 · the theorem joining the two statements. Both closed, and more was built than planned: one theorem, IntMulBridge.community_proves_challenge, whose type is the Google/CMU statement, compiled in the community release’s environment with the community theorem imported and the Google/CMU statement file copied byte for byte. The authors report their exponent-45 control refused by comparator. Our intake’s exponent controls got no verdict, by another method (A1).
