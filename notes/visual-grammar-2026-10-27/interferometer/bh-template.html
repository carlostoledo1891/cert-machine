The black hole, as a set

The famous image of M87 is one sky picked from infinitely many that the data allow. This instrument draws the rest: what the picks share, what nothing in the data decides, the lid no sky can exceed, the sky that breaks the story, and the hypothesis every verdict leans on. Every number is read from the instrument’s records or computed in your tab from the 838 released visibilities.

1 · a photograph is a pick

One sky, chosen

This is one of eighteen skies fitted to the released visibilities of 21 April 2018: the one with the best closure-phase fit. The famous image is a pick of the same kind, made with a different prior. A pick is CHOSEN, so its frame is dotted, and its warm colours mean one thing only: how bright this pick is.

Its ring is smaller than the published 43 µas. Under-regularised fits do that; the record says so. The dotted circle is the published diameter, drawn for scale.

2 · eighteen picks

Six seeds, three phase weights, three sparsities

Every tile fits the data by the rule it was fitted with, and every tile is a different picture. Three of them dropped the LMT telescope and parked about 7 Jy in the field. The stated hypothesis allows at most 2 Jy, so those three are outside the set: each carries a red ×, because the excess flux is the object that breaks the rule.

3 · agreement is still a pick

Where the picks agree, and where they don’t

Centred on their own flux, the fifteen picks that keep the hypothesis agree on brightness and disagree in blue stipple. It is tempting to call the warm part “what the data say”. It is not: eighteen samples are not the set, so their agreement is CHOSEN too. Only a certificate speaks for every sky.

4 · where

Nothing here says where M87 is

Slide a sky across the field and every visibility amplitude stays the same; so does every closure phase. Nothing in these files can locate the source. Left where their fits put them, the picks’ centres scatter by up to …. The cross at the centre is a convention, not a measurement: VOID, hatched.

5 · the lid

The most flux any allowed sky can hold

For every sky the data allow, at most this much flux fits inside a disk of radius r around the centre. The bound comes from a dual certificate: one inequality that must hold at every point of the field. It was checked on a fine grid with a margin, in floating point, so the lid is dashed: COMPUTED. Check that inequality with interval arithmetic and it turns solid. That is the paving instrument’s job, applied to the sky.

Each lid is true only under its own hypothesis, so every value is set in italic and names it. Change the dial above the sky and watch which lids survive: a lid carries over to any hypothesis that is stricter on every row, and to nothing else.

6 · the witness

A bright centre the amplitudes allow

This sky breaks none of the amplitude ceilings it was built against, and puts 0.140 Jy within 6 µas of the centre: 28% of its flux, in the middle of where the shadow should be. It is the exhibited counterexample to “the amplitudes alone force a dark centre”, so it is red. It looks nothing like a black hole, which is the point. Whatever darkens the centre in the famous image, it is not the amplitudes alone.

It is also the lower end of the lid at r = 6: no ceiling can sit below a sky that exists. Your tab reads an 8-bit copy of it, so it widens every visibility by the rounding of the copy before it judges; no row breaks beyond that rounding.

7 · what this tab found · 2026-10-27

The picks are not inside their own set

Measuring every pick against all 838 one-sided ceilings…

The current instrument page says “every sky drawn here is one the data allow”. The picks were admitted by a two-sided χ² fit; the lid and the witness use “every row within its ceiling”. One word, two admission rules: a READING hole, on our own page. The one-sided rule is also very strict: real data have outliers, and every row must hold. What needs fixing is the hypothesis, an outlier allowance or a systematic error budget, not the black hole. The dial lets you try both.

8 · your sky, your hypothesis

Draw a sky and ask the data

Build a sky from a ring and a central spot, or load one of the records. Your tab computes all 838 visibilities and checks each against its ceiling under the hypothesis on the dial. A row that breaks is drawn as a red × on the u–v plane: the datum that refutes your sky. The lid rail shows how much flux your sky puts inside each disk.
