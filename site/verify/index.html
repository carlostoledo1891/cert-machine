how it works

From a claim to a certificate

The product in four steps. An audit is the whole path for one published claim; a grader states a task family once and runs steps 3 and 4 on every answer a model gives.

  • 1 · The claim. What a model, a paper or a leaderboard said, with the files behind it: a configuration, a table, a proof, an answer. Each file is pinned by its SHA-256 before anything is read, so the verdict names exactly what it decided.
  • 2 · Stated. claimbox turns the claim’s words into an object a machine can check, prints it back in plain words, and refuses what does not come down to finitely many exact facts, naming why. A statement is read against the claim before anything is decided, and the reading is kept apart from the decision.
  • 3 · Decided. Exact rational arithmetic, interval enclosures rounded outward, or a second proof kernel, in code written apart from the claimant’s. No floating-point number takes part in a decision, and the decider is attacked with planted forgeries before it is trusted.
  • 4 · The certificate. One of three words. CERTIFIED, with a falsifier that would break it. REFUTED, with the object that breaks the claim. REFUSED, with the reason and what would make it decidable. Each re-runs with one command, without this engine.
  • An audit. For labs, AI-for-science companies and registries: a published claim goes in at step 1, and a report comes out listing every gap first, then every finite part decided, every formal statement read against the claim.
  • A grader. For RL and evaluation teams: a task family is stated once at step 2, then every model answer is decided at step 3 and the certificate is the reward. It must refuse planted wrong answers before it grades anything, so a policy cannot learn to fool it.

for researchers

Share the problem without giving it away

Researchers now hold back directions for fear that a draft pasted into a frontier model comes back as someone else’s paper; Terence Tao said as much in his Math 2.0 talk. A seal lets you prove you held the exact bytes by a date and reveal nothing until you choose to. Seal before anything goes into a cloud model.

1 · seal

The bytes, committed

A deterministic archive of the file or folder and a 32-byte salt stay on your machine. One digest comes out: SHA-256 of the salt and the archive’s hash, nothing a reader can invert or guess. The tool is one Python file in the repository: instruments/seal/seal.py.

2 · anchor

The date, from outside

The digest goes to OpenTimestamps (a Bitcoin-attested date) and to a public registry whose commit date is the second anchor. Neither anchor is ours to move.

3 · open

The reveal, when you choose

Hand over the archive and the salt. Anyone re-derives the digest with one line of shell, without this engine, and reads the anchors’ dates. Sealed file by file, one file can be shown while the rest stay sealed: the manifest names no path and no salt.

4 · decide

Three words

CERTIFIED: the bytes re-derive the digest and an anchor confirms the date. REFUTED: they do not, and the input that differs is named. REFUSED: a part is missing or no anchor is confirmed yet, with what would decide it. An opened seal can go straight into Statement Check or an audit.

What a seal does not say. It proves possession of exact bytes by a date. It does not say who first had the idea, that the idea is correct, or anything about credit; a short statement can be guessed and hashed, so seal the whole draft, and a lost salt is an unopenable seal. The commands, the shell one-liner and the battery of forgeries: the Seal page; the tool: instruments/seal on GitHub.

the limits

What it refuses

Claims that are not finite

An asymptotic statement, a “for all n”, or a proof written in prose is refused with the reason, and the refusal says what would make it decidable.

Your code

Nothing a claimant wrote is run. The decision comes from code written apart from it, so a bug cannot certify itself.

Meaning, passed off as a decision

A kernel cannot say what a statement means. Where a verdict rests on reading a statement, the report says so and keeps the reading apart from what was decided.

A verdict it cannot back

Absence of proof is never turned into CERTIFIED or REFUTED. Not peer-reviewed; every page ships its own falsifiers.

What it is not: a proof of inference. A zero-knowledge proof that a committed model produced an answer (Anthropic’s FlashProve, October 2026) says which model ran. Verify says whether the answer is right. One is the layer below the other; they compose, and neither replaces the other.
