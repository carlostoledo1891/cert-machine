WARRANT, the visual layer of evidence

An ordinary chart draws a proof, a floating-point estimate, a guess and a gap with the same line. In WARRANT each has its own stroke, a verdict is a word, and the build refuses a figure that claims more than its record.

The segments’ claim, with a tolerance: every straight segment is within ε = of √x on [0, 5/4]. ε is a choice: type it, or drag its handle along the ruler, in steps of 1/64. The solid tick is where the answer changes, decided once in exact rationals; a drag moves only ε, the segments raised by ε (dotted, above them), the × and the words.

It holds from ε = 1/8 up: the largest gap in the window is at x = 1/16, where the segment reads 1/8 and √x is 1/4. Below 1/8 a point breaks it: the hero’s own x = 1/2 while it can, then x = 1/16.

Four strokes, added one at a time

The weakest on the path wins

The standings are ordered, and a mark takes the weakest standing on its way to the pixel: one float makes it computed, one choice makes it chosen, one refusal leaves the void. Standing is not a confidence: a value known to many decimals can be undecided.

A float anywhere on the path: the mark is computed.
A choice anywhere on the path: chosen. A straight line between two exact knots is one of many.
A refusal anywhere on the path: nothing is drawn, and the void says so.

A verdict is a word in one shape, everywhere. A stroke never carries a verdict, so a refutation proved here is drawn solid.

A verdict over a chosen input names the input, in italic: it holds given that choice, and no other.

An empty set is ∅ and a word, never a zero-height bar. The red × is kept for one thing, an exhibited counterexample beside the words that name it: on this page, the hero’s.

how to show data · Charts

Charts you already draw

From here down, the ways this site shows data, by kind: charts, maps, routes and time, fields and images, systems, and text. Each is drawn from one of the instruments, twice where an ordinary version exists, and each ends with the rule that draws it, so the rule can be applied to data of your own.

Five charts of the kinds people draw every day, each twice: as a plotting library draws it, and in WARRANT. Two read a real record; one is computed from its equations as this page is built; two are illustrations, their numbers invented and said so on the figure. The arithmetic on an invention is still exact, so its ties, gaps and counts are decided given it; and no invention carries a red ×, which would be a forged counterexample.

1 · levels with intervals, by category · from a record

A design wave

The usual chart draws a trend with error bars. The spread is two choices, the block and the family: the picked 100-year wave runs from 13.05 m (monthly) to 17.38 m (weekly). The error bars are the only float on the page, and on annual maxima one family has no certified fit, so the choice between them is refused, not drawn.

The rule: Draw each certified level as a knot, an interval from a float method as a dashed whisker, and a choice the data cannot settle as a void with its reason.

2 · a family of curves on log axes · built from its equations

Moody’s chart

The usual chart draws an exact law and a 1939 fit in one ink, and carries a pen across the transition. Here 64/Re is solid, Colebrook’s curves are dashed, each names the roughness it was drawn given, and from Re 2300 to 4000, where no correlation holds, nothing is drawn.

The rule: Draw an exact law solid and a fit solved in floating point dashed, name each curve by the input it was drawn given, and leave a range where no correlation holds as a void, never bridged.

3 · a ranked bar chart · an illustration

A leaderboard

The usual chart ranks six models one to six. First place is a tie broken by name; fourth is a score someone reported and nobody re-counted; the zero at the bottom is a run that never happened.

The rule: Draw counts solid, a reported number dashed until it is re-counted, an order between equal values as a dotted choice, and a run that never happened as a void with NEEDS DATA, never a zero.

4 · counts over time with a nowcast · an illustration

An epidemic curve

The usual chart shows a dip and a fall. The dip is two days nobody reported, drawn as zero; the fall is a week still being counted. Here the gap is a void, the mean stops where the data stop, and the last week is a nowcast, dashed.

The rule: Draw counts received solid, unreported days as a void, a mean whose window was picked dotted and stopped at every gap, and the days still being counted as a dashed nowcast.

5 · an ordered field of counts with curves over it · from a record

Counts under a curve

On a linear ramp only the dense core of the hours shows, and the hours a 20-year contour is scored on, a few single hours at the edge, vanish. Here every hour shows in a stated class, each contour carries its exact count outside, and the edge a test adds to close an open file is dotted.

The rule: Draw counts as an ordered field in stated classes so a single count shows, draw each curve as its source lists it, and draw any edge the scorer adds to close it dotted, as a choice the count holds given. See it live →

Maps

Cells, regions, sites, reaches and fields: what a map says about each place, and what it may not.

1 · a map of cells · from a record

A map of cells

The usual map fills every cell. A cell where no family could be decided gets a zero, lands in the lowest shade, and reads as the calmest sea on the coast. Here a refused cell keeps its full square as a void with its reason, and the shades are only the cells the ledger decides.

The rule: Shade a cell only where its value is decided, and draw a refused cell as a void the size of a cell, with its reason in words, never as zero. See it live →

2 · a claim’s region on a map · from a record

A region named in words

The usual map puts a sentence over a picture and lets the eye agree. A region named in words has no edge until someone reads one in. Here the box is drawn dotted as the reading it is, the count inside it decides the claim, and the readings one cell away that change the verdict are drawn too.

The rule: Draw a region that a sentence names as a dotted box, the reading it is, decide the claim inside it, and show the readings one step away when they change the verdict. See it live →

3 · sites as points with decided words · from a record

Sites with a word each

The usual map prints the forecast’s one number at each site, and 4 of the nine read under the limit. Read against the band the forecast is known to miss by, only 1 is cleared and 2 are blocked; the other 6 are undecided, 3 of them sites whose one number looked fine. Here each site carries its decided word, its shape and the band it was decided on.

The rule: Mark each site with the word its band decides against the limit, cleared, blocked or undecided, in a shape as well as a word, and print the band it was decided on, never the forecast’s one number alone. See it live →

4 · a region whose edges are decided · from a record

A reach drawn as a band

The usual ring is one line from one glide ratio and one forecast wind, and everything inside it looks reachable. Here the inputs are ranges: the two solid edges are decided, the panel’s ring runs dashed between them, and 69 of the 108 airfields inside it are undecided, not reachable.

The rule: Draw a reach as the two edges the inputs decide, solid, with a halo where the model was never validated, the one-value ring dashed between them, and each place inside marked with its own verdict. See it live →

5 · a field drawn from a model · from a record

A model’s field

The usual map draws a model’s crests like a photograph, edge to edge, and every beach alike. Here the crests are dashed and the figure says it is a picture of the model; they stop where the model is refused, the beaches show where the model was checked and where it was only assumed, and the three where the check says it reads low are named.

The rule: Draw a model’s field dashed and say once that it is a picture of the model, leave a void where the model is refused, and mark each place by how far the model was checked there, naming the places where the check says it reads low. See it live →

how to show data · Routes and time

Routes and time

Data that moves: a flight along its route, a week of six-hour windows, a forecast and the sea that came after it, a star dimming as a planet crosses it, a price stepping as its rate moves. Each is read from an instrument's own record, and each is drawn so the stretch nobody saw, the hours nobody can decide and the claim nobody has checked yet look like what they are.

1 · a route on a map · built from its equations

A route, and where a decision about it stops

The usual map draws a flight as one line. It bridges 5 min 42 s without reports as if they were flown, and it cannot show where along the route a claim about the flight stops being decided. Here the track breaks where the reports stop, the stretch where the reserve is decided to hold is solid, and the rest is a void with its reason.

The rule: Draw a route from the reports that were received, break it wherever they stop, and mark the point where a decision about the route changes, with a solid line where it is decided and a void where it is not. See it live →

2 · a board of verdicts over time · from a record

A week of windows

A go/no-go board fills the cells that are go and leaves the rest blank, so blocked, cannot tell and not forecast that far look alike. Here each has its own shape and its word, and the hours past the forecast are one void with its reason.

The rule: Give each verdict on a board its own shape and its word (cleared filled, blocked outlined, undecided hatched), and draw the part with nothing to decide on as one void with its reason, never as a blank. See it live →

3 · a timeline over a forecast · from a record

An operating window

The usual chart draws the forecast as if it were the sea, with one bar for when the job can start. The forecast is a claim: here it is dashed, the limit it is decided against is solid, and below them each start time carries its verdict, cleared where the band's top edge stays under the limit for the whole job and a void where the band straddles it or the job outruns the forecast.

The rule: Draw a forecast dashed, the limit it is decided against solid, and the window the decision opens as a span with its word; where the forecast straddles the limit, draw the void, not a guess. See it live →

4 · a forecast scored against what happened · from a record

A forecast, graded

The usual verification plot lays forecasts and observations side by side and leaves the judging to the eye. Here each forecast is a claim, a miss is drawn as the gap between the claim and the sea, and the count of hits sits beside what each forecast claimed. The grade admits or prunes a forecaster; it never decides a window.

The rule: Draw a forecast's band beside what was later observed, draw each miss as the gap between them, and print the count of hits against the coverage the forecast claimed. See it live →

5 · measurements over time, with what they admit · from a record

A light curve, and the stars it admits

The usual chart draws one fitted curve through the points, solid, and quotes its radius to four decimals. Other stars fit the same points. Here each measurement is drawn as the allowance it is judged with, the fit is dashed, two very different stars that both pass through every allowance are dotted, and the radius is the interval the data decide.

The rule: Draw each measurement as the allowance it is judged with, a fitted model dashed, the extreme members the data admit dotted, and print the decided range in words, never one fit as the answer. See it live →

6 · a step function · from a record

A price under a rule table

The usual chart joins the prices into a sloping line and shows the printed one a little off it, which reads as rounding. The rule truncates, so the price moves in treads, and the printed rate stands for a whole box of rates. Here the treads are drawn flat, the box is marked, and the printed price sits on none of them: the gap is a decided fact, not noise.

The rule: Draw a value a rule truncates or rounds as the flat treads it can take, never as a slope between them, mark the box of inputs a printed figure stands for, and say in words whether the printed output sits on a tread. See it live →

Fields, images and physics

A field from a model, an image as the set of its fits, a shape from its chords, an error budget and a calibration band: five ways to draw physical data, each from one of the instruments’ own records, first as a plotting library draws it and then in WARRANT.

1 · a contour field from a model · from a record

A field from a model

The usual contour plot of a model’s field looks like a measurement, with speeds printed on it. Here the contours are dotted, because the profiles behind them were picked to be legible and only the scalings are the paper’s; the one solid mark is the core box, whose shrinking rates are exact.

The rule: Draw a field computed from a model dotted, as a picture of one chosen member, name its chosen inputs in italic, and draw solid only what the model decides, here the box its exact exponents fix. See it live →

2 · an image as the set of skies fitted to the data · from a record

An image as a set of fits

The usual image is one sky out of the many that fit the same visibilities, and a picture cannot say which of its features the data force. Here the five fitted skies are drawn at once: ink where all five are bright, texture where only some are.

The rule: Draw an image fitted to data as the set of its fits: ink where every fit agrees, texture where they differ (denser where more agree), edges in the stroke of how the fits were computed, and the level and the beam named as choices. See it live →

3 · a silhouette set from chords · from a record

A shape from its chords

The usual sketch joins the chord ends into an outline that looks like the body. That outline is not concave, so no convex body has it. Here the body is the set of shapes the chords allow, and the stations that saw nothing, which set its ceiling, are drawn as observations.

The rule: Draw a shape known only through cuts as the set of shapes the cuts allow: ink where every allowed shape covers, texture where some do, each cut and each empty probe as an observation, and a probe that returned nothing as a void with its reason. See it live →

4 · an error budget as nested boxes · built from its equations

An error budget

The usual error bar is one cross of symmetric whiskers. It hides that the error is a box in two dimensions, and that its size rests on three different things: the paper’s numbers, the numbers the paper leaves out, and a lag the page chose.

The rule: Draw an error budget as nested enclosures, one per premise, innermost first: solid where it rests on the source’s literals or on boxes around what the source leaves out, dotted where it adds a chosen scenario, and name the chosen inputs in italic. See it live →

5 · a band of every admissible fit around the published line · from a record

A calibration and its set

The usual calibration plot draws one fitted curve through the standards and reads a sample back to a number with a narrow ±. The fit is one member of a set: assuming only that more analyte means more signal, the standards allow every curve in the band, and the reading is anywhere between the two standards that bracket it.

The rule: Draw a fitted curve dotted inside the band of every curve the data and the stated assumption allow, read a measurement back through the band as an extent, and say in words any point the axis cannot place. See it live →

Geometry, networks and systems

Structures rather than series: a wiring diagram, a point in a simplex, an embedding with its tree, a ladder of enclosures against a wall, a matrix of verdicts, and an evolution experiment’s lines of descent. Each is read from an instrument’s own record and drawn twice. In a structure the standing sits on the parts: an edge, a port, a window, a cell, a winner.

1 · a wiring diagram · from a record

A wiring diagram

The usual diagram draws every connection as the same arrow, so it cannot show the one rule that matters: a value that came from floating point may prune, never decide. Here a wire is drawn in the stroke of what it carries, a port that decides is a square, a verdict port that never fired stays on the drawing, and the connection the engine refuses is a void with its reason.

The rule: Draw each wire in the stroke of what its source emits (solid for exact, dashed for float), draw a deciding port as a square, keep every verdict port on the drawing whether it fired or not, and put a refused connection as a hatched void with the reason, never as a line. See it live →

2 · a point in a simplex · from a record

An attention row as a point

Weights that sum to one are a point in a simplex, and a bar chart throws that away: thirty-one bars at one temperature say nothing about where the row is heading. Drawn as a point, the row’s path is visible, and the grammar shows how little of it is decided: nine solid knots near the centre, and a long dashed float trail to the corner.

The rule: Draw a composition that sums to one as a point in a simplex (restricted to a face you name in italic), each exact value a solid knot, a float path dashed, and magnify a short decided stretch in a ring rather than stretching the axes. See it live →

3 · an embedding with decided edges · from a record

An embedding with a tree

The usual scatter gives an eigendecomposition’s picture axes, as if its coordinates were measured, and draws every edge of the tree alike. Here the positions are said once to be a chosen picture, an edge in every minimum tree is solid, and an edge that ties with another of exactly the same length is dotted, its alternative drawn faintly beside it.

The rule: Draw a layout placed by a float solver without measurement axes and say once that it is a chosen picture; draw an edge that the numbers force solid, an edge picked among exact ties dotted with its length, and its tied alternatives as faint guides. See it live →

4 · a ladder of enclosures · from a record

Records against a wall

The usual chart puts every published ratio left of the wall, so every record looks accepted. Each norm was printed rounded to a whole number, so each ratio is really a window; drawn as windows, nine clear the wall and one crosses it, and its verdict is a void, open until someone publishes the norm in full.

The rule: Draw each value as the window its rounding leaves (a solid bracket) beside the printed figure (a dashed tick), put the threshold as a solid line, give each row its verdict word, and leave a hatched void where the window straddles the threshold. See it live →

5 · a matrix of verdict words · from a record

A matrix of verdicts

The usual grid fills a cell for right and leaves it empty for wrong, so a wrong verdict, a hedge and an answer that never came all look the same. Here each cell holds the word the model said, underlined dashed as a claim, beside the exact answer; a missing answer is a void, and the row order, a choice made to group the answers, is drawn dotted.

The rule: Put the decided answer in the first column, write each claim’s own verdict word in its cell (underlined dashed, marked ≠ where it differs), leave a hatched void where no answer came, and when you sort the rows keep their labels and draw the order dotted. See it live →

6 · arms over generations, with lines of descent · from a record

Lines of descent

The usual chart is a race of three curves from one start. Here the start is a program someone wrote, drawn dotted; each curve carries the births its winner descends from, read from the parent pointers; and a winner picked by birth order among births tied at the top score is ringed dotted, with how many it was picked from.

The rule: Draw each arm’s best-so-far as a solid step line told apart by shape and weight, put the hand-made starting point as a dotted line named in italic, mark the winner’s ancestors as knots from the record’s parent pointers, and ring a winner that was picked from a tie dotted, saying from how many. models as premises

When a number rests on a model

The arithmetic can be exact while its equations never met the thing they describe. So a number that rests on a physical model names its premises, each with how far it was checked, and a conclusion is as checked as its weakest premise. Here is a wave limit on Swell, the beach app:

the ladder
The verification-and-validation ladder of Roache (1998), Oberkampf and Roy (2010) and ASME V&V 20 (2009), cut to four grades a reader can tell apart. Never a probability, never a confidence.
the same chain, as the page prints it
  1. validatedunverified
    The open sea and its band covered 96.5% of 21,259 satellite passes it had not seen; it claims 9 in 10.
  2. comparedunverified
    The island model beside a Boussinesq model at twelve beaches, 556 cases of a 1 m swell: r 0.44, the island 0.27 m higher on average.
  3. assumed
    Breaking at 0.55 times the depth (Thornton & Guza, 1982); no sandbars; the shallow profile from the nautical chart.

The arithmetic is exact; the weakest premise is assumed, and neither model was verified against an exact solution of its own equations.

The four grades, and a read step
  • validated — checked against observations of the thing itself, held out, with the number. On Swell: the open sea’s band, against satellite passes it had not seen.
  • compared — checked against a model that keeps more of the physics, with the number; not against the thing itself. On Swell: the island model, against a Boussinesq model at twelve beaches.
  • verified — the code reproduces an exact solution of its own equations; not measured against the thing itself. On Swell: none yet.
  • assumed — adopted from a source, chosen, or read; no machine here checked it. On Swell: breaking at 0.55 times the depth (Thornton & Guza, 1982), and no sandbars.

A read step — a proof step read and not run by a machine — gets no grade of its own: it is assumed. It never caps a verdict; it is listed in what the page rests on. δ₃ = 117/2192 is an example: a decided finite core, and three steps proved on paper and read.

The foundations: one law, six axioms
  • A1 · Soundness. Drawing maps a record to marks; reading maps marks back to the records that could have produced them. A page is sound when every record it draws is among them: no mark stronger than its record, in standing, extent or verdict word.
  • A2 · Completeness. Every row of the record a page draws from is a mark, or the empty set with a word.
  • A3 · Outward. A lower end rounds down and an upper end up, in digits and in pixels, at the resolution the figure declares.
  • A4 · A scale with a guide. Four standings onto five strokes, the legend derived from the scale; every channel it uses is monotone in it, or constant.
  • A5 · A typed value. A value carries its datum, its standing, its premises and its record’s address; the arithmetic demotes it and the renderer only reads it.
  • A6 · A frame is a page. In motion every frame obeys A1; a standing never tweens and a value never travels.

The four standings: how a number was made

  • DECIDED — solid stroke; plain text. An exact decision backs it: an interval enclosure, an exact rational comparison, a proof.
  • COMPUTED — dashed stroke 5 4 (1.5 2 under 1.5 px); a dashed underline in text. Floating point, a solver stopped at a tolerance, a fit, a forecast, a number someone printed. It may be right; nothing decided it.
  • CHOSEN — dotted stroke 1 5 with round caps (1 3 under 1.5 px); a dotted underline in text. One member of a set the data admit, picked by something that is not the data: a tie-break, a default, a window, an interpolation.
  • REFUSED — no stroke: a hatched void at the size the value would have had, with ∅ and a word. The instrument declined or the input is missing. Never bridged, never averaged, never drawn as zero.

A mark takes the weakest standing on its path to the pixel: one float makes it computed, one choice makes it chosen, one refusal leaves the void. Standing is not a confidence. The stroke carries standing only; which series a line belongs to is carried by weight, opacity or a marker, never by a dash. A guide (a ruler, a leader) is dashed 2 3 and says nothing.

Verdicts are words, never strokes

  • CERTIFIED — decided true by an exact check.
  • REFUTED — decided false, with the object that breaks the claim.
  • REFUSED — the instrument declined, with the reason.
  • PARTIAL — one part certified, another refused.
  • NEEDS DATA — refused because the bytes that would decide it are missing; the bytes are named.
  • HOLDS · DOES NOT HOLD · UNDECIDED — a check over an input someone chose, always said "given" that input, in italic.

An empty set is ∅ and a word, never a zero-height bar. The one red (--witness #e0574e) marks an exhibited counterexample, on the object, beside the words that name it, and nothing else; never on invented data.

Numbers that rest on a model

  • VALIDATED — checked against observations of the thing itself, held out, with the number.
  • COMPARED — checked against a model that keeps more of the physics, with the number; not against the thing itself.
  • VERIFIED — the code reproduces an exact solution of its own equations; not measured against the thing itself.
  • ASSUMED — adopted from a source, chosen, or read; no machine here checked it.

Each premise carries a grade; a conclusion is as checked as its weakest premise; verification (the code solves its equations) and validation (the equations describe the thing) are separate. A forecast is computed and dashed; a field drawn from a model is a picture of it, said once; an extent carried by a model not validated where it is drawn gains a halo.

The sixteen rules, and two rulings

  • Rule 1. Standing is not a verdict. A verdict is a word in a chip; what a mark rests on is its stroke or underline. They never share a channel, so a refutation proved here is drawn solid.
  • Rule 2. A pick is a member of its set, and the build checks it. A figure that draws one member of a set the data admit draws a real member, verified exactly when the page is built.
  • Rule 3. A tie is chosen. When the optimum is not unique, the one drawn was picked by a tie-break: dotted, and said to be one of several.
  • Rule 4. Dash is for standing only. Which series or model a line belongs to is carried by weight and opacity, never by a dash, in every figure.
  • Rule 5. The one accent. The red × marks an exhibited counterexample: an object that broke a claim made about its own record, with words. Never a verdict word, a region, a scalar comparison or a deduction. When in doubt, ink.
  • Rule 6. Mood. A verdict that holds given a chosen input names it in italic, given h = 3/50; a hypothesis read but not machine-checked is named the same way.
  • Rule 7. An empty set is ∅. It gets ∅ and a word, never a zero-height bar or a zero.
  • Rule 8. Every printed number comes from its record, rounded outward. The build refuses when a record stops supporting its sentence. A printed bound rounds outward, never truncated: drawn here on the hero’s own enclosure.
  • Rule 9. A legend wherever a mark is. Every page that uses a stroke or underline of the grammar prints its key; tools/check-wiring.js refuses one that does not.
  • Rule 10. A page copied in is never edited. It changes only through its wrapper, by anchored replacements that each refuse unless their anchor occurs exactly once.
  • Rule 11. A number that rests on a model names its premises. In the order the number travelled, each with its grade and record, ending with the weakest.
  • Rule 12. A comparison is calibrated first. Before a comparison is trusted, the build re-derives it and it must reproduce the published one it stands in for.
  • Rule 13. The halo. An extent carried by a model not validated where it is drawn keeps its crisp edges and gains a blur outside them.
  • Rule 14. A forecast is computed and dashed, on canvas too. A canvas takes the same patterns: setLineDash is given G.claim(width).
  • Rule 15. A field drawn from a model is a picture of it. For crests or streamlines, the legend says so once.
  • Rule 16. Where the model reads low, “under the limit” is flagged. A flag, not a refusal: a refusal would remove an exact statement.
  • Ruling. A layout placed by a float solver is a chosen picture. The caption says so once; each mark keeps the standing of what it asserts, so an edge that exists is solid.
  • Ruling. A model’s answer is a claim someone made. It is computed, dashed, like a number someone printed; on the wiring instruments its wire is dashed.

Colour

  • Ink first. Every figure reads in greyscale; a colour comes with a shape and a word.
  • One colour, one meaning, in every project on the grammar, each registered with its date.
  • Colour never carries standing. The stroke does; nor does it stand in for a verdict word.
  • Four kinds only: the witness, identity, order, status.
  • Every pair passes the validator, on the figure’s own ground, for every simulated deficiency.
  • No yellow, no orange.
  • A new colour needs the report and an approval, recorded in the rulebook.
