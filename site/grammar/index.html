The visual grammar: what each mark rests on

WARRANT is the grammar the figures, tables and numbers on this site are drawn in. The stroke of every line and the underline of every number say what it rests on: an exact decision, floating-point arithmetic, a choice, or nothing at all. A verdict is always a word. It covers the instruments and the pages read most; every other report takes it when it is next edited, and material already submitted elsewhere stays exactly as submitted. Below: the four marks, drawn live by the module; the sixteen rules, each with the finding on one of these pages that taught it; the colour law; the premise ladder for numbers that rest on a model; how the build enforces all of it; and how to use it in figures of your own.

what it is

A stroke says what the number behind it rests on

A chart and a table both assert things, and neither usually says which of its marks the data forced and which the renderer, the solver or the tolerance chose. A fitted curve drawn solid looks exactly like a proved bound drawn solid. This grammar gives every mark one of four standings, and each standing one stroke:

  • DECIDED — solid; in text, plain. An exact decision backs it: an interval enclosure, an exact rational comparison, a proof.
  • COMPUTED — dashed, 5 4; in text, a dashed underline. Floating-point arithmetic, a solver stopped at a tolerance, a fit, a forecast, or a number someone printed: it may well be right, and nothing decided it.
  • CHOSEN — dotted, 1 5 with round caps; in text, a dotted underline. One member of a set the data admit, picked by something that is not the data: a tie-break, a default, a seed, a prior, an interpolation.
  • REFUSED — no mark at all. The instrument declined, or the input is missing; the void is drawn, hatched, and in text it reads refused. It is never interpolated across, never averaged, never a zero.

A mark takes the weakest standing on its path to the pixel: a knot computed exactly is solid, and the line drawn between two such knots is dotted, because a straight line is one curve of many through them. Standing is not a verdict. CERTIFIED, REFUTED and REFUSED are what an instrument concluded about a claim; they are said in words and chips, never in a stroke, and a refutation proved here is drawn solid. Nor is standing a confidence: a value known to many decimals can still be undecided, and a decided value can have a wide bracket, which is drawn as its own extent.

A premise chain, with its ladder

A number that rests on a model names what it rests on, in the order the number travelled, each premise with its grade: validated · compared · verified · assumed (the ladder is explained below). This is the chain behind a wave limit at a beach on Swell, built here from the records Swell reads:

  1. validatedunverified
    The open sea and its band covered 96.5% of 21,259 satellite passes it had not seen; it claims 9 in 10.
  2. comparedunverified
    The island model beside a Boussinesq model at twelve beaches, 556 cases of a 1 m swell: r 0.44, the island 0.27 m higher on average. Compared at those beaches; assumed at the others.
  3. assumed
    Breaking the wave breaks at 0.55 times the depth (Thornton & Guza, 1982); no sandbars; the shallow profile taken from the nautical chart.

The arithmetic is exact; the weakest premise is assumed, and neither model was verified against an exact solution of its own equations (D11). Where the island reads less than the Boussinesq model — Pântano do Sul (0.39×), Ingleses (0.43×) and Galheta (0.99×) — “under the limit” calls for more caution than the arithmetic shows.

the rules

Sixteen rules, each learned on a page of this site

The rulebook is design/WARRANT.md (v2, frozen 28 October 2026, with dated rulings since). Each rule below was found on a page here that broke it. Each names the page, what was wrong, and the change that fixed it; the fix is checked by that page’s own build, which refuses when its record stops supporting its sentence.

the colour law

Ink first, and one red

Ink comes first: a figure must read completely in greyscale, and a colour always arrives with a shape and a word. The law came from a measurement: run through the validator, the palette of Machinidae, a sibling project built on this grammar, showed its two main series colours ΔE 0.4 apart to a deuteranope. To that reader they were one colour.

  • 1. Ink first. Every figure reads completely in greyscale; a colour always comes with a shape and a word.
  • 2. One colour, one meaning, in every project built on the grammar. Each colour is registered, below, with its meaning and the date it was approved.
  • 3. Colour never carries standing (the stroke does), and it never stands in for a verdict word.
  • 4. Four kinds of colour only: the witness, one red, for a shown counterexample; identity, at most three hues for the series compared in one figure, each with its own shape; order, one hue in lightness steps, for an ordered scale; status, an app’s decided states, always with an icon and a word.
  • 5. Every pair passes the validator. Every set of colours that shares a figure is compared pair by pair, on the figure’s own ground, in OKLab (ΔE × 100) under the colour-vision model of Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009): ΔE ≥ 8 for each simulated colour-vision deficiency and ≥ 15 for normal vision, every pair and not only neighbours in a list.
  • 6. No yellow and no orange.
  • 7. A new colour enters only with the validator’s report and an approval, recorded in the rulebook.

the premise ladder

How far a premise was checked

Standing says how a number was computed. A number that rests on a physical model needs a second answer that standing cannot give: the arithmetic can be exact while the equations it was exact about have never met the thing they describe. So each premise carries a grade, which says where the checking of it stopped. Strongest first:

  • validated — checked against observations of the thing itself, held out, with the number. In the literature: validation. On Swell: the open sea’s band, against satellite passes it had not seen.
  • compared — checked against a model that keeps more of the physics, with the number; not against the thing itself. In the literature: code-to-code comparison. On Swell: the island model, against a Boussinesq model at twelve beaches.
  • verified — the code reproduces an exact solution of its own equations; not measured against the thing itself. In the literature: code verification. On Swell: none yet.
  • assumed — adopted from a source, chosen, or read; no machine here checked it. In the literature: an adopted assumption. On Swell: breaking at 0.55 times the depth (Thornton & Guza, 1982), and no sandbars.

The ladder is the verification-and-validation ladder of the modelling literature — Roache (1998), Oberkampf and Roy (2010), the ASME V&V 20 standard (2009) — cut to four grades a reader can tell apart. A conclusion is as checked as its weakest premise (W.weakest), and the page that states it names that premise. A grade is never a probability and never a confidence. It is carried by a word first and a three-step glyph second, never by colour.

A read step (ruled 29 October). A proof step that was read and not run by a machine gets no grade of its own: it is assumed, and the gloss says so. It does not cap a verdict. CERTIFIED, PARTIAL, REFUTED and REFUSED keep the meaning their certificate gives them; every read step is listed, graded assumed, in the page’s “what it rests on”, and the chain’s last line names it. A reader always sees where the machine stopped; the verdict word never hides a reading, and a reading never demotes a decision it does not touch. δ₃ = 117/2192 is an example: a decided finite core, and three steps proved on paper and read, listed as assumed.

Where a model carries an extent, the same idea is drawn as the halo (rule 13, the key above): blur is the sign of uncertainty readers understand most readily (MacEachren et al. 2012).

how it is enforced

The checks that refuse a page

A grammar that no build checks is a suggestion. Five programs read every built page and refuse the site when a page breaks the grammar or its layout gets worse. Each carries red controls, planted violations it must catch, so a check that cannot fail is caught too. The numbers below are read from their output and their records as this page is built.

  • tools/check-grammar.js — the dash census. Only the five patterns of design/grammar.js may appear in a built page; three uses of a dash array that are not lines (an arc length, a draw-on animation, and a retired pattern that survives only in the three submitted Petrobras pages, kept as submitted) are declared by name, each with its reason. The first census, on 5 September, counted twenty-five patterns: the dash had been derived from the stroke width, so every width minted a pattern of its own, one of them carrying a float artefact (1.04 5.460000000000001) into published HTML. At the last sync, 535 conforming uses and 0 outside the five:
    • 5 4 computed, the line pattern — 249 uses
    • 1.5 2 computed, under 1.5 px of stroke — 51 uses
    • 1 5 chosen, round caps — 91 uses
    • 1 3 chosen, under 1.5 px — 53 uses
    • 2 3 a guide: an axis, a ruler, a ghost; it says nothing — 84 uses
  • tools/check-marks.js — a printed number is re-read from its record. A value written through W.val carries the address of its record cell (data-rec: a file under certs/ or corpus/ and a pointer) and the format it was printed by; at every build this gate follows each address from the built page back into the record, prints the datum again by the same rule (design/record.js), and refuses a page whose text differs, whose pointer no longer resolves, or whose decided value was cut to nearest where an end was needed. At the last check, 247 numbers on 6 pages were re-read this way, and 1,540 values still carry no address: that count is recorded and may only shrink. Four planted marks — a dangling pointer, an upper end rounded inward, a wrong digit, a decided value cut short — are refused on every run.
  • tools/check-wiring.js — a legend wherever a mark is. A page with a mark of the grammar and no legend is refused. At the last check, 52 of the 127 pages it walks draw the grammar, and every one prints its legend. Its red control plants a computed line with no legend, and must catch it.
  • tools/check-render.js — what a page shows. No text a builder leaked (a template that never filled, a cell reading undefined or NaN, a stray backtick); no mark whose paint does not resolve; no figure thinner in ink than its record (100 figures recorded); no new mark too faint to see; no script error while the page loads. Its paint check was made strict when the legend’s void swatch fell through to SVG’s default fill, black on a near-black ground, and had passed every run, because black is a paint.
  • tools/check-measure.js — the layout ruler. Every built page driven in Chrome at 1440, 768, 390 px; the number of left edges, sideways overflow, blocks escaping the screen and clipped elements may only go down (137 pages recorded).

This page is held to the same standard by its own builder, tools/build-grammar.js, which reads every number above from its record and refuses to build when one stops saying what the page says.

use it

In figures of your own

The module is one file, playground/warrant.js, MIT-licensed, with the five patterns in design/grammar.js beside it; it needs no library. In Node, with what each call returns as this page was built:

const W = require('./playground/warrant.js');

// a line: its standing picks the stroke
W.attrs(W.COMPUTED)
  → class="w-computed" fill="none" stroke-width="2" stroke-dasharray="5 4"
W.attrs(W.meet(W.DECIDED, W.CHOSEN), { width: 1 })
  → class="w-chosen" fill="none" stroke-width="1" stroke-dasharray="1 3" stroke-linecap="round"

// a number in text
W.value('3.1416', W.COMPUTED)
  → <span class="w-val w-computed" title="computed — float, or a number someone printed; nothing decided it">3.1416</span>

// the legend, compact beside a figure, and the stylesheet, once per page
W.legendHtml({ compact: true })
W.css()

// a chain of premises, in the order the number travelled through them
W.premisesHtml([
  { grade: W.VALIDATED, label: 'The open sea and its band', evidence: '...' },
  { grade: W.ASSUMED, label: 'Breaking', evidence: '...' },
], { meetLine: 'The weakest premise is assumed.' })

On a canvas, setLineDash takes the same patterns from grammar.js: G.dash.claim is [5, 4] and G.dash.pick is [1, 5]. The legend and the ladder in Portuguese — suposto, verificado, comparado, validado — are in playground/warrant-pt.js. The rules are in design/WARRANT.md.

A print version and a Python kit for matplotlib are in preparation.
