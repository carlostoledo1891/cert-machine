Every number a simulator writes is an exact fraction.

So whether a recorded run kept its energy, to any tolerance you name, can be decided with whole numbers and no rounding. Three scenes whose physics is known exactly, run in two engines built for simulating robots: MuJoCo 4.0.1 with each of its five integrators, and Newton 1.8.0 with two of its solvers, at their defaults and with the angular damping off. Pick a run: your tab fetches the recorded bytes and decides them again.

What the 100 recorded runs say.

In each cell: the largest distance from the law anywhere in the run, rounded up, and the tightest of six tolerances the whole run stays inside. Configurations that wrote the same bytes share a row. Nothing was refused: the 1,464 verdicts are 393 Certified and 1,071 Refuted.

Two published sentences, decided.

None of this is a fault report. An integrator of finite order is expected to move away from the true motion, and MuJoCo’s documentation describes the behaviour above. What this page adds is the decision: each statement checked on the recorded bytes with whole numbers, the step where it first fails, and the same check run again in your tab.

One sentence of that documentation is a claim exact enough to decide: that under its implicit integrators the spin energy of a free body never increases. On the 8 recorded runs it covers, it holds at every step with no tolerance at all. (The four discrete runs are the same bytes as implicitfast in this scene, so it holds there too.)

Newton’s class description says its semi-implicit method preserves energy. That is a description of the method, with no bound. The solver’s constructor also takes an angular damping, documented, with the default 0.05, and a body built with the defaults is damped by design: on the 4 recorded runs the spin energy is more than 1% down within 0.11 seconds and 60.6% to 62.9% down after 10.

That is a documented default beside a sentence, not a bug: passing angular_damping=0.0 turns it off, and Newton’s own verification tests do. SolverFeatherstone carries the same sentence and the same default, and wrote identical files with and without it. The report has the detail.

How a float gets decided.

The bytes. A run is a file: the engine’s time, positions and velocities after every step, as it wrote them. Each of the 100 files is named by its sha256, and your tab checks that before deciding anything.

The fraction. A float, 64-bit or 32-bit, is a whole number times a power of two. Energy and angular momentum are sums of products of those, so they are fractions too, and “within 1%” becomes a comparison of two whole numbers. Only the pendulum needs a cosine, which is trapped between two fractions as tightly as the comparison needs.

Twice. The record was written only after two programs that share no code, one in JavaScript and one in Python, agreed on every field of every run, and after 12 planted files with a known answer were each decided as planted.

The rules were written and committed before the first simulation step of each engine; one law was added after the MuJoCo runs and is marked as such. The report has the rules, the limits and what is still open.

Two engines, one version of each, one machine. Every verdict is about a recorded file of bytes, not about the engine in general; the tolerances are this page’s, not a promise either engine makes; and a law that holds at every recorded step says nothing about the instants between them.
