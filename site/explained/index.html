The easy read · cert-machine, the method paper, as a story

A machine says it found new mathematics. Who checks?

Search programs and language models now propose constants, counterexamples and record-breaking constructions faster than anyone can referee them, and each one arrives with a checker written by the same machine. This is how an independent machine decides such claims exactly: five acts and an epilogue, five instruments you can touch, every number read from a public record when the page was built.

Act I · a claim is an inequality

A verdict is one of three words.

Strip any machine-made mathematical claim down to what a computer can decide and you get this quantity is at most this number, for every input in this box. The machine reads it as exactly that, over the exact bytes the claimant published, and answers with one of three words.

certified

The inequality holds over the whole box, proved by exact arithmetic or by outward-rounded intervals. Shipped with the certificate and the thing that would have falsified it.

refuted

A specific input violates the inequality. Shipped with that input, so anyone can check the refutation in one line.

refused

The claim cannot be decided from the bytes: a file is missing, a number is not printed, a function has no exact decider. Shipped with the reason and, where there is one, the threshold that would change the word.

The third word is the one that makes the other two honest. A checker that must answer yes or no will guess when it cannot decide. This one says why it cannot, and what would convert a refusal into a decision. That turns the machine into an incentive: publish the file, print the digit, and the word changes. A floating-point comparison on its own is never a verdict here. It is a screen, and a screen may only prune what is not worth certifying, never admit.

Act II · the reading
One string of digits, two numbers.

  
Every decimal a machine prints is an exact rational. The number the machine meant is usually a binary double, and the two differ in the seventeenth place. Which one is the claim?

  

    

      
Instrument 2 · print √3/2 to k digits

      
The same point, three readings.

      
Two unit vectors at exactly sixty degrees: (1, 0) and (½, √3/2). A claimant prints the second coordinate as a decimal. Read it back three ways and decide, exactly, whether the pair is touching, too close, or apart.

      

      

      

      
The test is the one the machine uses for kissing configurations: with p the inner product and A, B the squared norms, the angle is at least sixty degrees if and only if p ≤ 0 or 4p² ≤ AB. Here that is the sign of 3/4 − y², decided over exact rationals. The microscope below the readout places each reading by its distance from √3/2 on a log scale; that placement is a float of the exact gap, a picture. The side and the shape are decided: ◇ fails the sixty-degree test, ○ passes it apart, ● passes it touching.

    

    

      
In 2026 a public arena published a 605-point candidate for the kissing number in dimension eleven, its points printed to fifteen digits. Read as printed digits, 9,510 of its 182,710 pairs are closer than sixty degrees. Read as the doubles those digits denote, 9,480. Both readings refute the record; the counts differ because the reading is part of the verdict.

      
So every object the machine decides carries a sentence saying how the bytes were read, and when a platform's own verifier reads them another way, that is written down too. A verdict without its reading is a number without a unit.

Act III · the trial
We tried to fool it 572 times.

  
How do you test a judge? Plant errors whose truth you know, and count the ones it misses. The benchmark was pre-registered, kill switch included, before the paper was written.

  

    

      
Take 97 published objects the machine had already decided. Mutate each in ways whose truth is known by an independent exact witness: 322 false, 153 true but changed, 97 malformed. Count how often the machine says certified to something false or refuted to something true. What a tolerance costs

      
The same mutants were also checked the way most code checks numbers: compare in floating point, allow a tolerance. Loosen it and you accept more lies; tighten it and you start rejecting the truth. There is no setting that does both jobs. Exact arithmetic has no setting.

Instrument 3 · plant an error

      
Twelve points. Break them, or try.

      
The twelve points that touch a central sphere in the face-centred cubic packing: every permutation of (±1, ±1, 0). The claim: all twelve are at least sixty degrees apart. Decided here pair by pair in exact integers, 4p² ≤ AB.

      

      

        
      

      

      
Two buttons are controls. Multiplying by 3 is not an error and must leave the word alone; swapping two coordinates only looks harmless, makes one point a copy of another, and must not. The ring is a picture: the points are spaced around it, not placed in space, and every line on it is a contact decided exactly.

    

  






  
Act IV · the scale
Floating point can be exact. You have to prove when.

  
In September 2026 an autonomous research system published 19 new kissing-number bounds, the largest with 53,301,140 points in dimension 55, and its own verification files said, honestly, that the pairwise angles had not been checked. Checking them is 9.75 × 1015 pairs.

  

    

      
The trick is old and elementary. The coordinates are small integers, so every inner product is an integer, and a floating-point unit computes an integer product exactly as long as no partial sum can leave the range the format holds exactly: 2¹¹ for half precision, 2²⁴ for single. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality every partial sum is at most the largest squared norm in the configuration. Check that bound against the data before the run, assert it in the code, and the tensor cores of a rented GPU become an exact integer engine.

Act V · the proof that is mostly out of reach
A famous theorem, and the ten sentences a machine can check.

  
In 2026 a language model found a proof that more than two thirds of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function are simple and on the critical line. Two mathematicians verified it; it was formalised in Lean. Most of it has no finite layer. The machine says exactly which part does.

  

    

      
A translator with a closed grammar read every sentence the story's sources print, the manuscripts, the Lean files, the research post, even a video's captions, and either reduced it to a decidable claim or refused it with a rule.

      

      
65 printed sentences: 10 read; 43 refused as prose, 6 with no printed quantity, 3 ambiguous, 2 asymptotic, 1 approximate. Every theorem is among the refused: a statement about a limit is not a finite claim, and the grammar says so rather than inventing one.

      
What is read is the finite layer underneath: the formalisation's one numerical certificate, 256 integer enclosures at scale 2140, and the constants printed to their digits. The certificate's three inequalities hold over its integers, decided by two programs that share no code with Lean. Its one real-number hypothesis, that the enclosures enclose anything, rests on a file the release describes as "available from the authors". No published byte decides it. We asked for the file, in public.

      
Then something small and new

      
Once the constants were rational arithmetic they could be optimised. The proportion of simple zeros of ξ′ depends on a window function through a ratio of two quadratic forms, so the best polynomial window of each degree is exact linear algebra. The paper's quartic sits 8.85 × 10-7 below its own family's optimum, and the certificate's nine-term series truncation costs more than the whole window gap, 3.42 × 10-7. A degree-six window and fifteen terms raise the formalisation's constants from 0.86864017 to 0.86864150, with an exact certificate that no even polynomial window of degree up to twenty does better. As a calibration, the same machinery with the ζ kernel returns the Montgomery–Taylor constant to 18 digits.

Epilogue · where it stops
A referee that never guesses must say, out loud, where it stops.

  

    
Theorems
A statement about every integer, or about a limit, is not a finite claim. The machine refuses it with the rule and leaves it to proofs and proof assistants.

    
Hypotheses nobody can check
A certificate kernel-checked downstream of a file the public cannot read is conditional, for the kernel and for the machine alike. The word is refused, and the ask is the file.

    
Its own code
Soundness rests on arithmetic; the arithmetic is held to an independent library on 1,012,121 cases of 24 operations, to a second implementation on every decision, and to the 572 mutants above. When it fails, the failure is on the record first.

  

  
Refusal is a verdict.

  
The register this page is read from holds 132 claims from 59 claimants across 25 sources: 107 hold, 24 carry a defect on record, and every row carries the object that justifies its word. The machine has never improved a theorem. It has moved an asterisk, counted a wrong digit, published the threshold that would turn a refusal green, and once, when the arithmetic allowed it, raised a constant by a unit of the sixth digit with a certificate attached. That is the job.
