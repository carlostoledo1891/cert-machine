NIST’s ECDSA curve seeds: the timestamp-and-lrand48 origin, re-decided from the standards

On 16 October 2026 Anthropic published that the fourteen seeds behind the NIST and ANSI X9.62 elliptic curves — a question open since 1997 and carrying a bounty — are outputs of the C library’s lrand48, seeded from Unix timestamps of May 1997 to April 1998, and that six base points come from FreeLIP’s random generator. Every row of that claim that a computation can decide is re-decided here, the same evening, from the POSIX specification of drand48, from ANSI X9.62’s derivation as FIPS 186-2 prints its intermediates, and from FreeLIP’s source — by two programs that share no code with each other or with the claimant’s. All 34 rows hold. The claims beyond the checkable core are listed as readings, each with what would decide it.

The claim: anthropic.com/research/ecdsa-curve-origins as fetched 2026-10-16 (sha256 afb5fba0659f…), its two code blocks pinned as evidence and never run. The publisher edited the post on 17 October (14:19 UTC): copy edits only, namely serial commas, thousands separators (1,842; 1/5,810), one “on” and “Acknowledgments”. The code, its printed outputs, the figures and every number are unchanged, and the edited bytes are pinned beside the decided ones (5f4b5b730c97…). The published objects: FIPS PUB 186-2 (7384ae9ddbf8…) — for each prime curve p, r, the seed s, c, b, G; for each binary curve the field polynomial, r, and the seed and b in the normal basis — and OpenSSL’s crypto/ec/ec_curve.c at a pinned commit for the five X9.62 example curves, whose standard is paywalled; the two sources must agree on every NIST field or the reader refuses. The specifications: IEEE Std 1003.1 for the drand48 family; FreeLIP 1.1’s lip.c (Lenstra–Leyland, 1997) for zrstarts, zrandom and zrandomb. Pre-registered in corpus/curveseeds/preregistration.json with one dated amendment before any row was decided. Decided: that each published seed is the stated candidate of the stated srand48 run (14 rows); that each seed yields its published curve by X9.62’s SHA-1 procedure (14); that each of six base points is the first point FreeLIP’s generator draws from the stated seed (6). Not decided: the skipped-candidate claim, which needs point counting (the optional lane below counts apart); the probabilities and the interpretation; anything about the security of the curves.

A CERTIFIED row says: start the POSIX generator with srand48(argument), take the n-th block of twenty outputs (prime curves: the top byte of each, lrand48() >> 23) or of five outputs (binary curves: each whole 31-bit value as a 32-bit word), and the 160 bits are the published seed. The candidate index is 1-based, as the post defines it ("the first 20 bytes are candidate 1"). The published seeds are read from the pinned FIPS 186-2 text layer (the NIST curves; the binary seeds in the normal basis FIPS gives them in) and from OpenSSL’s table (the X9.62 example curves), never retyped. The four binary runs start within 470 seconds of one another on the same afternoon (the stated clocks 206, 154 and 110 s apart), given the halved-clock reading; without the halving the srand48 arguments read as February 1984 and the red control shows no seed reproduces.

ANSI X9.62-1998 A.3.3 turns a seed into a curve: with t the field size in bits, s = ⌊(t − 1)/160⌋ and v = t − 160s, the chain is the rightmost v bits of SHA-1(seed) followed by SHA-1(seed + 1) … SHA-1(seed + s). In the prime case the leftmost bit of the first block is cleared and the result is c, with b any solution of b²c ≡ a³ (mod p), a = −3; FIPS 186-2 prints that c, and the calibration that regenerates it is what caught the leftmost-bit rule (amendment 1). In the binary case the chain is b itself, in the normal basis FIPS uses for s and b. This row is the standard’s own promise, not the post’s; it is decided so that one record closes the chain from a 32-bit clock value to the published curve. For the X9.62 example curves no c is printed anywhere open, so the congruence alone decides.

FreeLIP (lip.c): zrstarts(seed) sets the state to the seed and the multiplier to 3¹²¹ mod (2¹⁰⁷ − 1); zrandom(b) advances the state and returns it mod b, redrawing in the rare case the state falls in the top partial block; zrandomb(p) draws one limb more than p has, the first most significant, and reduces mod p. The limb is 26 bits (NBITS in the SINGLE_MUL build of lip.h; 30 bits fails the red control), seven single draws are discarded first (given the post’s reading of the generating program; zero fails the red control), and the first x with x³ − 3x + b a quadratic residue is the base point’s abscissa. The post explains no other base point — P-224, P-256, P-384, P-521 and the binary curves stay open — and nothing here changes that.

The optional lane, counted apart. Every candidate below the published index of each run, derived by X9.62 and point-counted with PARI/GP (Schoof–Elkies–Atkin with the seadata tables) — outside the standard library, so outside the certificate above. A candidate is viable when its c gives a curve (c ≢ 0, 4c + 27 ≢ 0, −27/c a square mod p); it then has two curves, y² = x³ − 3x ± b, which are quadratic twists of each other when p ≡ 3 (mod 4) and isomorphic when p ≡ 1 (mod 4). The published b is one of the two roots, and the post’s "parameters chosen the same way" is read as that root.

§5 · conventions

The conventions every verdict above rests on

  • C1. timestamps are UTC; the srand48 argument is the Unix time (the binary curves: its integer half)
  • C2. candidate n is 1-based: the n-th block of 20 lrand48 calls (prime) or 5 calls (binary) — the post: "the first 20 bytes are candidate 1"
  • C3. prime-curve byte = lrand48() >> 23, the top 8 bits of the 48-bit state; binary-curve word = lrand48(), 31 bits printed as a 32-bit word
  • C4. the seed → curve step is ANSI X9.62-1998 A.3.3 as FIPS 186-2 prints its intermediates: the prime case clears the leftmost bit of the first SHA-1 block (amendment 1), the binary case does not; FIPS gives the binary seed and b in the normal basis
  • C5. base points: FreeLIP zrstarts(seed) / zrandom / zrandomb as lip.c codes them, NBITS = 26 (the SINGLE_MUL build), seven zrandom draws discarded first (the post's reading of the generating program), the first x with x³ − 3x + b a quadratic residue

One amendment to the pre-registration, dated before any row was decided. Amendment 1. the first attempt produced c = fefba166… where FIPS prints 7efba166…; for P-192, b099d2bb against 3099d2bb — the leftmost bit of the first SHA-1 block is cleared. That is ANSI X9.62-1998 A.3.3.1's own rule for the prime case (set the leftmost bit of W0 to 0), omitted from the claims text above; the binary case A.3.3.2 has no such step and B-233's normal-basis b reproduced without it. the prime derivation clears bit v−1 of the first block; a red control is added — the chain without the clearing must not reproduce c. Nothing else changes; the calibration against the standard's own printed intermediate is exactly what caught this.

§6 · limits

What is not claimed

  • That the clock values are moments in 1997. The computation shows that a 32-bit srand48 argument and a small index regenerate 160 published bits, fourteen times. That the argument was the wall clock of a program is the natural reading and the post’s; it is not a computation.
  • Uniqueness. That no other generator or convention produces the same bytes is not decided; the red controls show the nearby conventions do not.
  • Security. Nothing here bears on the strength of any curve. The post’s "much less plausible" is interpretation and is listed as such.
  • The skipped candidates. Whether candidate 876 of the P-256 run, three P-224 candidates and seven X9.62 candidates also yield prime-order curves needs point counting; it is R1, not a row of this certificate, and the optional lane’s own counts are reported apart.
  • Same-evening work. Built in the hours after the post appeared, from the standards, with the forgeries listed; nothing has been reviewed by anyone else yet. The pre-registration, the pins and the two deciders are in the repository for whoever wants to break it.
