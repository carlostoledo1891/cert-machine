Three Lean proofs of bounded prime gaps, and the 16 premises Lean does not check

Three Lean proofs bound the gaps between primes: OpenAI’s (186), one whose repository credits “GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI), prompted by Romyar Sharifi.” (182), and Axiom Math’s (212). The proofs are theirs, and nothing on this page proves a premise or verifies any of the three bounds. Each proof starts from statements it takes as given, which Lean does not check. What is still open

  • Nothing here proves a premise, and nothing here verifies 186, 182 or 212. Each proof says: if its premises hold, then its bound. This page is about the premises, not about the proofs.
  • Instances are not a proof. The rank-four bound was decided at every prime below 2,000 and at none above. A finite check can refute a premise that was written down wrongly; it cannot prove one. For all primes the bound is Deligne’s theorem, not this run’s.
  • The 182’s 262 numbers were reproduced, not decided. They came out of the authors’ own programs, run again by me. A replay of a claimant’s programs is never an independent decision. And the step from each computed bound to the integral it stands for is argued in the package’s comments: it is in no program and not in Lean.
  • Which number belongs to which Lean row is the package’s own assignment. My comparison re-derives that key from the Lean row and requires it to be equal, which checks the pairing against Lean’s own fields and no further.
  • The readings of the 212 are readings. Two AI readers compared each quoted premise with the paper it cites. A reading is not a decision, and neither reader is a human referee.
  • Not decided: the 182’s local Fourier hypothesis. Its constants are free, so no finite test can refute it: a larger threshold or a larger constant escapes any instance.
  • Not decided: the 212’s certificate. It asserts that a certain function exists, and no such function is published. The question is open with the authors as issue #2 of their repository.
  • Two of the 182’s premises are the 186’s, taken over. The 182 carries a copy of the 186’s Lean file that is byte-identical, so its two shared premises inherit the checks of the 186. That its names resolve to those definitions is my reading of the file; I did not ask Lean.
  • The replay ran on my own build of FLINT, on Linux. The package is tested by its authors on macOS with its bundled libraries. No byte of the package was changed; six things in the environment around it were, and each is listed in the records.
  • A recomputation of the 262 numbers by programs of mine has not been started. That is what would turn “reproduced” into something stronger for that row.

1 · the 182’s rank-four premise

Every case for the primes below 2,000

The 182 adds a premise the 186 does not have: a bound on a sum over a finite field, one for every prime p and every nonzero c. Its size is to be at most 4 times p√p. It is Deligne’s bound for the rank-four Kloosterman sum, taken as a hypothesis. A computer can decide it prime by prime, which proves nothing about all primes and still tests whether the Lean statement says something false.

  • All hold. 276,747 cases, for the 303 primes below 2,000, each decided by two programs that share no code: one in exact integers, one in interval arithmetic. None fails; none is left undecided.
  • The two programs agree case by case. On all 276,747 cases their enclosures of the sum overlap. A third program, a direct triple sum, recomputed the 149 cases at the primes up to 31 and landed inside both.
  • Wrong versions are caught. With 4 replaced by 3, 496 cases fail in both programs, the same 496, the earliest at p = 73. With 4 replaced by 2, 13,696 fail in both, the same number at every prime.
  • How close it comes. The sum reaches 3.6085 times p√p against the assumed 4, at p = 1,493 and c = 689.
2 · the 182’s numbers

The authors’ numerical package, run again

Another premise of the 182 is a list of 262 inequalities between integrals and fractions: 5 cap bounds, 120 outer and 137 inner. Its authors support them with a numerical package, released as an archive. I ran it again.

  • What was run. The release archive, pinned by its hash, and the repository at its pinned commit, on a rented Linux machine. The arithmetic library is FLINT v3.6.0 with one upstream correction (7ad753d5), built from source. No byte of the package was changed.
  • The package accepts its own run. Its 14 audits pass, its 4 rejection tests all reject, and all 14 production stages pass, in 2 h 52 min. Its acceptance step reports a complete recomputation of the 262 inequalities.
  • My comparison. A script of mine, with its hash logged before production started, compares the 262 fresh values with the fractions it parses from the Lean files at the pinned commit, not with the package’s own targets. 262 of 262 are on the right side.
  • There is no slack. All 262 fresh values are equal to the numbers written in Lean, and equal to the published values. The Lean numbers are exactly what the package computes, so nothing separates a computed bound from the bound Lean assumes.
  • The comparison catches a wrong number. With one Lean number tightened past its value, it fails and names the row. With one row removed, it fails on the count.
3 · the 212’s quoted theorems

Seven quoted premises, read blind twice

7 of the 8 premises of the 212 are theorems quoted from two papers. For these the question is whether the Lean text says what the paper says.

  • How they were read. By two AI readers. Each read the cited paper and recorded what it states before opening the Lean file; the second reader did not see the other’s work.
  • What both found. All 7 differ from the statements they cite. In 6, the Lean text bounds an error over all n, where the paper sums over n between x and 2x; a helper for the paper’s version is defined in the file and never used. The other one adds a condition its source does not have.
  • Already public. The readings, hypothesis by hypothesis, are in corpus/bgp212-readings-2026-11-12, and the main difference is reported to the authors as issue #1 of their repository.
  • Readings, not decisions. A reading compares two texts. Neither reader is a human referee, and no program decided anything in this section.
4 · the 212’s certificate

A certificate with no published witness

  • What it asks for. The remaining premise of the 212 says that a function with a stated property exists. To check it, one needs the function.
  • None is published. I looked in the repository at its pinned commit, whose own README says the certificate is assumed, and in the library it depends on, which holds such functions for two other bounds. Finding one myself would be a research computation, and was not attempted.
  • Asked. The question is open with the authors as issue #2 of their repository. The row stays NOT DECIDED.
the method

How it was done

  • Rules before results. The rules for each stage, with its verdict words and its predictions, were written and sealed before the stage ran: a salted fingerprint of the private work, posted to a public registry, which proves the date without showing the content. There are 4 fingerprints (the rules; the results; amendment A1, the rules; amendment A1, the results), and the rules are now public: corpus/prime-gap-premises/PREREGISTRATION.md.
  • A wrong version on every check. A bound made too small, a number tightened, a row removed: each check had to catch its own.
  • Departures are listed. Among them: the first rented machine was deleted partway through the long run, and the whole step was run again from the start on a second one.
  • Size. One day, 12 November 2026: a laptop and 2 rented machines, about US$2.50 by the clock.

check it yourself

How to re-run it

  • The record. node tools/record-prime-gap-premises.js --check re-derives every number on this page from corpus/prime-gap-premises (45 files, each pinned by its hash) and refuses on any difference.
  • The two deciders. In that folder, python3 decider_a.py (it needs NumPy; about 109 minutes) and node decider_b.js (about 3 minutes) decide the rank-four premise again; a smaller bound on the command line makes a shorter run. Given --values and a file name, each also writes its value for every case, and compare.py compares the two files and runs the direct triple sum.
  • The comparison script. corpus/prime-gap-premises/step5/compare182.py needs a run of the authors’ package and a copy of their repository at the pinned commit.
  • What is withheld, and why. The package’s own output from my run is not published, because the 182 repository carries no licence: what I read from it is published, with the hashes of the files. Two large files of per-case values are given by hash; the deciders write them again. corpus/prime-gap-premises/PUBLICATION-NOTES.md says what differs from the private files.
  • What was checked. openai/PrimeGaps186 at 61340d0b, romyar1/prime-gaps-lean at b77bf175, AxiomMath/BGP212 at 6641780b. The earlier check of the 186: its report.
