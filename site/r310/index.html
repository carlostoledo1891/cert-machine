R(3,10), in the open

Is there a graph on 40 vertices with no triangle and no 10 vertices pairwise unjoined? If none exists, R(3,10) = 40; if one does, 41. Nobody knows, and nothing on this page decides it. the question, from the ground up

Two rules that pull against each other

Take 40 points and join some pairs. Two rules: no three points all joined to each other, and no ten points with no line among them. Lines kill the unjoined sets; too many lines close a triangle. If no drawing obeys both rules, R(3,10) = 40; if one does, it is 41.

Inside the 40-point question sits every smaller one

Pick j points with no lines among them. The points none of them touches must obey the same two rules with 10 − j in place of 10, so their number is capped by a smaller Ramsey number, and those are all known exactly. In the hard case every point has 9 neighbours and leaves 30 untouched, under the cap of 35.

The ladder: seven rungs are solid

R(3,k) is the first number of points at which no triangle and no independent k-set cannot both hold. Up to 23 there is a proof a person can read; 28 and 36 needed a computer.

Why 40 is different: the slack is 13

In the hard, regular case the untouched part of each point has a fixed number of lines. Counting works when that number falls below the fewest lines such a graph can have. The bars show how far above the minimum it lands. No regular case ever closed had more than 5.

The reach of methods: none gets under 43

Each method was run on the solved rungs first, then at k = 10. Its reach is the smallest number of points it can prove impossible; the answer needs 40. One more method, colouring, reaches only 82.

Three phantoms explain almost every failure

A phantom passes all of a method’s checks without being a graph on 40 points. A method that accepts a phantom can never prove that 40 is impossible.

blind to agreement

The one-view phantom

Take one real graph on 30 points and pretend it is what every one of the 40 points sees outside its neighbours. Every count made from a single point is then consistent, at any depth.

It forgets that the views of two points overlap and must agree where they do. This is why ten methods stop at 43. Sharper methods are fooled the same way by a blend of about seven real graphs.

blind to triples

The pair phantom

A table of how many pairs of each kind exist, with every pair consistent in itself. It satisfies 35 exact equations in whole numbers.

Pairs are never asked to agree on a third point. The same phantom, one level up. Found at the stratum with largest codegree 4.

blind to size

The scale phantom

A real graph with the same proportions as ours, 320 points with 72 neighbours each, where every neighbourhood is a largest unconnected set. Built from a 2010 paper’s recipe.

Any argument that only uses ratios would exclude it too, and it exists. So ratios alone can never work.

a fourth fact, not a phantom

Real graphs on 40 points obey every local rule (9 neighbours each, no triangle, 1 to 3 shared neighbours) and still contain ten unconnected points. So nothing built from local rules alone can finish the proof. Three kinds of input survive all three phantoms: an exact small Ramsey number applied to a region of the graph (18 points untouched by 4 chosen ones must contain 6 unconnected, since R(3,6) = 18); whole-number constraints; and statements that force two or more points’ views to agree.

where a counterexample could still hide

Every stratum, and how far it has got

Any (3,10;40)-graph may be taken maximal triangle-free, so it falls in exactly one stratum below. A proof of R(3,10) = 40 has to empty all of them. The hard half is the regular one, split by its largest codegree c*: the most neighbours two unjoined vertices have in common.

Theorem C35

Any finite triangle-free simple graph with no independent set of size 10 that contains an induced copy of the circulant C35(1,7,11,16) (vertices 0..34, u ~ v iff v − u ≡ ±1, ±7, ±11 or ±16 mod 35) has at most 39 vertices, and any four distinct vertices outside the copy induce a 4-cycle.

The bound 39 is attained. So no triangle-free graph on 40 vertices with no independent 10-set contains an induced C35(1,7,11,16).

Why 39. Call the copy’s 35 vertices the core and the rest outside. If an outside vertex were unjoined to two other outside vertices, their three core-neighbourhoods would be three sets that Lemma P, a finite fact checked by computer, says cannot exist; so the missing edges among the outside vertices form a matching. Five outside vertices missing only a matching always contain a triangle, so there are at most four, and 35 + 4 = 39; four with no triangle must miss two disjoint edges, which leaves exactly a 4-cycle.

Exoo’s square. Of the 82,251 ways to remove four of the 39 vertices of Exoo’s 1989 graph, exactly one leaves an induced copy of C35(1,7,11,16), and the four removed vertices induce a 4-cycle: the equality case of the theorem appears in Exoo’s example. (checked on the isomorphism class identified from our own files; Exoo’s pinned bytes were not at home.)

A smaller certificate. For Lemma P, 385 of the 3,360 independent 8-sets suffice, in five orbits of the circulant’s 210 affine symmetries (105 + 70 + 70 + 105 + 35). No union of whole orbits with fewer sets works (1,082 non-empty unions checked). Smaller sub-families exist (an irredundant one of 368 sets), so 385 is minimal only among unions of whole orbits. Confirmed by our own search and by a SAT proof that lrat-check and cake_lpr accept.

That Exoo’s graph is C35 plus four vertices in exactly one way was already reported by Unsolved Lab; the full count over all 82,251 four-subsets and the smaller certificate came from a reading by GPT-6.2-astra (via Carlos Toledo’s chat); all of it is checked by our own code.

Proved in Lean 4.35.0 with Mathlib, on the three standard axioms only. Its key lemma is checked by Lean’s kernel on a certificate of 32,529 nodes, with no native code. It was also decided twice outside Lean, by a SAT proof two checkers accept and by our own search over 403,541 nodes.

where an idea could enter

Six questions open in the plain sense

None of these needs the machinery. Each is a place where a new way of looking would change what gets tried next.

What flips between 22 and 23?

Ten regular graphs exist on 22 points; none on 23. Every count we can write is satisfied on both sides. Something that is not a count separates them. At 18 points the answer is now known: the counts are forced to a single table, every local piece is a real graph, and still the pieces cannot be assembled into one graph, even ignoring the ten-set rule. At 23 they can be assembled, so something more breaks there. Name it, and it is the thing to carry up to 40.

What is the smallest statement about two views agreeing?

Each point sees 30 others outside its neighbours. Two joined points share 22 of those. A proof must use that overlap. Listing all overlaps is the brute force we ruled out; a single inequality about them is what is missing.

Why do extreme graphs end 8, 5, 2, 0?

In every solved case, a graph at the limit contains unconnected sets whose untouched parts are a fixed 8-point graph, then a 5-cycle, then a single line. All 477,142 graphs one rung down do. At 40 only the last step is proved. Counting cannot prove the next; it would need 45 points.

Is the regular case the whole problem?

In every catalogue, whenever a graph exists, one with the maximum possible number of lines exists too. If that held at 40, a counterexample would exist only if a 9-regular one did, and half the map would vanish. No proof is known at any size.

Can rigid spots be forced, then chained?

When a region is exactly as large as a Ramsey number allows, its shape is one of a handful. One such spot (three points fully joined to four) fixes seven whole neighbourhoods. The open question is whether such spots must exist, and whether two of them can coexist.

Where do the 13 extra lines go?

Each untouched part has 13 more lines than the leanest possible graph of its size. The leanest graphs are understood, built from a few patterns. Nothing is known 13 lines above them. A description of what the surplus must look like would be new mathematics.

How a claim travels

  1. 1ProposedOne model family proposes: Claude, or GPT-6.2-astra. Until something checks it, it is a claim.
  2. 2CheckedCode written from the statement alone, without reading the proposer’s code, or the other family: an exact checker, a kernel, a second enumeration.
  3. 3AuditedBy us, not a second party: a control-room note, gaps first: what holds, what the report says that the files do not, what was not run.
  4. 4VerdictCERTIFIED, REFUTED or REFUSED, each with its reason. A refusal is a verdict.
  5. 5SealedThe record’s digest is stamped in Bitcoin, before anyone outside sees it.
  6. 6OpenedAfter its audit, on my word: the files, the salt and the digest, so anyone can re-derive the anchor.

Glossary

  • unconnected set. Points with no line between any two of them. Mathematicians say “independent set”.
  • regular. Every point has the same number of neighbours. Here 9, giving 180 lines.
  • codegree. How many neighbours two unjoined points share.
  • outside graph. What is left after removing a point and all its neighbours.
  • gate. A pass/fail rule written before a run.
  • phantom. Something that passes a method’s checks without being a real graph.
  • largest codegree c*. The most neighbours two unjoined points have in common; the regular case is split by it, from 3 to 7.
  • window W(n, d, cap). The d-regular triangle-free graphs on n vertices in which every two non-adjacent vertices have between 1 and cap common neighbours, with no independence condition at all.
  • the twins. The regular cases already closed that are built like the case at 40: (6,18), (7,23), (8,28). Every path is asked first to close them without the catalogue of the level below.
  • second program. Code written from the statement alone by another session, without reading the first program; “independent” on this page means that and nothing more.
