claimbox · the claim language for cert-machine

The language for claims an exact checker can decide

State a claim so that a machine can decide it, or learn exactly why it cannot.

A claim written for people is rarely precise enough for a machine: which file, which digits, which reading of the bytes, which bound. claimbox is the language that makes it precise, and cert-machine is the separate checker that decides it. The claim is printed back in plain words from the object the checker receives; every number points to a line of its source or to a pinned file; the reading of the bytes is declared; an unclear claim gets a named hole or a fork instead of a guess. claimbox never decides. Every verdict on this page is cert-machine’s: CERTIFIED, REFUTED with the object that breaks it, or REFUSED with the reason.

Today claimbox reads a closed list of claim shapes with patterns; the language is built in the order of the roadmap below. Every count on this page is read from claimbox’s run records when the page is built, and the build refuses a page whose text no longer matches them.

the route of a claim, today

From a sentence to one of three words

claimbox states; cert-machine decides; the two share no code.

  1. The sentence, the source, the bytesas the claimant printed and published them.
  2. claimbox reads the shapeor names the rule it cannot state the claim under, before anything runs.
  3. The objectthe pinned bytes, their declared reading, each bound as the exact rational its digits mean.
  4. cert-machine’s checkera separate program, run on the object as it is.
  5. One of three wordsCERTIFIED, REFUTED with the object that breaks it, or REFUSED with the reason.

the language

What a claim will look like

A sketch of the syntax, which is the first step of the roadmap. The claim, its pin, the fields claimbox reads from it today and cert-machine’s word are the record’s; the layout is the design.

data   M = artifacts/riemann_104/machine.txt   -- pinned: sha256 ac0c7e8a76679ba2a3f2c1814166b392c8301e51110a08bcd2c06624be35f25d
read   M as a (Q, 2) transition table
source V = verify.py                        -- the producer’s own checker

claim  "after 9,037 steps from the blank tape, artifacts/riemann_104/machine.txt is in state 19 with the head at -7, 19 nonzero cells"
       quote from V

$ claimbox check
means      run M from the blank tape for 9,037 steps: the state is 19, the head is at -7, 19 cells are nonzero
kind       finite: decided now
word       CERTIFIED   (cert-machine)
rests on   machine.txt as pinned, the definition “after”, the reading “(Q, 2) table”

records/fork-605.json · sha256 d7df6d6bc37f. The same pinned bytes, two exact answers: the reading belongs to the claim. A fork shows every reading's word; when the words differ it says split, and claimbox never merges them into one.

  • Printed back. The “means” line is printed from the object cert-machine receives, by the grammar run backwards, never by a model. What is read is what is checked.
  • Every number has an address: a line of a source or a pinned file. A number with no address is not a claim.
  • Every reading is written down: decimal digits or IEEE doubles, exact or wrapping integers. The same file read two ways can give two different exact answers, so the reading belongs to the claim.
  • Holes. A claim that cannot be stated exactly yet keeps a named hole: the bytes are not published, no decider exists for this quantity. A hole is not a verdict.
  • Forks. An ambiguous claim is decided under every declared reading, and every word is shown; when they differ the fork is split, and said so.
  • Watchers. A claim about every case is run as a watcher that halts on a counterexample, and reports “not refuted up to N”, never CERTIFIED.
  • Rests on. Every word comes with the files, definitions and readings it depends on.

the commitments

What claimbox holds itself to

  1. cert-machine is the only judge. claimbox never prints CERTIFIED or REFUTED on its own; it shows cert-machine’s word.
  2. Say what is checked. The plain-words print-back comes from the object cert-machine receives, not from what was typed.
  3. Every number has an address: a line of a source or a pinned file.
  4. Every reading is written down. There is no default.
  5. Anyone may propose; only checks count. People, models and search may write claims; a proposal is marked as one until it is validated, and two proposals count as two only when they are of different kinds.
  6. Unclear is an answer. A named hole, or a fork with every reading decided.
  7. Planted wrong claims in every feature, every one of which must be caught, with the results, misses included, published.
  8. Never say more than was checked. CERTIFIED means this object, under this reading, holds on these bytes. It does not mean the sentence is true.
  9. The trusted part stays small: the step from a claim to the object the checker reads is short enough to read in an afternoon.
  10. cert-machine stays separate. claimbox sends it objects; a change there is made there first.

Written down before the run

Of the AI-written claims about the Riemann certificate, 15 of 15 came out as the word written beside each before it ran; of the claims written for the Riemann test, 99 of 99. The false ones are REFUTED or not stated with their rule; the true ones are CERTIFIED.

From prose to claims, so far

Of the 80 AI-written phrasings, the grammar proposes 2 claims and a model, claude-opus-5-5, proposes 50; for the other 30 the model names a hole. A proposal is checked four ways: every number in it is found in its sentence, it reads back with the sentence’s relation, it moves exactly with the sentence when one digit of the bar is changed, and it compiles to the same object as the decided reference. Of the model’s 50 claims, 40 equal the reference field for field, 1 states the same bar printed another way, none differ from it, and 9 have no decided reference to compare with. On a copy of the source with the bar moved, the model’s statement moved with it in 38 runs and did not in 1. cert-machine certifies 52 of the 52 claims proposed.

Validated: 2, the sentences where two kinds of proposer agree. Proposed by one kind only: 40. Refused: 8, where a proposal fails a check. Of these, 5 fail only the read-back (the sentence writes its relation in words), 2 print the bar in Lean’s notation (the check reads neither yet), and 1 fails the moved bar. Not stated: 30, each with a named hole. The model’s run took 125 calls and 0.7495 US dollars, under a cap of 20.

The register’s sentences

Of cert-machine’s register, 97 of 132 sentences take a shape today. The rest are not stated: 22 print no quantity, 11 are prose, 2 are asymptotic. A refusal is a measurement, not a failure.

Not new, and said so: three verdicts, published denominators, controlled languages and hash pinning all exist elsewhere. What claimbox puts together is a deterministic statement layer, printed back from the object; every number anchored; the reading declared and forked; an exact separate checker with three words; and the refusal rate published with planted controls.

what claimbox does not claim

Limits, stated first

  • Faithfulness to an author’s intent is not established. The best methods of 2026 for checking a translation still accept some wrong statements. claimbox tests its translations with planted changes and publishes how many it refuses; it does not certify them.
  • Two models agreeing is not independent confirmation. Models tend to make the same mistakes, so agreement counts only between proposers of different kinds, and is tested on planted sources.
  • CERTIFIED is about an object, not a sentence. It means this object, under this reading, holds on these pinned bytes.
  • “Not refuted up to N” is not evidence for every case. A watcher reports exactly how far it searched.
  • Real-number identities are not decided in general. Only the forms the checker admits are decided; the rest are refused with the reason.
  • A small checker can still be wrong. Keeping it small and separate shrinks what must be trusted; planted controls are how it is watched.

The repository, the commands

Standard-library Python. The test battery runs cert-machine’s checker as a separate program, so it needs a local copy of cert-machine.

git clone https://github.com/carlostoledo1891/claimbox
cd claimbox
python3 -m claimbox shape "K(11) >= 593"     # the shape, or the rule that refuses it
python3 tests/battery.py                    # red controls, calibration, coverage, planted defects
python3 -m web.build --check                # rebuild this page and read every count again
references

What the design rests on

  • Gordon, Milner, Wadsworth (1979), Edinburgh LCF; Barendregt & Wiedijk (2005), “The challenge of computer mathematics”. A small checker is the only thing that decides.
  • Pnueli, Siegel, Singerman (1998), “Translation validation”; McConnell, Mehlhorn, Näher, Schweitzer (2011), “Certifying algorithms”. Check each translation and each output, not the translator.
  • Knight & Leveson (1986), on independently written versions failing together; Kim, Garg, Peng, Garg (2025), “Correlated errors in large language models”. Agreement is not independence.
  • Fuchs, Kaljurand, Kuhn (2008), Attempto Controlled English; Ranta (2004), Grammatical Framework; Kuhn (2014), a survey of controlled natural languages. A readable syntax that parses one way and reads back.
  • Bos (1996), Hole Semantics; Copestake, Flickinger, Pollard, Sag (2005), Minimal Recursion Semantics; Belnap (1977), a four-valued logic. Holes, forks, and a split that is not a refusal.
  • Goldberg (1991), “What every computer scientist should know about floating-point arithmetic”; RFC 8259, on the precision of JSON numbers. The reading of the bytes is part of the claim.
  • Mei & Douglas (2026), “Beyond LLM Judge: Mechanical Verification for Agentic Autoformalization”; Zhang et al. (2026), “Beyond Compilation”; Ammanamanchi, Bhat, Biderman (2026), “Faults in Our Formal Benchmarking”. Preprints. Mechanical checks over judges; compiling is not faithfulness; the statements themselves carry defects.
  • Nuijten et al. (2016), statcheck; Brown & Heathers (2017), the GRIM test. Printed numbers recomputed: the closest precedents.
  • Stroebl, Kapoor, Narayanan (2024), on the limits of resampling with imperfect verifiers; Georgiev, Gómez-Serrano, Tao, Wagner (2025), mathematical exploration at scale. A proposal loop finds the loophole; exact checking closes it.
  • Aaronson (2020), “The Busy Beaver Frontier”; Google DeepMind, cryptids (2026). Claims about every case, and small machines that encode them.
